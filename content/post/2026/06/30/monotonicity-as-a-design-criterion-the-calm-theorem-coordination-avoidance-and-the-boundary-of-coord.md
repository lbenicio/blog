There is a pattern that recurs in nearly every distributed system design review. Someone identifies a guarantee that must hold across machines — a counter must never go negative, a username must be unique, an inventory must not be oversold — and the room converges on the same answer within a minute: put a consensus protocol in front of it. Raft, Paxos, Zab, or a managed equivalent. The reasoning is sound as far as it goes. Consensus gives you a linearizable register, and a linearizable register is a comfortable thing to build on, because you can serialize decisions and thereby implement almost any guarantee you like.

The problem is that the guarantee is rarely the only thing you wanted. You also wanted the system to stay available when a partition splits your replicas. You wanted a write in Frankfurt acknowledged without a round trip to Oregon. You wanted the throughput ceiling to be set by your hardware rather than by a single leader's serialization point. Consensus is not a free abstraction; it is a purchase, and the currency is latency, availability, and throughput. The question a design review should be asking is not *how do we coordinate this?* but *does this operation actually require coordination, and if so, what is the smallest scope in which we can confine it?*

That question has a precise answer for a surprisingly large class of programs. The answer is called the CALM theorem, and it says, roughly, that a program can be implemented with no coordination at all — each node answering from its own local state, with no waiting and no agreement — if and only if the program is *monotone*. Monotonicity is not a property of your storage engine, your replication protocol, or your network topology. It is a property of the computation you are trying to perform. And because it is a property of the computation, it is something you can often change by reformulating the problem.

This article is about that boundary: where coordination is genuinely required, where it is merely habitual, and how to tell the difference. We will build up the formal statement carefully, because the informal version — *monotone programs are easy* — is true but too vague to design against. Then we will look at what monotonicity means in the concrete terms that appear in real systems: set union, append-only logs, lattice-ordered state. And we will spend time on the operations that break it, because that is where the interesting engineering lives. Most production systems are not monotone, and the useful question is not whether they are monotone but which parts of them are, and how far the non-monotone residue can be pushed to the edge.

## What coordination means, and what it costs

Before the theorem, the vocabulary. In distributed systems, *communication* and *coordination* are not the same thing, and conflating them makes the whole discussion incoherent.

Communication is the movement of bytes. Replicas exchange updates, caches are invalidated, heartbeats are sent, logs are shipped. Communication is unavoidable in any distributed system, and for the most part it is cheap and asynchronous: you send a message and get on with your life.

Coordination is stronger. It is the act of establishing agreement about ordering or about a shared decision. A consensus round is coordination. Two-phase commit is coordination. Leader election is coordination. A distributed lock is coordination. What makes these operations expensive is not the bytes — they are small — but the round trips and the quorum requirement. Coordination requires at least one message round trip between participants before anyone can act, and in a wide-area deployment that round trip is bounded below by the speed of light in fiber. A cross-continental round trip is on the order of a hundred milliseconds; a cross-availability-zone round trip inside a region is on the order of a millisecond; a same-rack round trip is on the order of a hundred microseconds. None of those numbers can be improved by better software.

Worse, coordination couples availability to the health of the network. If a quorum of participants cannot be reached, a coordinated operation cannot proceed. That is the substance of the CAP theorem: under a partition, a coordinated (linearizable) system must choose between refusing to answer and answering something that may not reflect the latest committed state. There is no third option, and no amount of engineering removes the choice.

There is also a throughput cost that is easy to underestimate. A consensus protocol typically funnels writes through a single leader and a single log. The leader appends, replicates to a quorum, and commits; the serialization point is the log itself. Production implementations pipeline and batch aggressively, and the achievable throughput is high, but the fundamental structure is a single ordered sequence, and throughput is bounded by how fast entries can be pushed through it. Sharding the log across independent consensus groups is the standard escape hatch. It works, but it multiplies the coordination overhead by the number of groups and reintroduces cross-group coordination whenever an operation touches more than one group.

So the cost model is: coordination costs at least one round trip of latency, costs availability under partition, and imposes a serialization bottleneck. When we say a program is *coordination-free*, we mean that none of these costs are paid on the critical path — each node's output is a function of its own local input, and it can produce that output without waiting for or agreeing with anyone else. Messages may still flow: a coordination-free system can gossip, replicate, and converge. What it cannot do is make its answer depend on whether another node's message has arrived, or on the order in which messages arrived.

## The CALM theorem

The CALM theorem — *Consistency As Logical Monotonicity* — emerged from a line of work connecting declarative networking, Datalog semantics, and distributed consistency, developed by Joseph Hellerstein, Peter Alvaro, and colleagues, and building on formal results by Tom Ameloot, Frank Neven, and Jan Van den Bussche on relational transducers and declarative networking. The headline statement, as presented in the 2020 *Communications of the ACM* article *Keeping CALM: When Distributed Consistency Is Easy*, is:

> A program has a consistent, coordination-free distributed implementation if and only if it is monotone.

Every word in that sentence is load-bearing, so it is worth unpacking.

**A program.** The theorem is about a program expressed in a formal language with set semantics. The canonical setting is a fragment of Datalog, or a relational transducer: the program is a set of rules, and the state is a set of facts. This is not the same object as your Go service. The gap between the formal object and the production system is where most of the practical difficulty lives, and we will return to it repeatedly.

**Consistent.** Consistency here means that the set of outputs a distributed execution can produce is exactly the set of outputs a single-site, serial execution could produce. If you ran the program on one machine with all the inputs, you would get some answer; the distributed implementation must be capable of producing that answer, and must not produce answers that the serial execution could not. This is a strong notion. It rules out *eventually the system converges to something reasonable*, because *something reasonable* is not the same as *the serial answer*.

**Coordination-free.** Formally, each node computes a function of its local input alone. Nodes may communicate — the constructive direction of the theorem relies on nodes exchanging derived facts — but no node's output depends on the ordering of messages or on agreement with another node. That is what makes the implementation immune to partition: since no node waits for another, a partition is just a period during which messages are delayed.

**If and only if it is monotone.** A program is monotone if adding more input facts never removes an output fact. In set terms, if the input set I grows to I' ⊇ I, the output set P(I) must satisfy P(I) ⊆ P(I'). Monotone programs are exactly those whose answers only accumulate.

The *if* direction is the constructive, comforting half: if your program is monotone, there is a coordination-free implementation. The reason is confluence. Monotone rules over sets only add facts and never retract them, so the order in which rules are applied does not affect the final set of derived facts. Any two nodes that have seen the same facts derive the same facts; any two nodes that have seen different facts converge on the union once they exchange what they know. The least fixed point of a monotone rule set is unique and independent of evaluation order. This is precisely the argument that underlies conflict-free replicated data types: a CRDT is, in effect, a monotone program whose state is drawn from a join-semilattice, and its convergence guarantee is a consequence of the same confluence property.

The *only if* direction is the surprising and more useful half. It says that if your program is *not* monotone — if there exists some input on which adding a fact causes an output fact to disappear — then no coordination-free implementation can be consistent. This is not a statement about clever engineering; it is a lower bound. The proof proceeds by constructing an adversarial execution in which two nodes receive facts in different orders and must each decide whether to retract an output, while neither has enough information locally to know whether the other has seen the retraction-triggering fact. Any coordination-free protocol must either have one node answer incorrectly or produce an answer the serial execution could not.

That is the theorem's real payload: it converts *should we coordinate?* from a matter of taste into a question about the structure of the computation. It also tells you where to look when you want to avoid coordination. You do not need a better consensus implementation. You need a monotone formulation of the problem, or a way to confine the non-monotone part to a small, slow path.

## Monotonicity, precisely

To use the theorem as a design tool rather than a slogan, it helps to be precise about the order-theoretic content. Monotonicity is defined relative to a partial order, and the choice of partial order is itself a design decision.

A function f from a poset (A, ⊑) to a poset (B, ⊑) is monotone if x ⊑ y implies f(x) ⊑ f(y). In the database setting, A and B are sets of facts ordered by subset inclusion, and the program is a function from an input instance to an output instance. Under inclusion, monotone means: more input facts, more output facts. Nothing is ever removed.

Inclusion is not the only useful order, and this is where the design leverage comes from. Consider a grow-only counter. Its state is a natural number, and the natural order is ≤. Increment is monotone under ≤: a larger starting value produces a larger result. Merge is max, which is the least upper bound in that order. The state forms a join-semilattice, and the merge operation is associative, commutative, and idempotent. Two replicas that have each seen a subset of the increments can merge by taking the maximum, and the result is the same regardless of merge order. The same reasoning extends to sets under union, to maps under pointwise join, and to registers under last-writer-wins with a total order on timestamps — but each of these extensions carries a caveat, and the caveats are where systems get into trouble.

## Where the semilattice story gets subtle

The claim that the same reasoning "extends" to sets, maps, and last-writer-wins registers is true, but it compresses four caveats into a clause. Each caveat corresponds to a family of production incidents, and each one marks a place where a design that looked coordination-free quietly acquires a coordination requirement.

**Convergence of state is not convergence of answers.** A join-semilattice guarantees that two replicas holding states s₁ and s₂ will converge once they exchange them: both will eventually hold s₁ ⊔ s₂. It says nothing about what a replica *answers* while it holds only s₁. If the answer is computed by a monotone query over the state — the set of order ids, the maximum timestamp, the count of distinct users — then the answer is itself a monotone function of the state, and it converges along with the state. If the answer is computed by a non-monotone query, it will fluctuate as the state grows, and two replicas asked the same question at the same logical moment will give different answers. The monotone part of your system is the *state*; the consistency of the *answer* depends on the query you run over it, and those are separate decisions.

This is more useful than it first appears, because it means query placement is a design lever. A non-monotone query evaluated independently on every replica is a source of visible inconsistency. The same query evaluated once, on a replica that has collected the relevant facts, is perfectly consistent — it just requires *collection*, which is communication rather than coordination. The distinction we drew earlier pays off here: gathering facts from many nodes into one place is a bandwidth and latency cost, not an agreement cost, and it does not require a quorum or a leader.

**Last-writer-wins is monotone in the wrong thing.** An LWW register merges two values by keeping the one with the greater timestamp. The merge is associative, commutative, and idempotent, so the register converges. But the *value* is emphatically not monotone: it can go from `A` to `B` and back to `A` as writers arrive. What is monotone is the pair `(timestamp, value)` under the lexicographic order, and that is the object the semilattice reasoning actually applies to. The practical consequence is that you can merge LWW registers freely, but you cannot assume that any function of their values inherits monotonicity. A dashboard that sums LWW counters will not converge, because the sum of non-monotone quantities is non-monotone. A dashboard that sums grow-only counters will.

**Deletion is accumulation in disguise.** A set under union can only grow, so a system that needs to delete must represent deletion as a fact: a tombstone, a removal event, a version vector entry. The state still grows monotonically; it is the *interpretation* of the state that produces the apparent removal. This is the standard construction behind observed-remove sets and their relatives, and it is worth internalizing as a general move: whenever a problem seems to require retraction, look for the monotone fact whose presence *means* retraction.

**Garbage collection reintroduces coordination.** Tombstones and causal contexts grow without bound, so every real system garbage-collects them, and garbage collection requires knowing that no replica will ever again deliver a fact about a deleted element. That knowledge is a stability watermark, and establishing one is a coordination problem — a lease, a bounded clock skew assumption, or a consensus round. The point is not that monotone systems are secretly coordinated, but that the coordination moves from the write path to a background path. That relocation is usually the whole win: the write path is what your users wait on, and the background path can run on a schedule.

**Operation-based replication assumes an ordered channel.** State-based CRDTs can merge any two states in any order, which is why they tolerate arbitrary message reordering and duplication. Operation-based variants are cheaper on the wire but require causal delivery: a node must not apply an operation before the operations it depends on. Causal delivery is achievable without consensus — vector clocks suffice — but it is an ordering assumption about the transport, and it can force buffering, which is a form of waiting. Coordination-free does not mean assumption-free. It means that the assumptions are about the channel rather than about agreement among participants, and channel assumptions are strictly weaker.

## What non-monotonicity actually looks like

The theorem gives a decision procedure that fits in one line: find any two input instances I ⊆ I′, and check whether P(I′) contains every fact in P(I). If adding a fact ever removes an output fact, the program is not monotone, and no coordination-free implementation can be consistent.

In practice you rarely need to construct the counterexample, because non-monotone programs come in a small number of recognizable shapes. It is worth naming them, because recognizing the shape is most of the work of removing it.

- **Negation and anti-joins.** "This username is available" is true until someone registers it. "Orders with no shipment record" becomes false the moment a shipment arrives. Any output that asserts the *absence* of a fact is non-monotone by construction.
- **Thresholds from above.** "Fewer than ten items remain" is true and then false. "Inventory is above zero" is the same statement in different clothes. Contrast with "at least ten items have been sold," which, once true, stays true forever.
- **Aggregation over a set that can shrink.** Sum, average, and minimum are all non-monotone when elements can be removed. Minimum is antitone even over a growing set — adding an element can only lower it — which is a reminder that monotonicity is always relative to an order, and reversing the order can sometimes rescue the program.
- **Uniqueness and functional dependencies.** "No two accounts share an email address" is violated by adding an account, so the set of states satisfying it is not closed under growth. Uniqueness is the canonical coordination-requiring constraint, and it is worth noting how much engineering has gone into avoiding it: partitioned key spaces, reservation ranges, and eventually-consistent claim sets with deterministic conflict resolution are all attempts to make a non-monotone constraint monotone in practice.
- **Universal quantification over a growing domain.** "Every region has reported" is monotone if the set of regions is fixed, because once the last region reports, further facts cannot un-report it. It becomes non-monotone the moment a new region can join the cluster, because the new region has not reported and the universal statement is retracted. Fixed domains are monotone; growing domains are dangerous.
- **Invariants.** "The balance is never negative," "the sum of the parts equals the whole," "no seat is sold twice." These are the constraints that fill design documents, and each one is a statement about all reachable states rather than about a single output. They are the hardest case, and the one that consumes most of the coordination budget in real systems.

Two observations cut across the list. First, the polarity of a threshold is a free choice: "at least N" is monotone and "at most N" is not, and a surprising number of invariants can be restated with the monotone polarity, at the cost of changing what the system reports. Second, *detecting* a violation is monotone even when *preventing* it is not. "We have observed a state in which the balance went negative" is a fact that accumulates and never retracts, and a replica can assert it locally the moment it sees the offending state. The system has traded prevention for detection, which is a real loss, but it is often a loss worth taking: an audit log, a reconciliation job, or a compensating transaction is frequently cheaper than a global serialization point on the hot path.

The theorem does not tell you that non-monotone programs are wrong. It tells you what they cost. A program that violates monotonicity can still be built, but consistency for the violating part must be purchased with coordination, and the price is paid on every operation that touches it. That is the sentence to bring to a design review.

## Reformulation: turning retraction into accumulation

Because the coordination requirement attaches to the *structure* of the computation rather than to the problem statement, it can often be discharged by rewriting the computation. Four rewrites recur.

**Represent removal as a fact.** As above: tombstones, removal events, causal contexts. The state becomes a monotone record of everything that has happened, and the apparent deletion is a read-time interpretation. The cost is space and garbage collection; the benefit is that writes never need to consult another node.

**Accumulate monotonically, then decide once.** Split the program into a monotone core that gathers all the facts relevant to a decision, and a non-monotone decision function that runs over the gathered set. The decision function is evaluated where all the facts are present, so it needs no coordination — it needs only that the collection be complete, which is a matter of waiting for messages rather than agreeing with peers. This is the shape of most batch analytics, of map-reduce-style pipelines, and of the two-phase designs that separate ingestion from evaluation. It is also the shape that lets you keep an online path fully coordination-free while a slower path enforces the invariant.

**Change the order.** A quantity that decreases under inclusion can be monotone under a different order. The minimum of a growing set is monotone in the reverse numeric order. "The latest known value" is monotone in time. Before concluding that a computation is non-monotone, check whether the output is being compared in the right poset; sometimes the answer is already accumulating and only the comparison is wrong.

**Bound the input.** A non-monotone check over an unbounded domain becomes a finite, local check if the domain is bounded. If at most one claim per key can be made per epoch, then the uniqueness check reduces to a per-epoch comparison that a single node can perform. Bounding is not free — it usually requires coordination to establish the epoch boundary — but the coordination happens once per epoch rather than once per operation.

## Confining the residue: escrow, single-writer, and the coordination boundary

Once you accept that a non-monotone core exists, the design question becomes how small you can make it and how tightly you can fence it. The goal is a system whose *critical path* is coordination-free and whose coordinated component runs rarely, off the hot path, and in a scope no larger than the invariant requires.

**Single-writer per key.** If every key has exactly one node authorized to decide its value, then decisions about that key need no agreement, because there is no one to agree with. The coordination is pushed into the assignment of keys to nodes, which is a membership problem — solvable with consensus, but solved once and amortized over every subsequent operation. Partitioning a key space is the oldest trick in the book and remains the most effective, because it converts a per-operation coordination cost into a per-reconfiguration coordination cost.

**Escrow.** When the non-monotone constraint is a resource bound — inventory must not go negative, a rate limit must not be exceeded, a quota must not be overspent — the standard move is to divide the resource into allocations and hand each node a share. A node can then spend its share with purely local, monotone decisions, because spending within a budget can never violate the global bound. Coordination is needed only when a node exhausts its share and requests more, and the amount of coordination is proportional to the number of refills rather than the number of operations. The arithmetic is worth doing explicitly: if each node holds a budget of a hundred units, you have divided your coordination cost by a hundred, at the price of leaving up to a hundred units of the resource stranded at a node that may never use them. Escrow trades utilization for availability, and the trade is tunable by choosing the allocation size.

The availability property that falls out of escrow is the one that motivated the whole discussion. If the coordinator that hands out allocations becomes unreachable, nodes continue serving from their remaining budgets. The system degrades in capacity, not in availability, and the invariant still holds. That is a qualitatively better failure mode than refusing writes, and it comes from reformulating the constraint rather than from a better consensus implementation.

**Narrow the serialization point.** A global log serializes everything; the point of the exercise is to serialize as little as possible. Two operations need to share a serialization point only if they can violate the same invariant. Different invariants can be assigned different coordination domains, and domains that never interact can be independent consensus groups. This is not a novel insight, but it is routinely ignored: systems reach for one log because one log is easy to reason about, and then pay for it on every unrelated write.

**Amortize and batch.** Every coordinated operation is a round trip, and round trips are the unit of cost. A lease of a thousand identifiers costs one round trip; a thousand individual requests cost a thousand. Where the workload permits, batching turns a coordination-dominated system into a communication-dominated one, which is a different regime entirely.

The residue that remains after all of this — the operations that genuinely must agree, on every invocation, with no batching and no delegation — is usually much smaller than the initial design suggested, and it is worth naming precisely. That naming is the deliverable of the exercise, and it is also the thing that determines whether the system stays available under partition: the answer is yes, provided the residue is confined to operations whose failure the business can tolerate, and no, otherwise.

## The boundary is wider than pure monotonicity

There is a final refinement worth flagging, because it is the difference between using the CALM theorem as a tool and treating it as a prohibition. The theorem's "only if" direction is a statement about *consistency*: a non-monotone program cannot have a coordination-free implementation that is guaranteed to produce the serial answer. But many systems do not need the serial answer. They need to preserve an invariant — the balance never goes negative, the seats never oversell — and that is a strictly weaker requirement.

The generalization is called invariant confluence. Informally, a program is invariant-confluent with respect to an invariant if any two states that satisfy the invariant and are reachable by executing the same set of operations in different orders can be merged into a state that also satisfies the invariant, without further coordination. Transfers between accounts of a fixed total are the textbook example: they do not commute as individual operations, but any two states reachable by different orderings of the same transfers can be merged, and the total is preserved either way. Decrements against a shared balance are not invariant-confluent, because two nodes can each decrement below the bound independently and there is no merge that restores it.

This matters because it explains the gap between theory and practice. Systems routinely run non-monotone operations without consensus, and they do so correctly, because the operations commute with respect to the invariant they care about even though they do not commute with respect to the exact state. The CALM theorem tells you where consistency requires coordination; invariant confluence tells you how much of that coordination you can avoid by asking for less. Both are worth having in your head when the next design review asks whether the counter can go negative, and someone reaches for the consensus library before asking what the counter is actually for.

## Monotonicity as an analysis you can run

The CALM theorem is often introduced as a classification of programs, but its practical value is as an analysis you can run on a system you already have. The analysis has four steps, and none of them require a formal methods background.

First, identify the input facts. These are the things the system learns from the outside world: a user registered, an order was placed, a sensor reading arrived, a payment cleared. In a Datalog-style formulation, each of these is a ground atom, and the input instance is the set of all such atoms observed so far. The crucial move is to treat the input as a set rather than a stream. A stream has an order; a set does not. If your computation depends on the order in which facts arrive, that dependence is exactly the thing that will force coordination, and making it explicit is the first step toward removing it.

Second, identify the output facts. These are the conclusions the system publishes: this user is active, this order is shippable, this account has a positive balance, this seat is sold. Again, write them as a set. If the output is a single value rather than a set of facts, ask what facts that value summarizes. A count is a summary of a set of counted facts. A minimum is a summary of a set of values. A boolean is a summary of a set of conditions. The summary is where non-monotonicity often hides, because the underlying facts may accumulate while the summary changes.

Third, write the rules that derive outputs from inputs and from other outputs. If the rules are recursive, as in reachability or transitive closure, the program computes a fixed point. Monotonicity of the rules guarantees that the fixed point is unique and independent of evaluation order, which is the formal content of confluence. If any rule contains negation, aggregation, or a comparison that can flip from true to false as more facts arrive, the fixed point may depend on order, and coordination enters.

Fourth, check the polarity of every derived predicate. A predicate is monotone if its extension grows with the input. Reachability is monotone: if there is a path from A to B in a graph, adding edges cannot remove it. Shortest distance is not monotone in the same sense: adding a shorter edge can reduce the distance, so a fact like distance(A,B) = 5 may need to be retracted. But the same computation can be represented monotonically by deriving facts of the form distanceAtMost(A,B,d). Adding edges can only add such facts, never remove them. The exact shortest distance is then recovered at read time as the smallest d for which distanceAtMost(A,B,d) holds. The state is monotone; the read is not. This representation trick is one of the most useful tools in the whole subject, and it generalizes far beyond graphs.

The lesson of the four steps is that monotonicity is a property of a *formulation*, not of a problem. Two systems that answer the same question can differ in whether they require coordination, because one represents the answer as a set of accumulating facts and the other represents it as a mutable value. The mutable value is often more convenient to read, but the accumulating facts are what let replicas operate independently.

## Thresholds, polarities, and the representation trick

The representation trick deserves a section of its own because it is the main lever for moving a computation across the coordination boundary. The general pattern is: replace a non-monotone quantity with a monotone family of threshold facts, and compute the quantity at the edge.

Consider a counter that can go up and down. The obvious state is an integer, and the obvious operation is increment or decrement. Under the natural order, increment is monotone and decrement is not. But the counter can be represented as two grow-only counters: one for increments and one for decrements. The state is the pair (inc, dec), ordered pointwise. Both operations only add to one component, so the state is monotone. The current value is inc - dec, computed at read time. Replicas merge by taking the pointwise maximum of each component, which is the join in the product semilattice. The read is non-monotone, but the read is local: any replica that has received all the increments and decrements can compute the same value, and a replica that has received only some of them computes a value that is consistent with its local knowledge.

This is the same construction as the threshold representation. A fact like balance >= k is not monotone if the balance can decrease. But the pair (credits, debits) is monotone, and the predicate credits - debits >= k is evaluated at read time. If you need to enforce the invariant that the balance never goes negative, the monotone state does not enforce it; it records the history that lets you detect a violation. Detection is monotone: once you have observed a state where debits exceeded credits, that observation is a fact that never retracts. Enforcement is not monotone, and it is the part that requires coordination.

The polarity of a threshold is a free choice in many specifications. 'At least ten items have been sold' is monotone: once true, always true. 'Fewer than ten items remain' is not. If the business requirement can be phrased in the monotone polarity, the system can often avoid coordination entirely. This is not a word game. A recommendation system that says 'show this item once at least a hundred users have liked it' is monotone. A system that says 'show this item while fewer than a hundred users have disliked it' is not, because a new dislike can retract the recommendation. The first can be computed independently on every replica; the second requires a global view of dislikes, or a local approximation with a bounded error.

The same reasoning applies to invariants. An invariant is a predicate over states that must remain true. Some invariants are preserved by monotone operations: if the invariant is 'the set of approved users only grows,' then adding an approval cannot break it. Other invariants are not: 'the balance never goes negative' can be broken by a decrement. The design question is whether the invariant can be enforced by a monotone operation on a derived state. Escrow is the canonical example: instead of allowing arbitrary decrements against a shared balance, the system divides the balance into allocations and lets each node decrement its own allocation. Decrementing an allocation is monotone with respect to the invariant, because the allocation is a local budget and spending it cannot exceed the global bound. The coordination moves to the allocation step, which happens once per allocation rather than once per decrement.

## A worked boundary: inventory and uniqueness

Inventory is the example that makes the boundary concrete. The business invariant is that available inventory never goes negative. The input facts are receipts (stock arrived), orders (a customer wants to buy), and cancellations. The output facts are reservations and shipments. The naive formulation has a mutable available count, and every order decrements it. That decrement is non-monotone, so the naive formulation requires coordination on every order.

The escrow formulation changes the state. The warehouse divides the stock into allocations and assigns each allocation to a node. Each node's state is a grow-only record of receipts and a local budget. An order is accepted if it can be satisfied from the local budget, and accepting it adds a reservation fact. The reservation fact is monotone: it is never removed. The local budget decreases, but the decrease is a local decision that cannot violate the global invariant, because the node never spends more than it was allocated. When a node exhausts its budget, it requests a refill from a coordinator. The coordinator's decision is coordinated, but it happens once per refill, not once per order.

The failure mode is instructive. If the coordinator is unreachable, nodes continue accepting orders from their remaining budgets. The system does not refuse writes; it degrades in capacity. Some inventory may be stranded at nodes that never use it, so utilization is lower than a perfectly coordinated system. The trade is tunable: larger allocations mean fewer refills and more stranded inventory; smaller allocations mean more refills and better utilization. The invariant holds in either case, because the sum of local budgets never exceeds the total stock.

Uniqueness is the other canonical non-monotone constraint. 'No two accounts share an email address' is violated by adding an account, so the set of valid states is not closed under growth. There is no coordination-free implementation that guarantees global uniqueness for an unbounded, dynamic key space. The practical responses are all attempts to bound the problem. Partition the key space by a hash of the key, so that each key has a single writer; the writer can check uniqueness locally because it is the only node that can create the key. This works if the partition is stable and the key space is fixed. If keys can be created in any partition, you need a reservation range or a claim protocol. A claim protocol can be coordination-free if it uses a deterministic conflict resolution rule and accepts that two nodes may temporarily both believe they own the key; the system then resolves the conflict by a total order on claims. That is not the same as guaranteeing uniqueness at write time. It guarantees eventual uniqueness, which is often enough for usernames but not for financial identifiers.

The difference between inventory and uniqueness is the difference between an invariant that can be enforced by escrow and one that cannot. Inventory can be bounded by allocating a finite resource. Uniqueness is a global property of an unbounded set, and bounding it requires either partitioning the set or accepting temporary violations. Recognizing which case you are in is the first step of the design review.

## Testing and operating the boundary

Once a system is split into a monotone core and a coordinated residue, the testing strategy should follow the split. The monotone core can be tested with property-based tests that generate random sets of input facts and check that the output is a subset of the output for any superset of those facts. If the test finds a counterexample, the program is not monotone, and the counterexample is usually a small, understandable scenario. The coordinated residue can be tested with model checking or with fault-injection tests that partition the network and verify that the invariant is preserved or that the system degrades in the expected way.

Operation is where the boundary becomes visible. A monotone core can be reconciled by exchanging state and merging; the merge is a join, and the result is independent of the order of exchanges. The coordinated residue needs a log, a leader, or a lease, and its health must be monitored separately. The most common operational failure is not a bug in the consensus protocol; it is a monotone core that was assumed to be monotone but is not. A read path that computes a non-monotone summary over a monotone state will show different answers on different replicas, and the difference will look like a consistency bug even though the state is converging correctly. The fix is usually to move the summary to a single reader or to make the summary itself monotone.

Garbage collection is the other operational boundary. Tombstones and causal contexts grow without bound, and removing them requires a stability watermark. The watermark is a coordination problem, but it is a background problem. The write path remains coordination-free, and the garbage collector can run on a schedule, with a lease, or with a conservative clock-skew assumption. The important thing is to make the watermark explicit, because an implicit watermark is an implicit coordination requirement, and implicit requirements are the ones that surprise you during an incident.

## The economics of coordination

The final piece of the design vocabulary is economic. Coordination is not free, and it is not infinitely expensive. It is a resource that can be bought in bulk, spent on the critical path, or amortized across many operations. The escrow example shows the arithmetic: a refill costs one round trip and enables many local operations. The batch example is the same: a lease of a thousand identifiers costs one round trip and enables a thousand local decisions. The design question is not 'can we avoid coordination?' but 'what is the largest unit of coordination we can buy, and how much of the workload can be served from that unit?'

The answer depends on the latency budget and the failure tolerance. A cross-continental round trip is a hundred milliseconds; if the user-facing latency budget is fifty milliseconds, coordination cannot be on the critical path. A same-rack round trip is a hundred microseconds; if the budget is ten milliseconds, coordination is affordable, and the engineering effort may be better spent elsewhere. The CALM theorem does not make that decision for you. It tells you which parts of the system can be made coordination-free, and it tells you what you give up if you insist on coordinating the rest. The rest is economics, and the economics are local to your system.

## Questions to ask before reaching for consensus

- What is the input fact set, and does the computation depend on the order in which facts arrive?
- Which outputs are derived by negation, aggregation, or a threshold that can flip from true to false?
- Can the non-monotone output be represented as a monotone family of threshold facts, with the summary computed at read time?
- If the invariant is a resource bound, can it be enforced by escrow, and what is the allocation size that balances refill cost against stranded capacity?
- If the constraint is uniqueness, can the key space be partitioned so that each key has a single writer?
- What is the smallest set of operations that must share a serialization point, and can they be batched or leased?
- Where does garbage collection require a stability watermark, and who establishes it?
- What does the system do when the coordinated component is unavailable: refuse writes, degrade capacity, or violate the invariant?

These questions do not replace the theorem. They are the theorem applied to the messy particulars of a real system, and they are usually enough to turn a vague argument about consistency into a concrete design decision.

## Coordination domains and the algebra of composition

The CALM theorem classifies a program, but real systems are compositions. Composition is not automatically monotone-preserving. If P and Q are monotone over the same input, their union is monotone: adding facts can only add outputs from each. If Q consumes P's output, monotonicity is preserved as long as Q's rules are monotone in that input. The danger is a non-monotone rule anywhere in the dependency chain. A single negation, aggregation, or threshold can infect every predicate downstream.

This suggests a useful abstraction: the coordination domain of a program. The coordination domain is the smallest set of facts whose relative order or agreement can affect the output. For a monotone program, it is empty. For a non-monotone program, it is the set of facts that participate in the non-monotone rule, together with the facts that rule depends on. The design goal is not to eliminate the coordination domain but to shrink it and move it off the critical path.

Dependency graphs make this concrete. Draw a graph whose nodes are predicates and whose edges go from body predicates to head predicates. A cycle in this graph means recursion. A cycle that passes through negation means the program cannot be stratified, and the fixed point may depend on evaluation order. If there is no cycle through negation, the program can be stratified: predicates are partitioned into strata, and a stratum is computed only after all lower strata are complete. Within a stratum, the rules are monotone, so any node can evaluate them independently. At stratum boundaries, the computation becomes non-monotone, and that is where coordination is required.

The composition rule is then: monotone components compose freely; non-monotone components require a boundary. The boundary is not a service boundary. It is a predicate boundary. Two services can exchange monotone facts without coordination, while a single service can contain a non-monotone predicate that needs coordination. The theorem is about computations, not deployments.

## Stratification, seals, and epochs

Stratified negation gives a template for coordination-free systems with non-monotone decisions. Compute the lower strata monotonically, then seal the stratum, then evaluate the non-monotone rule over the sealed facts. The seal is the coordination event. It says: no more facts of this class will arrive for this epoch. Once the seal is in place, the non-monotone rule can run on any node that has the sealed facts, and it will produce the same answer.

The seal can be implemented in several ways. A time-based epoch uses bounded clock skew: if all nodes agree that timestamps are within delta, then a fact with timestamp older than the epoch boundary can be considered final. This is coordination-free in the sense that no node waits for another, but it relies on an assumption about clocks. A consensus-based seal uses a round of agreement to choose the epoch number and the cut. That is coordination, but it happens once per epoch rather than once per operation. A watermark-based seal uses progress of a stream: when all inputs have advanced past a point, the point is sealed. This is common in streaming systems and is a form of coordination that is amortized across many events.

The trade is freshness against coordination. A short epoch means frequent seals and fresh non-monotone decisions, but more coordination. A long epoch means rare seals and stale decisions, but less coordination. The correct epoch length is a business decision, not a technical one. If a uniqueness check can tolerate one minute of staleness, the system can seal once per minute and resolve conflicts in batch. If it cannot, the epoch must be short, and the coordination cost rises.

This is where the CALM theorem connects to practical stream processing. A windowed aggregation is a monotone accumulation followed by a non-monotone close. The window is the stratum. The trigger is the seal. The output is the non-monotone decision. Systems that do this well are not avoiding coordination; they are amortizing it and making it explicit.

## Schema evolution and monotonicity

Schema evolution is a common source of hidden non-monotonicity. Adding a field is usually monotone if old facts are interpreted with a default. Changing the meaning of a field is not. If a migration rewrites historical facts, it retracts the old facts and adds new ones, which is a non-monotone operation. A system that does this on every schema change will require coordination for the migration, even if its steady-state operations are monotone.

The monotone alternative is to treat schema as data. Every fact carries a schema version, and every schema version is itself a fact. A migration adds new schema facts and new derived facts; it does not delete old ones. Read paths interpret facts according to the schema version that was current when the fact was written. This is more storage and more read-time logic, but it preserves monotonicity. Corrections are handled the same way: a correction is a new fact with a higher version, not a deletion. The read path resolves to the latest version. This is last-writer-wins at the schema level, and it inherits the caveats of LWW: the underlying versioned facts are monotone, but the interpreted value is not.

Backfills are the other schema-adjacent hazard. A backfill recomputes derived facts from historical inputs. If the derivation is monotone, the backfill only adds facts, and it can run coordination-free. If the derivation contains a non-monotone rule, the backfill may need to retract previously derived facts. The safe pattern is to write backfilled facts into a new versioned namespace and let the read path prefer the new namespace once it is complete. The completion of the backfill is a seal, and the seal is the coordination event.

## Read paths and the cost of non-monotone views

A monotone state does not guarantee a monotone view. This is the most common way a system that looks coordination-free becomes inconsistent in practice. The state is a set of facts. The view is a query over those facts. If the query is monotone, it can be evaluated independently on every replica, and the results converge. If the query contains negation, aggregation, or a threshold from above, the view can fluctuate as the state grows.

Incremental view maintenance makes the cost visible. A monotone view can be maintained by unioning deltas: when a new fact arrives, derive its consequences and add them. A non-monotone view requires deletion propagation. When a fact is removed, the system must determine which derived facts depended on it and remove them too. In a monotone state, facts are never removed, so deletion propagation seems unnecessary. But the view may still need to retract a conclusion: the conclusion 'there are no shipments for this order' was true, and now it is false. The underlying facts only grew, but the view retracted. To maintain that view incrementally, the system must know when the retraction happens, and that requires either a global view or a seal.

The design response is to separate the monotone core from the non-monotone view. Push monotone filters and projections to the edge. Push non-monotone aggregation to a collector. The collector does not need consensus if it is the only node evaluating the view; it needs to gather facts, which is communication. If the collector is unavailable, the edge can still serve monotone views. The non-monotone view is degraded or stale, but the system remains available. This is a different failure mode from refusing writes, and it is often the right one.

## Approximate monotonicity and bounded error

Exact non-monotone constraints can sometimes be replaced by monotone approximations. A Bloom filter is the canonical example. Adding an element sets bits; membership tests return 'possibly present' or 'definitely absent.' The 'possibly present' predicate is monotone: once true, it stays true. The 'definitely absent' predicate is non-monotone. If the business can tolerate false positives, a Bloom filter can provide coordination-free duplicate detection. Two nodes may both believe an element is new, but the conflict can be resolved later. The error is bounded by the filter's false-positive rate, and the rate is a tunable design parameter.

The same pattern applies to cardinality estimation, heavy hitters, and quantiles. Sketches such as HyperLogLog and count-min sketches are mergeable and monotone under their respective join operations. They do not give exact answers, but they give answers with bounded error, and they let every node operate on local state. The coordination budget is traded for an error budget. This is often a better trade than a consensus round on every operation, especially when the exact answer is not actionable.

Bounded staleness is a related relaxation. If the business can tolerate an answer that is stale by T, the system can avoid coordination for T. This is not the same as monotonicity, because the underlying computation may still be non-monotone. But it changes the requirement: instead of 'the answer must be the serial answer,' the requirement becomes 'the answer must be within T of the serial answer.' That weaker requirement may be achievable with local state and periodic reconciliation. The CALM theorem gives the exact boundary; bounded staleness moves the boundary by asking for less.

## The boundary as an architectural artifact

The practical output of this analysis is an architectural artifact: a boundary diagram. For each invariant in the system, list the input facts, the monotone core, the coordination domain, the seal mechanism, and the failure mode. The diagram should show which operations are coordination-free and which are not. It should not be a service diagram, because coordination boundaries cross service boundaries and sometimes run through them.

The diagram has three regions. The first is the monotone core: operations that only add facts, can be evaluated on any replica, and converge by merging. The second is the coordination domain: operations that require agreement, with the smallest possible scope. The third is the seal: the mechanism that closes an epoch or a stratum so that non-monotone decisions can run. The seal is where latency and availability costs appear, so it should be as infrequent as the business allows.

Operationally, the boundary should be monitored. A coordination rate that spikes is a signal that a monotone core has acquired a non-monotone dependency. A seal that is delayed is a signal that freshness is degrading. An error bound that is exceeded is a signal that an approximation is no longer safe. These are not consensus-protocol metrics; they are boundary metrics. They tell you whether the design assumptions still hold.

The CALM theorem does not design the system. It tells you where the design decisions are. The boundary diagram is the place to record them, and the next design review is the place to question them.

## The Limits of Local Knowledge: Quorum-Free but Not Partition-Oblivious

Coordination-free execution is often mistaken for transport-independence. It is not. A node that never receives facts from a partitioned peer can still produce a locally correct answer, but that answer is correct only with respect to the facts the node has. The theorem's consistency definition assumes all facts eventually arrive. In a real system, *eventually* is bounded by business patience. The design must distinguish between a fact that is absent because it has not arrived and a fact that is absent because it does not exist. The former is temporary and monotone; the latter is a closed-world assumption, and closed-world reasoning is non-monotone. This is why "no shipment exists" is dangerous even when the shipment set is a grow-only set: the query treats missing facts as definitive. The fix is to carry an explicit completeness fact — a watermark, a sequence number, an "I have seen everything up to here" marker — and to make the non-monotone read conditional on it. That marker is a coordination artifact, but it can be produced by a single writer or a per-partition sequence, not by consensus. The insight is that monotonicity applies to the facts, while completeness applies to the observer. A replica can be monotone in its state and still non-monotone in its conclusions if it confuses "not yet seen" with "not true."

The same distinction explains why monotone systems still need reconciliation boundaries. A merge is a join, but a join only defines the state that results from the facts each replica has. It does not define when a replica has enough facts to answer a question. That boundary is an interface contract: the producer promises that no fact below the watermark will appear later, and the consumer promises to withhold non-monotone conclusions until the watermark advances. The contract is not free, but it is local to the read path and can be implemented with per-partition counters. It is the difference between a system that is coordination-free in its writes and one that is merely uncoordinated in its reads.
