# Parallel Betweenness Centrality: Source-Parallel Brandes, Dependency Graphs, and the Engineering of Graph Analytics at Scale

Betweenness centrality is one of those graph metrics that looks simple in a paper and becomes a systems problem in production. The definition is compact: a vertex is central if many shortest paths pass through it. The exact algorithm is also compact, at least in its sequential form. But the moment we try to compute exact betweenness centrality on a graph with hundreds of millions of edges, on a machine with tens or hundreds of cores, the interesting questions stop being about the formula and start being about memory traffic, load imbalance, accumulation order, graph layout, and the cost of preserving determinism.

This article examines exact betweenness centrality through the lens of Brandes' algorithm and source-level parallelism. The goal is not to present a new centrality measure or to survey every approximation technique. The goal is to treat betweenness centrality as a parallel systems workload: what work is independent, what work is not, where the dependencies live, what data structures dominate the memory hierarchy, and which trade-offs are unavoidable when the graph no longer fits comfortably in cache.

The assumptions matter. We will focus on exact betweenness centrality for static graphs with non-negative edge weights. Unweighted graphs are the special case where breadth-first search replaces Dijkstra's algorithm. We will assume a shared-memory machine with a read-only graph structure and enough memory to hold the graph plus per-thread workspace. We will not assume that the graph is dense, that the degree distribution is uniform, or that the vertices are numbered in a way that correlates with work. Those assumptions are exactly where many naive parallel implementations fail.

## The Metric and the Workload

For a graph G = (V, E), the betweenness centrality of a vertex v is commonly defined as the sum over all ordered pairs of distinct vertices s and t, with s and t different from v, of the fraction of shortest paths from s to t that pass through v. If sigma_st is the number of shortest paths from s to t, and sigma_st(v) is the number of those paths that pass through v, then the contribution of the pair (s, t) to v is sigma_st(v) / sigma_st. The sum is over all such pairs. For undirected graphs, the ordered-pair definition counts each unordered pair twice, so implementations often divide by two at the end. For directed graphs, no such correction is needed.

That definition is mathematically clean. Computationally, it hides a large amount of work. A direct implementation would run a shortest-path search for every pair of sources and targets. Brandes' algorithm avoids that by reorganizing the computation around each source. For a fixed source s, we compute shortest-path distances and shortest-path counts from s to every reachable vertex. Then we accumulate dependencies in reverse order of distance from s. The dependency of s on a vertex v, often written as delta_s(v), captures how much of the shortest-path structure rooted at s flows through v. The final betweenness centrality of v is the sum of delta_s(v) over all sources s, with s not equal to v.

The key structural fact is that the dependency accumulation for a fixed source is not a simple reduction over independent vertices. It is a reverse traversal of the shortest-path DAG rooted at s. A vertex v receives contributions from vertices w that are farther from s and for which v lies on a shortest path from s to w. The contribution from w to v is proportional to the number of shortest paths from s to v divided by the number of shortest paths from s to w, multiplied by one plus the accumulated dependency of w. In symbols, delta_s(v) = sum over w in successors(v) of (sigma_s(v) / sigma_s(w)) * (1 + delta_s(w)). The sum is over successors in the shortest-path DAG, not over all graph neighbors. This recurrence is the heart of Brandes' algorithm, and it is also the source of most of the parallelization difficulty.

The sequential algorithm is straightforward. For each source s, run a breadth-first search or Dijkstra's algorithm to compute distances and shortest-path counts. Record the order in which vertices are settled. Then process vertices in reverse order, propagating dependencies from successors to predecessors. The final accumulation into the global betweenness array is a simple addition. The per-source work is O(V + E) for unweighted graphs and O(E + V log V) for weighted graphs with a binary heap, ignoring the cost of the final accumulation. Over all sources, the sequential work is O(VE) for unweighted graphs and O(VE + V^2 log V) for weighted graphs. Those expressions are simple, but they conceal a lot of irregularity. The actual cost depends on the reachable subgraph from each source, the degree distribution, the memory layout, and the number of shortest paths that must be represented.

## Brandes' Recurrence Is a Dependency Problem

The first thing to understand about parallel betweenness centrality is that the input graph is not the only graph in the computation. For each source, Brandes' algorithm implicitly builds a shortest-path DAG. The vertices of that DAG are the vertices reachable from the source. The edges are the predecessor relationships discovered during the shortest-path search. The dependency accumulation traverses that DAG in reverse topological order. The input graph may be sparse and well behaved, but the shortest-path DAG can have very different structure. A high-degree vertex can appear as a predecessor for many vertices, creating a large fan-in or fan-out. A long chain of vertices can create a deep dependency chain. A graph with many equal-length shortest paths can create a dense DAG even when the input graph is sparse.

This distinction matters for parallelization. If we parallelize over sources, each source owns a separate shortest-path DAG. There is no cross-source dependency during the per-source computation. The only shared state is the final betweenness array. That is the attractive part of source parallelism. It is also the part that can mislead us into thinking the problem is embarrassingly parallel. At the top level, it is. At the level of a single source, the dependency accumulation is a graph traversal with real dependencies. If we later want to parallelize within a source, we must respect those dependencies. If we only parallelize across sources, we avoid the dependency problem but inherit a different set of problems: load imbalance, memory pressure, and nondeterministic accumulation.

Source parallelism is therefore best understood as a decomposition of the outer loop. Each worker takes one or more sources, runs the full per-source Brandes computation, and contributes to the global centrality array. The per-source computation is sequential internally. This is a common and often sensible design. It preserves the sequential algorithm's correctness argument, keeps the per-source data structures thread-local, and avoids fine-grained synchronization. The cost is that the parallel granularity is the source. If the graph has few vertices relative to the number of cores, or if the work per source is highly variable, source parallelism can leave cores idle. If the graph has many vertices, source parallelism usually has enough independent work, but the memory traffic and the final reduction become the dominant concerns.

The final reduction is where source parallelism becomes less innocent. Each source produces a vector of contributions, one per vertex. If every worker writes directly into a shared centrality array, the writes must be synchronized. Atomic floating-point additions are possible, but they are expensive and they introduce nondeterminism. Per-thread accumulation buffers avoid atomics but multiply memory usage by the number of threads. A two-level reduction can reduce contention, but it adds a second pass over the centrality array. The right choice depends on the number of vertices, the number of threads, and whether reproducibility is required. There is no single answer that is independent of the deployment.

## Source Parallelism: The Obvious Decomposition and Its Hidden Costs

The obvious parallel loop is over sources. For each source s in a work queue, compute the shortest-path DAG, accumulate dependencies, and add the per-source contributions to the global result. The loop is easy to express and easy to reason about. The hidden costs are in the details.

The first cost is load imbalance. The work for a source is not determined by the source's degree alone. It is determined by the size and shape of the reachable subgraph. A source in a dense cluster may reach a large fraction of the graph. A source in a peripheral tree may reach only a small component. If sources are assigned statically by vertex identifier, the assignment may correlate with graph structure in unfortunate ways. If sources are assigned dynamically, the scheduler can balance the work, but dynamic assignment adds synchronization and can hurt locality. The trade-off is between utilization and overhead. For graphs with highly skewed reachable-set sizes, dynamic scheduling with coarse chunks is often necessary. For graphs with uniform reachable-set sizes, static assignment may be cheaper and more predictable.

The second cost is memory. The per-source computation needs arrays for distances, shortest-path counts, predecessor lists, and the order in which vertices are settled. Some of these arrays can be reused across sources within a thread. Others cannot. The predecessor lists are the most troublesome. A vertex can have many predecessors in the shortest-path DAG. Storing predecessor lists as per-vertex dynamic arrays is convenient but expensive: each source may allocate and free many small vectors. Storing predecessors as edge indices in a flat array is more cache-friendly, but it requires knowing the total number of predecessor entries in advance or using a two-pass approach. A common compromise is to store predecessor lists in a per-thread arena with chunked allocation. The arena avoids per-vertex allocation overhead and keeps the memory footprint bounded, but it complicates the code and can increase the working set.

The third cost is the final accumulation. If each thread has its own centrality buffer, the reduction is a simple element-wise sum. That sum is deterministic only if the reduction order is fixed. If threads process sources in a nondeterministic order, the floating-point result can differ from run to run. The differences are usually small, but they are real. For applications that compare centrality rankings across runs, or that need reproducible artifacts, nondeterminism is a problem. The alternatives are to fix the source assignment order, to use a deterministic reduction tree, or to use a numerically stable accumulation scheme. Each alternative has a cost. Fixing the order reduces scheduling flexibility. A deterministic reduction tree adds a synchronization structure. Compensated summation adds arithmetic and memory traffic. The choice depends on whether bitwise reproducibility is a requirement or merely a convenience.

## Work, Span, and the Shape of the Graph

A useful way to reason about source-parallel Brandes is to separate work from span. The total work is the sum of the per-source work. The span, or critical path, is the maximum per-source work if sources are processed in parallel and each source is processed sequentially. In the ideal case, the parallel time is the total work divided by the number of processors, plus the span. Source parallelism has a span equal to the slowest source. That is a simple model, but it explains a lot of observed behavior. If one source reaches almost the entire graph and another source reaches only a small component, the slowest source dominates the tail. Adding more processors does not reduce the span unless the slowest source itself is parallelized or the work is split more finely.

This is why source parallelism alone can hit a scalability wall. The wall is not necessarily caused by synchronization or memory bandwidth. It can be caused by the largest per-source computation. If the graph has a giant component and a few high-degree vertices that reach most of it, the sources corresponding to those vertices are expensive. The parallel schedule may finish most sources quickly and then leave many cores idle while one or a few workers finish the expensive sources. Dynamic scheduling helps only if there are enough other sources to fill the idle cores. If the expensive sources are the last ones, the tail is exposed. A work-stealing scheduler can mitigate this by allowing idle workers to steal smaller chunks, but it cannot split a single source unless the algorithm supports intra-source parallelism.

Intra-source parallelism is harder because the dependency accumulation is a reverse traversal of the shortest-path DAG. The shortest-path search itself can be parallelized in some cases, but the dependency accumulation has dependencies from farther vertices to nearer vertices. A level-synchronous approach can process vertices at the same distance in parallel, but the amount of parallelism at each level depends on the graph. In a graph with a long diameter, each level may contain few vertices, so level-synchronous parallelism has limited width. In a graph with a small diameter and many vertices at each level, level-synchronous parallelism can be effective. The trade-off is between the simplicity of source parallelism and the additional parallelism of intra-source decomposition. Source parallelism is easier to implement and reason about; intra-source parallelism can reduce the span but requires more careful synchronization and memory management.

## Memory Traffic and Locality

Betweenness centrality is often described as a compute-intensive graph algorithm, but in practice it is frequently memory-bound. The per-source shortest-path search reads the graph structure and writes distances and path counts. The dependency accumulation reads predecessor lists and writes dependency values. The final accumulation reads and writes the centrality array. On a large graph, the graph structure itself may not fit in cache. The per-source arrays are sized by the number of vertices and can also exceed cache. The result is a workload with irregular access patterns and limited temporal locality.

The graph representation matters. A compressed sparse row layout stores outgoing edges contiguously and is well suited to breadth-first search and Dijkstra's algorithm. A compressed sparse column layout stores incoming edges contiguously and can be useful for reverse traversals. For Brandes' algorithm, the shortest-path search typically follows outgoing edges, while the dependency accumulation follows predecessor relationships. If predecessor lists are stored explicitly, the accumulation can follow those lists directly. If the graph is stored only in one direction, the accumulation may need to reconstruct predecessor relationships or traverse the graph in reverse. The choice affects both memory traffic and code complexity.

A common design is to store the graph in compressed sparse row format and to build predecessor lists during the shortest-path search. The predecessor lists are then consumed during the dependency accumulation. This avoids a separate reverse graph but increases the memory footprint of the per-source computation. An alternative is to store both outgoing and incoming edges and to use the incoming edges during accumulation. That doubles the graph storage but can reduce the per-source workspace. The trade-off is between static memory overhead and dynamic memory traffic. For a graph that is read many times, the static overhead may be acceptable. For a graph that is already at the memory limit, the dynamic approach may be the only option.

Locality also depends on the order in which vertices are processed. A breadth-first search visits vertices in increasing distance from the source. The order is determined by the graph structure, not by the vertex numbering. If the graph is stored in a cache-friendly order, the search may have good locality. If the graph is stored in an arbitrary order, the search may jump across memory. Graph reordering techniques can improve locality, but they change the vertex numbering and can affect the assignment of sources to workers. The interaction between graph layout and parallel scheduling is one of the less obvious systems issues in betweenness centrality.

## Determinism, Accumulation, and Numerical Drift

The final centrality values are sums of many per-source contributions. In exact arithmetic, the order of summation does not matter. In floating-point arithmetic, it does. Source-parallel execution changes the order in which contributions are added. Even if each per-source computation is deterministic, the global sum can differ from run to run. The differences are usually small, but they can affect rankings when two vertices have nearly equal centrality. They can also affect reproducibility in scientific workflows, regression tests, and audit trails.

There are several ways to handle this. The simplest is to accept nondeterminism and document it. The next is to make the reduction deterministic by fixing the order in which per-thread buffers are combined. That is often enough for reproducibility, even if the per-thread assignment is not fixed. A stronger approach is to fix the assignment of sources to workers and to use a deterministic reduction tree. That gives bitwise reproducibility at the cost of scheduling flexibility. A different approach is to use a numerically stable accumulation scheme, such as compensated summation or pairwise summation, to reduce the error regardless of order. That improves accuracy but does not guarantee bitwise reproducibility.

The right choice depends on the application. If betweenness centrality is used for exploratory analysis, small floating-point differences may be acceptable. If it is used to produce a ranked list that is compared across releases, reproducibility may be more important than the last few percent of parallel efficiency. The engineering point is that determinism is a design decision, not an afterthought. It affects the data structures, the scheduling policy, and the reduction strategy.

## Graph Representation and Traversal Discipline

The graph representation is not a minor implementation detail. It determines how much memory the algorithm touches, how many cache misses it incurs, and how much parallelism is available. A compressed sparse row representation with 32-bit vertex identifiers and 64-bit edge indices is a common baseline. It stores offsets for each vertex and a flat array of edge targets. For weighted graphs, it also stores edge weights. The representation is compact and supports efficient iteration over outgoing edges. It does not directly support predecessor iteration unless the graph is symmetric or a reverse index is built.

For source-parallel Brandes, the graph is read-only during the per-source computation. That is an advantage: multiple threads can read the same graph structure without synchronization. The per-source arrays are the mutable state. They should be thread-local or partitioned by thread to avoid false sharing. The centrality array is the only shared mutable state, and it should be updated in a way that minimizes contention. If the array is large, false sharing is less of an issue because different threads are likely to update different cache lines. If the array is small, false sharing can be significant. Padding the per-thread buffers or using a two-level reduction can help.

The traversal discipline also matters. A breadth-first search that uses a queue can be implemented with a simple array-based queue or a more complex frontier-based approach. The array-based queue is easy to implement and has good locality, but it may not be the best choice for very large graphs. A frontier-based approach that processes vertices level by level can expose more parallelism, but it also creates more synchronization points. For source-parallel Brandes, the per-source search is sequential, so the simpler queue-based approach is often sufficient. The dependency accumulation can use the order in which vertices were settled, which is naturally produced by the search. That order is a topological order of the shortest-path DAG, so processing it in reverse gives a valid dependency order.

## Weighted Graphs and the Cost of Priority Queues

When edges carry non-negative weights, the shortest-path search inside Brandes' algorithm is no longer breadth-first. It becomes Dijkstra's algorithm, or a variant that maintains a priority queue of frontier vertices keyed by tentative distance. The dependency accumulation remains the same in structure: process vertices in reverse order of settled distance, propagate from successors to predecessors. The difference is that the order is now determined by a priority queue rather than a FIFO queue, and the cost of maintaining that order can dominate the per-source work.

The sequential cost of Dijkstra with a binary heap is O(E + V log V) per source, but the constant factors are larger than BFS. Each edge relaxation may trigger a decrease-key operation or a push of a duplicate entry. A decrease-key heap reduces memory but requires an indexable heap. A duplicate-entry heap is simpler and often faster in practice because it avoids the bookkeeping, but it increases the number of heap operations. For Brandes, the heap operations are not the only cost. The algorithm must also record the order in which vertices are settled, update shortest-path counts when equal-distance paths are found, and build predecessor lists. Those operations are memory-intensive and can be more expensive than the heap itself on graphs with high average degree.

The choice of priority queue is therefore a systems decision. A binary heap is general and predictable. A pairing heap or Fibonacci heap has better theoretical bounds for decrease-key but often loses in practice due to pointer chasing and poor cache behavior. A radix heap or bucket queue can be much faster when edge weights are small integers, because it replaces comparisons with array indexing. For floating-point weights, a bucket queue is not directly applicable unless the weights are quantized. Delta-stepping and other bucketed Dijkstra variants expose more parallelism and can be adapted to Brandes, but they change the order in which vertices at the same distance are processed. That order affects the dependency accumulation only if there are ties in distance. If the algorithm processes all vertices at a given distance before moving to the next distance, the dependency accumulation can still be correct, but the predecessor lists must be complete before accumulation begins.

Tie handling is a subtle correctness issue. In an unweighted graph, all edges have the same weight, so equal-distance paths are common. In a weighted graph, equal-distance paths can also occur, especially with integer weights or repeated weights. The shortest-path count sigma_s(v) must include all shortest paths, and the predecessor list must include all predecessors that lie on a shortest path. If the search settles a vertex before all equal-distance predecessors have been discovered, the count and the predecessor list may be incomplete. A standard Dijkstra implementation handles this by allowing multiple heap entries for the same vertex and by updating the count when a new equal-distance path is found. The dependency accumulation must then use the final predecessor lists, not an intermediate version. This is one reason why the per-source computation is usually split into a search phase and an accumulation phase.

Weighted graphs also increase the memory footprint. Edge weights must be stored alongside edge targets, and the priority queue can grow to O(E) entries in the duplicate-entry approach. The per-source arrays for distances and path counts may need 64-bit or floating-point types. If the graph has a wide range of weights, distances can exceed 32-bit integers, and path counts can overflow even 64-bit integers on graphs with many shortest paths. Implementations often use double-precision floating-point for path counts to avoid overflow, but that introduces rounding error and makes exact comparison of counts unreliable. An alternative is to use arbitrary-precision integers or logarithms, but both add cost. In practice, many systems accept floating-point path counts and rely on the fact that the ratios in the dependency recurrence are stable enough for centrality ranking.

Source parallelism for weighted graphs inherits the same outer-loop structure, but the load imbalance can be worse. A source in a region with large weights may settle fewer vertices, while a source in a region with small weights may settle many. The priority queue operations also have variable cost. Dynamic scheduling is usually necessary. The final reduction is unchanged, but the per-source workspace is larger, so memory pressure can limit the number of concurrent sources. If the graph is weighted and the machine has many cores, the bottleneck may shift from the heap to memory bandwidth.

## Intra-Source Parallelism and Level-Synchronous Dependency Accumulation

Source parallelism treats each source as an indivisible unit. When the number of sources is large and the per-source work is balanced, that is enough. When the graph has a few very expensive sources, or when the machine has more cores than there are sources, intra-source parallelism becomes necessary. The goal is to parallelize the work for a single source without violating the dependencies in the shortest-path DAG.

The shortest-path search can be parallelized in several ways. A level-synchronous BFS processes all vertices at distance d before moving to distance d+1. Within a level, vertices can be expanded in parallel. The frontier for the next level is built by having each thread process a subset of the current frontier and append neighbors to thread-local buffers. The buffers are then merged. This approach exposes parallelism proportional to the frontier size. For graphs with a small diameter and wide frontiers, the parallelism can be substantial. For graphs with a large diameter and narrow frontiers, the parallelism is limited. Weighted graphs can use a parallel Dijkstra or delta-stepping, but the synchronization and priority queue contention can be significant.

The dependency accumulation is harder to parallelize. The recurrence processes vertices in reverse order of distance. A level-synchronous approach processes vertices at distance d after all vertices at distance greater than d have been processed. Within a level, vertices can be processed in parallel because they do not depend on each other. Each vertex v at distance d accumulates contributions from its successors, which are at distance d+1 or greater. If the successors have already been processed, the contribution to v can be computed independently. The result is a level-by-level reverse traversal. The parallelism at each level is the number of vertices at that distance. Again, the width of the levels determines the available parallelism.

There are several implementation challenges. First, the dependency values for vertices at the current level must be written, and vertices at the previous level will read them. If the levels are processed in separate parallel phases, a barrier between levels is needed. That barrier can be expensive if the levels are narrow. Second, the accumulation into the global centrality array must be synchronized or buffered. If multiple threads update the same vertex, atomics or per-thread buffers are required. Third, the predecessor lists must be partitioned or traversed in a way that avoids false sharing. Fourth, the order of floating-point additions within a level can vary, so determinism requires a fixed reduction order or a deterministic partitioning of the work.

A common hybrid is to use source parallelism at the outer level and level-synchronous parallelism at the inner level only when the frontier or level width exceeds a threshold. This avoids the overhead of intra-source parallelism for small sources and captures additional parallelism for large ones. The threshold can be based on the number of vertices in the level, the number of edges to process, or the estimated work. The hybrid approach also allows the scheduler to balance the load more finely: a worker can steal a portion of a level rather than an entire source. Work-stealing frameworks can support this if the per-source computation is expressed as a set of tasks.

The shape of the graph determines whether intra-source parallelism helps. In a scale-free graph, a few high-degree vertices can create wide frontiers, but the diameter may be small. In a road network, the diameter is large and the frontiers are narrow, so level-synchronous parallelism may not help much. In a social network, the diameter is small and the frontiers are wide, so it can help. The engineering lesson is that there is no universal parallel decomposition. The right choice depends on the graph's degree distribution, diameter, and the distribution of shortest-path DAG widths.

## Predecessor Representations Under Memory Pressure

The predecessor lists are the largest per-source data structure in Brandes' algorithm. For each vertex, the algorithm must know which vertices precede it on a shortest path from the source. The total number of predecessor entries across all vertices is at most the number of edges in the shortest-path DAG, which can be as large as E. Storing these lists explicitly requires memory proportional to the DAG size. On a large graph, that can be a significant fraction of the graph itself. When many threads run concurrently, the aggregate memory can exceed the available RAM.

There are several strategies for reducing this pressure. The first is to store predecessors as edge indices rather than vertex identifiers. An edge index is a single integer that points into the graph's edge array. If the graph is stored in compressed sparse row format, the edge index identifies both the source and the target. This can reduce the size of each predecessor entry and improve locality. The second is to store predecessors in a flat array with offsets, similar to CSR. The offsets array has one entry per vertex, and the flat array stores the predecessor edge indices. This avoids per-vertex dynamic allocation and makes the memory layout predictable. The third is to use a two-pass approach: first compute the shortest-path distances and counts, then recompute the predecessor relationships during the dependency accumulation by scanning incoming edges. This avoids storing predecessor lists entirely, but it requires either a reverse graph or a way to iterate incoming edges. If the graph is stored in both directions, the reverse edges can be used. If not, the algorithm can scan all edges to find those that satisfy the predecessor condition, which is much more expensive.

A fourth strategy is to compress the predecessor lists. Many vertices have a single predecessor. A compact representation can store a flag for single-predecessor vertices and a full list only for vertices with multiple predecessors. This can reduce memory significantly on graphs where most shortest paths are unique. A fifth strategy is to bound the number of predecessors stored per vertex. That changes the algorithm from exact to approximate, but it may be acceptable for exploratory analysis. The approximation error depends on the graph and the bound. In general, truncating predecessor lists is dangerous because it can break the path-count ratios. A safer approximation is to sample sources, not to truncate predecessors.

Path counts also consume memory and can overflow. If sigma_s(v) is stored as a 32-bit integer, it can overflow on graphs with many shortest paths. A 64-bit integer is safer but still not unbounded. Floating-point path counts avoid overflow but introduce rounding. Some implementations use logarithms of path counts to keep the dynamic range manageable, but then the dependency recurrence requires exponentials or careful scaling. The choice depends on the graph and the required accuracy. For exact betweenness centrality, the path counts must be exact enough to compute the ratios correctly. In practice, double-precision floating-point is often used, and the resulting centrality values are treated as exact for ranking purposes.

## Distributed and Accelerator Considerations

When the graph no longer fits in the memory of a single machine, betweenness centrality must be distributed. The standard approach is to partition the graph across processes, replicate a subset of vertices as ghosts, and run per-source Brandes computations that traverse partition boundaries. The source loop can be distributed: each process owns a subset of sources. For each source, the shortest-path search may need to visit vertices on other processes. That requires communication. The dependency accumulation also requires communication because a vertex on one process may have successors on another.

The communication pattern is irregular and depends on the graph partition. A vertex-cut partition can reduce communication for high-degree vertices, while an edge-cut partition can reduce it for graphs with good community structure. The per-source search can be implemented with a distributed BFS or Dijkstra, where each process maintains a local frontier and exchanges boundary vertices. The dependency accumulation can be done in reverse levels, with messages carrying dependency contributions. The final centrality values are reduced across processes. The main challenges are load imbalance, communication overhead, and fault tolerance. If one partition contains a high-degree vertex that is visited by many sources, it can become a hotspot. Replication and caching can help, but they increase memory usage.

Accelerators such as GPUs introduce a different set of trade-offs. A GPU has high memory bandwidth and many lightweight threads, but it also has limited memory capacity and requires coalesced access patterns. Source parallelism maps well to GPUs if each thread or warp processes a source. However, the per-source data structures must fit in GPU memory, and the graph must be stored in a format that supports coalesced reads. The dependency accumulation can suffer from warp divergence because different vertices have different numbers of predecessors. Atomics are available but can be slow when many threads update the same centrality values. A common approach is to use per-thread or per-block accumulation buffers and then reduce them. Determinism on GPUs is even harder because the order of atomic operations is not fixed. If reproducibility is required, the reduction must be structured deterministically. One approach is to assign each block a fixed range of sources and to reduce block-local buffers in a fixed tree order, but that assignment can reduce the available parallelism and may leave some blocks idle. The tension between determinism and utilization is not unique to GPUs; it appears whenever the reduction order is part of the performance model.

## Scheduling and Work Stealing for Irregular Sources

Source-parallel Brandes is an irregular workload. The cost of a source is not a function of its identifier, its degree, or any single local property. It is a function of the reachable subgraph, the number of shortest paths, the shape of the shortest-path DAG, and the cost of the priority queue when edges are weighted. A scheduler that ignores this irregularity will either over-partition the work and pay synchronization overhead, or under-partition it and leave cores idle. The practical question is how to assign sources to workers so that the tail of the schedule is short without turning the scheduler itself into the bottleneck.

Static chunking is the simplest policy. Sources are divided into contiguous ranges, and each worker receives one range. If the graph has a uniform degree distribution and the reachable-set sizes do not vary much, static chunking can be efficient because it avoids synchronization and keeps each worker's access pattern predictable. On real graphs, however, the reachable-set sizes often follow a heavy-tailed distribution. A few sources reach a large fraction of the graph, while many sources reach only a small neighborhood. Static assignment can produce a schedule in which one worker processes several expensive sources while others finish early. The result is a long tail that grows with the number of cores, because the expensive work cannot be subdivided.

Dynamic scheduling addresses this by assigning sources from a shared queue as workers become available. The queue can be a simple atomic counter over a source array. Each worker atomically increments the counter, takes the next source, and processes it. This policy balances the total work well when the number of sources is much larger than the number of workers. It also introduces contention on the counter and reduces locality because consecutive sources are not necessarily processed by the same worker. The contention is usually small if the per-source work is large, but it can become visible when sources are cheap. Guided scheduling is a middle ground: the chunk size starts large and shrinks as the remaining work decreases. This reduces both the number of scheduling operations and the tail, at the cost of a slightly more complex scheduler.

Work stealing is a more adaptive alternative. Each worker maintains a deque of sources. When a worker finishes its own deque, it steals from the tail of another worker's deque. The owner takes from the head, which tends to preserve locality, while the thief takes from the tail, which tends to steal larger chunks. Work stealing is effective when the cost of a source is unknown and when the graph has a few very expensive sources. It also composes naturally with intra-source parallelism: a worker that is processing a large source can publish sub-tasks for the levels of the shortest-path DAG, and idle workers can steal those sub-tasks. The main costs are the bookkeeping of the deques and the potential for stolen tasks to access data that is not in the thief's cache. For large graphs, the cache cost is often secondary to the benefit of reducing the tail.

A useful refinement is to estimate source cost before scheduling. A cheap pre-pass can compute the degree of each source, the size of its connected component, or a sample of its reachable set. These estimates are not exact, but they can be used to sort sources so that expensive sources are scheduled first. Scheduling expensive sources early is a classic list-scheduling heuristic: it reduces the chance that a single expensive source becomes the final task and exposes the tail. The pre-pass adds overhead, but it can be amortized over many sources. If the graph is static and the same centrality computation is run repeatedly, the estimates can be cached and reused. If the graph changes between runs, the estimates must be recomputed or invalidated.

Task granularity is the other half of the scheduling problem. A source is a natural task, but it may be too coarse for a machine with many cores and too fine for a machine with few cores. The right granularity depends on the per-source work and the cost of creating and scheduling a task. For unweighted graphs with small reachable sets, grouping several sources into one task can reduce overhead. For weighted graphs with large reachable sets, splitting a source into level-based sub-tasks can reduce the tail. A hybrid scheduler can start with source-level tasks and split a task only when a worker becomes idle and no source-level tasks remain. This avoids the overhead of fine-grained tasks for the common case while preserving the ability to use idle cores for the largest sources.

Thread affinity and oversubscription also matter. If the runtime allows the operating system to migrate threads between cores, the per-thread workspace may lose cache locality and NUMA locality. Pinning workers to cores can improve performance, but it can also reduce the scheduler's ability to balance work if the pinned cores have different memory access costs. Oversubscribing the machine with more threads than cores is usually counterproductive for a memory-bound workload, because the extra threads compete for memory bandwidth and cache. The best policy is often to use one worker per hardware thread, to pin workers when the runtime supports it, and to let work stealing handle imbalance rather than relying on the OS scheduler.

## NUMA, Memory Allocation, and Reduction Hierarchies

On a multi-socket machine, memory access is not uniform. A thread on socket 0 reading a graph partition allocated on socket 1 pays a remote latency and consumes interconnect bandwidth. For source-parallel Brandes, the graph is read-only and shared, so the natural choice is to interleave its pages across sockets or to replicate it per socket. Interleaving balances bandwidth but increases latency for any single access. Replication reduces remote traffic but multiplies memory usage. The right choice depends on the graph size and the number of sockets. If the graph fits in the aggregate memory but not in a single socket's memory, interleaving is the only option. If the graph is small enough to replicate, per-socket copies can reduce remote traffic and improve locality, especially when the per-source arrays are also socket-local.

The per-source arrays are thread-local and should be allocated by the thread that uses them. If the arrays are allocated by the main thread and then used by workers, the pages may be placed on the wrong socket. First-touch initialization is a simple remedy: each worker writes to its own arrays before the parallel loop begins, forcing the operating system to place the pages on the worker's socket. The initialization adds a pass over the arrays, but it is usually negligible compared with the per-source work. Alternatively, the runtime can bind memory to the worker's socket using an explicit memory policy. The best approach depends on the operating system and the available APIs, but the principle is the same: allocate where you use.

Memory allocation itself can become a bottleneck. The per-source computation needs arrays for distances, path counts, predecessor lists, and the settled order. If these arrays are allocated and freed for every source, the allocator becomes a serialization point and the memory footprint fluctuates. A better design is to allocate the arrays once per worker and reuse them across sources. The arrays must be cleared or reset between sources, but clearing a large array is cheaper than allocating it. For predecessor lists, which vary in size, a per-thread arena with chunked allocation is a common solution. The arena allocates large blocks from the system and carves them into smaller chunks as needed. When a source is finished, the arena's bump pointer is reset, and the memory is reused for the next source. This avoids per-vertex allocation and keeps the working set stable. The trade-off is that the arena may hold more memory than a source strictly needs, because the high-water mark of one source determines the arena size for all sources. If the distribution of predecessor-list sizes is heavy-tailed, the arena can be much larger than the average source requires.

False sharing is another memory-level concern. The global centrality array is shared, and different workers may update adjacent entries. If two workers write to different vertices that happen to share a cache line, the cache line ping-pongs between cores even though the logical updates are independent. Padding the per-thread centrality buffers to cache-line boundaries avoids this problem, at the cost of increased memory usage. The same applies to per-thread counters, flags, and reduction buffers. On modern processors, a cache line is typically 64 bytes, and alignment attributes can be used to pad structures. The padding is a small price for avoiding a large amount of coherence traffic.

The reduction hierarchy is the final stage of the computation. If each worker has a private centrality buffer, the buffers must be summed into a global result. A flat reduction in which every worker adds its buffer to the global array with atomics is simple but can be slow and nondeterministic. A hierarchical reduction is usually better: workers first reduce their buffers into per-socket or per-group buffers, and then the group buffers are reduced into the global array. The hierarchy reduces contention and improves locality, because each level of the reduction touches memory that is close to the workers involved. The reduction can be deterministic if the order of the additions is fixed. For example, the workers can write their buffers to a preallocated array indexed by worker identifier, and a single thread can sum the buffers in a fixed order. That serializes the final reduction, but the serial part is small compared with the per-source work. If the centrality array is large, the reduction can be parallelized over vertices, with each thread summing the same vertex across all worker buffers. The order of the summation can be fixed by iterating over worker identifiers in increasing order.

Memory bandwidth is often the limiting resource. Adding more workers increases the number of concurrent memory streams, but it also increases contention for the memory controller. Beyond a certain point, additional workers do not improve throughput and may even reduce it because they evict each other's cache lines. The optimal number of workers for a memory-bound graph algorithm can be less than the number of hardware threads. Performance counters that measure memory bandwidth, cache misses, and interconnect traffic can help determine the saturation point. If the graph is large and the per-source arrays are also large, the working set may exceed the last-level cache, and every source will stream data from main memory. In that regime, the algorithm is bandwidth-bound, and the best optimization is to reduce the number of bytes touched per source, not to add more parallelism.

## Performance Modeling, Verification, and Operational Practice

A simple roofline model for Brandes' algorithm starts by counting the bytes moved per edge traversal. For an unweighted graph in compressed sparse row format, a breadth-first search reads the edge targets and the offsets, and writes distances and path counts. The dependency accumulation reads predecessor lists and writes dependency values. The arithmetic per edge is small: a few additions, multiplications, and divisions. The ratio of floating-point operations to bytes is low, so the algorithm is memory-bound on most hardware. The model explains why graph reordering, cache blocking, and compressed representations can matter more than instruction-level optimizations. It also explains why source parallelism scales well until memory bandwidth saturates, and why adding more cores after saturation can hurt.

Hardware performance counters can validate the model. Metrics such as last-level cache misses per edge, memory bandwidth utilization, and interconnect traffic can show whether the implementation is close to the roofline or whether it is leaving bandwidth unused. If the miss rate is high and bandwidth is not saturated, the access pattern may be the problem. If bandwidth is saturated, the only way to improve performance is to reduce the bytes moved. That can mean using smaller data types, compressing predecessor lists, or changing the graph layout to improve spatial locality.

Verification is as important as performance. A parallel betweenness implementation should be tested against a sequential reference on small graphs where exhaustive or all-pairs computation is feasible. The comparison should check not only the final centrality values but also intermediate quantities such as shortest-path counts and dependency values for individual sources. Invariants provide additional checks. For a fixed source, the sum of the dependencies over all vertices has a known relationship to the shortest-path structure, and the path counts must satisfy the recurrence that each vertex's count equals the sum of its predecessors' counts. These invariants can be checked at runtime with modest overhead, or in a debug build. They catch errors in tie handling, predecessor construction, and accumulation order that might otherwise produce plausible but incorrect centrality values.

Edge cases deserve explicit tests. Disconnected graphs should produce zero contributions from sources that cannot reach a vertex. Self-loops should not affect shortest paths unless they have zero weight, and zero-weight edges require special care because they can create cycles of equal distance. Parallel edges can multiply path counts and must be handled consistently. Directed graphs should not be symmetrized accidentally. Weighted graphs with floating-point weights should be tested for tie handling when distances are equal within rounding error. These cases are easy to overlook in a parallel implementation because the bugs may appear only for specific source and target combinations.

Operational practice adds another layer. Long-running centrality computations should report progress, because a graph with hundreds of millions of edges can take hours even on a large machine. Checkpointing the per-source results allows a job to resume after a failure, but it also increases I/O traffic. Resource limits such as cgroup memory limits can cause the process to be killed if the per-thread arenas grow too large. Containerized deployments may restrict thread affinity or memory binding, which can change the performance characteristics. Logging the scheduling policy, the number of workers, the graph layout, and the reduction strategy makes it possible to reproduce a run and to diagnose performance regressions. Reproducibility is easier if the source assignment and the reduction order are deterministic, but even a nondeterministic run can be useful for exploratory analysis if the numerical differences are small and well understood.

These concerns do not replace the algorithmic core. They surround it. The shortest-path DAG, the dependency recurrence, and the source-level decomposition remain the foundation. The engineering work is in making that foundation survive contact with real memory hierarchies, real schedulers, and real operational constraints. The next set of trade-offs appears when the graph is too large for one machine, when the computation is distributed across processes, and when the centrality values must be compared across runs that used different levels of parallelism. Those are the points where the systems story becomes as important as the mathematics.

## Autotuning the Source-Parallel Configuration

A source-parallel Brandes implementation exposes more configuration parameters than a first reading suggests. Scheduling policy, source chunk size, worker count, per-thread predecessor arena size, reduction-buffer padding, and flat versus hierarchical reduction all interact with the graph and the machine. Autotuning searches this space automatically rather than fixing values by hand. The search is complicated because the best configuration for one graph may be poor for another.

A practical autotuner begins with a cost model. It does not need to predict absolute runtime; it needs to rank configurations. For source-parallel Brandes, the dominant terms are reachable vertices, traversed edges, predecessor entries, and priority-queue operations for weighted graphs. These can be estimated from a small sample of sources. The sample should include high-degree vertices, low-degree vertices, vertices in the largest component, and vertices in small components. If the graph has known communities, the sample can be stratified by partition. The estimates then choose a chunk size and a scheduling policy. Low variance among source costs favors static chunking. High variance favors dynamic scheduling or work stealing.

Online adaptation is an alternative to a separate tuning phase. The runtime can begin with a conservative static assignment, measure chunk completion times, and switch to a finer-grained policy if imbalance exceeds a threshold. The switch must be cheap. A common approach is a global atomic counter that lets workers claim additional sources after finishing assigned chunks. This is dynamic scheduling with a static prefix. The prefix provides locality early; the dynamic tail handles expensive sources.

Autotuning must respect determinism constraints. If bitwise reproducibility is required, the configuration space is restricted to schedules with a fixed reduction order. That may exclude work stealing or dynamic source assignment, because those change the order in which per-source contributions are added. A deterministic autotuner can still tune chunk size and thread count, but it must fix the mapping from sources to reduction slots. One way is to assign each source a fixed slot in a preallocated contribution array and reduce slots in a fixed order regardless of which worker computed them. The scheduler can then use any policy without affecting the numerical result. The cost is memory: one vector per source, or per chunk if chunks are fixed. For many vertices and sources, that memory can be substantial.

## Error Budgets and Sampling as a Scheduling Decision

Exact betweenness centrality is often the right target for a benchmark or correctness test, but production analytics frequently runs on a sample of sources. Sampling changes the problem from exact computation to estimation. From a scheduling perspective, sampling reduces the number of tasks, not the cost of each task. The per-source Brandes computation is unchanged. The final estimate scales sampled contributions by the inverse sampling probability. The variance depends on the variability of per-source contributions across the population. If contributions are similar, a small sample suffices. If a few sources dominate, the sample must include them or the estimate will be biased and noisy.

An adaptive sampler can allocate the source budget in batches. It runs a first batch, estimates variance, and decides whether to continue or stop. The stopping rule can be based on a confidence interval for each vertex or a global error budget. The engineering challenge is that adaptive stopping introduces data-dependent control flow that is hard to parallelize deterministically. If the decision depends on partial results, different runs may use different sample sizes. That is acceptable for exploration but not for reproducible reporting. A deterministic adaptive sampler can fix batch sizes and the stopping rule in advance, or use a fixed seed and a fixed order of source evaluation. The latter preserves reproducibility but reduces the ability to stop early.

Sampling also interacts with load balancing. A uniform random sample inherits the same heavy-tailed cost distribution as the full set. Stratified sampling can reduce both variance and imbalance. If the graph has a known partition, the sampler can draw sources from each partition in proportion to its size, then schedule them with the same work-stealing policy used for exact computation. The stratification metadata can be computed once and reused across runs. For dynamic graphs, the metadata must be refreshed, but the refresh cost is usually small compared with the centrality computation. The decision between exact and sampled betweenness is therefore not only statistical. It is a scheduling decision, a memory decision, and a reproducibility decision. The exact algorithm remains the reference point, but the production configuration is often a carefully bounded approximation.
