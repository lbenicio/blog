# The Coordination Tax: Flat Combining and the Design of Contention-Aware Shared Data Structures

Most discussions of parallel data structures begin with progress guarantees. A stack is lock-free, a queue is wait-free, a hash table uses fine-grained locks, and the conversation moves quickly to linearizability and memory reclamation. Those properties matter. They are not, however, the only forces that determine whether a shared data structure works well in a real system. A lock-free algorithm can be correct, formally non-blocking, and still lose badly under contention because every operation pays a coordination tax: cache-line transfers, failed compare-and-swap attempts, retry loops, queueing delay, and scheduler interference. The tax is not a constant. It depends on the access pattern, the number of threads, the operation mix, the length of critical sections, the memory topology, and the cost of the operations being coordinated.

This article treats flat combining as a contention-aware design pattern rather than as a universal replacement for lock-free programming. Flat combining is a way to concentrate coordination: instead of letting every thread contend directly on a shared object, threads publish requests into per-thread records, and one elected thread applies a batch of those requests under a single mutual-exclusion lock. The point is not to eliminate the lock. The point is to change what the lock protects and how often it is acquired. The design question becomes: what work should the lock aggregate, and what does that aggregation cost in latency, fairness, and memory traffic?

The argument is not that flat combining is always better. It is that contention is a design variable, and flat combining makes that variable explicit. A lock-free stack may be the right choice when operations are rare and latency-sensitive. A flat-combining design may be the right choice when many threads perform short operations on the same data structure and the dominant cost is coherence traffic rather than algorithmic work. A sharded design may be better when the workload can be partitioned so that threads rarely touch the same cache line. The engineering task is to understand which cost dominates and to choose a coordination strategy that charges the right price.

## The coordination tax is not a constant

A shared data structure's cost has at least three components: algorithmic work, synchronization work, and queueing. Algorithmic work is the work required to perform the operation itself, such as traversing a tree or updating a hash bucket. Synchronization work is the work required to coordinate with other threads, such as acquiring a lock, executing a compare-and-swap, or waiting for a memory ordering. Queueing is the delay caused by other threads getting service first. Under low contention, algorithmic work dominates. Under high contention, synchronization and queueing can dominate even when the algorithmic work is trivial.

Consider a simple counter. An atomic increment is a small amount of algorithmic work. Under low contention, a single atomic instruction may be enough. Under high contention, many threads issue atomic operations to the same cache line. The cache line bounces between cores, each successful update invalidates the copies held by other cores, and failed compare-and-swap loops repeat. The algorithmic work did not change. The synchronization work did. A lock-based counter can have the same problem: threads form a queue on the lock, the lock word becomes a hot cache line, and the operating system may deschedule a thread while it holds the lock. The lock serializes the operation, but it does not necessarily reduce the coherence traffic around the lock word.

This is why progress guarantees alone are an incomplete guide. A lock-free algorithm avoids blocking, but it does not avoid contention. It may spend more work retrying than a lock-based algorithm spends waiting. A lock-based algorithm may serialize operations, but it may also reduce the number of coherence transfers if the critical section is short and the lock is acquired once for a batch of work. The interesting question is not whether an algorithm is lock-free in the abstract. The interesting question is what it charges when many threads want the same data structure at the same time.

Flat combining sits between these poles. It uses a lock, but the lock is held by a combiner that performs many operations per acquisition. It uses per-thread publication records, but those records are not the shared data structure. They are a staging area. The combiner reads the staging area, applies the operations to the shared object, and publishes the results. The lock is coarse in scope, because it protects the shared object and the combiner role, but it can be fine in time, because the combiner holds it only for a batch. The design succeeds when the batch is large enough to amortize the lock and small enough to keep latency and fairness under control.

## A mental model: one active thread, many published requests

In a flat-combining design, each participating thread has a publication record. The record contains enough information to describe an operation: an operation code, arguments, a result slot, a state field, and often a sequence number or generation counter. To perform an operation, a thread writes a descriptor into its record and then attempts to become the combiner. Exactly one thread wins the combiner role, typically by acquiring a lock or by winning a try-lock. The winner scans the publication records, applies the pending operations to the shared data structure, writes results into the records, marks them complete, and releases the lock. Threads that did not become the combiner wait for their records to be marked complete.

The combiner is not necessarily a dedicated server thread. It is whichever thread wins the role for that batch. This matters because it avoids a separate thread and a separate queue, but it also means the combiner's own operation is part of the batch. The combiner can process its own request first, last, or in sequence order. That choice affects fairness and linearization. A combiner that always processes its own request first can starve other threads. A combiner that processes requests in publication sequence order preserves a natural real-time ordering, but it may reduce locality if the operations touch different parts of the data structure.

The lock in a flat-combining design is not protecting the publication records. Each record is owned by one thread and read by the combiner. The lock protects the shared object and the combiner role. The publication records are a concurrent data structure in their own right, but a simple one: one writer per record, one reader at a time, and a completion flag. The hard part is not the lock. The hard part is the protocol between the publishing thread and the combiner: memory ordering, record reuse, completion detection, and the handoff of the combiner role.

A useful mental model is a ticket window. Each thread writes a request on its own slip of paper and places it in a visible slot. One thread becomes the clerk, collects all pending slips, performs the requested transactions against the shared ledger, writes the receipts back into the slots, and then stops being the clerk. The ledger is protected by a single lock, but the clerk does not take the lock once per slip. The clerk takes it once per collection. The cost of the lock is amortized over the batch. The cost of the collection is the scan over the slots. If the batch is large, the scan is cheap per operation. If the batch is small, the scan can dominate.

## Publication protocol in detail

The publication protocol needs to answer four questions. First, how does a thread announce that its record contains a pending operation? Second, how does the combiner know that the record is stable and safe to read? Third, how does the combiner publish a result without racing with the record owner? Fourth, how does the record get reused without confusing a stale completion with a new operation?

A typical protocol uses a state field per record. The owner writes the operation descriptor, then stores a pending state with release semantics. The combiner loads the state with acquire semantics. If the state is pending, the combiner can read the descriptor. The release and acquire pair ensures that the descriptor writes are visible before the state is observed. The combiner applies the operation, writes the result, and stores a completed state with release semantics. The owner waits by loading the state with acquire semantics. When it observes completed, it can read the result. The record cannot be reused until the owner has consumed the result and reset the state. A generation counter or sequence number helps distinguish a completion from a previous operation on the same record.

This protocol is simple in outline and subtle in practice. The owner must not overwrite a record while the combiner might still be reading it. The combiner must not mark a record complete before the result is visible. The owner must not spin forever if the combiner is delayed. The state field must not share a cache line with a field that the owner writes frequently, or the completion flag will cause false sharing. Publication records are often padded to cache-line size for this reason. The padding costs memory, but it prevents the completion flags of different threads from invalidating each other.

The combiner's scan is another design point. A naive scan visits every registered record on every batch. If there are P participating threads and the batch contains B operations, the scan cost is O(P) per batch and O(P/B) per operation. When B is close to P, the scan is cheap per operation. When B is much smaller than P, the scan can dominate. A combiner can maintain a bitmap of active records, a linked list of pending records, or a queue of published requests. Each structure has its own contention and memory-ordering costs. A bitmap is compact but requires atomic updates. A linked list avoids scanning empty records but introduces pointer chasing. A queue can preserve order but becomes another shared data structure that needs its own coordination. The right choice depends on the expected batch size and the number of participants.

## Why batching changes the cost curve

Batching changes the economics of coordination in three ways. First, it amortizes the lock acquisition. A lock acquisition is not just an atomic instruction. It is a cache-line transfer, a possible scheduler interaction, and a serialization point. If the combiner acquires the lock once for a batch of B operations, the lock cost per operation is divided by B. Second, batching improves locality. The combiner applies many operations to the same data structure while the relevant cache lines are already in its cache. A sequence of independent operations on the same hash bucket or the same tree node can be performed with fewer coherence transfers than the same operations performed by different threads. Third, batching reduces failed synchronization attempts. Threads that would otherwise retry a compare-and-swap loop publish a request and wait. The combiner performs the operation once. The wasted work of retries is replaced by the cost of publication and the cost of waiting.

Batching also changes the ordering semantics. A flat-combining design must define a linearization order for the batch. If the combiner processes requests in publication sequence order, the resulting order is consistent with the real-time order of publication, assuming the sequence numbers are assigned correctly. If the combiner groups operations by type or by data locality, it may improve throughput but it may violate the order that clients expect. For a counter, order may not matter. For a map, the order of puts and gets matters. For a queue, the order of enqueues and dequeues matters. The combiner cannot simply reorder operations for convenience unless the data structure's semantics allow it. The batch is a scheduling unit, not a license to ignore linearizability.

The batching trade-off is therefore not just throughput versus latency. It is also throughput versus semantic freedom. A larger batch amortizes more coordination, but it also holds the lock longer, increases the tail latency of non-combiner threads, and gives the combiner more opportunity to make ordering decisions that affect correctness. A smaller batch reduces latency and preserves a tighter real-time order, but it pays more lock acquisitions and more scans. The design needs a bounded batch size, a bounded lock hold time, or both. The bound should be chosen from the workload's latency budget, not from a fixed constant that happens to look good in a microbenchmark.

## The latency trade

Flat combining optimizes throughput under contention at the expense of latency. A thread that does not become the combiner must wait for the combiner to finish the batch. If the batch is short, the wait is short. If the batch contains a long operation, every other thread waits behind it. This is head-of-line blocking at the level of the combiner. A range query, a resize, or a traversal that touches many nodes can turn a fast-path operation into a convoy. A flat-combining design should either keep the critical section short or separate long operations from the combining path. Long operations can be split into chunks, executed outside the combiner with a different protocol, or served by a read-only path that does not require the combiner.

Read-mostly workloads are a particularly important case. If most operations are reads and only a few are writes, serializing all reads through a combiner is wasteful. A read can often be served directly from the shared data structure if the structure supports concurrent reads and the read does not need to be ordered with a write in a way that requires the combiner. A flat-combining design can use a read fast path and reserve the combiner for writes. The read path must still respect the memory ordering and versioning rules of the data structure. If reads can observe a partially applied batch, the design is broken. The combiner must publish a consistent state before readers can observe it, or readers must use a versioning scheme that detects and retries inconsistent reads.

Fairness is the other side of the latency trade. The combiner role is a temporary privilege. If one thread repeatedly wins the role, it can starve other threads even if each individual batch is short. A try-lock election is simple, but it is not fair. A thread that is already running and has its cache line hot may win repeatedly. A design can use a handoff mechanism, a bounded batch size, a round-robin election, or a backoff policy to limit starvation. The goal is not perfect fairness. The goal is bounded waiting for every participant under the workload's assumptions. If the design assumes that threads are scheduled by a preemptive operating system and that a thread can be descheduled while holding the combiner lock, the waiting bound must account for scheduling delay, not just algorithmic delay.

The memory topology also changes the latency trade. On a cache-coherent multicore with a single socket, the combiner's scan of publication records is relatively cheap. On a NUMA system, the records may be spread across nodes. The combiner may read remote cache lines for every batch, and the publishing threads may write to remote records. A hierarchical flat-combining design can combine requests within a node and then combine the node-level batches at a higher level. The hierarchy reduces remote traffic but adds complexity and additional latency. The design should be chosen from the topology, not from an assumption that all cache lines are equally close.

The first design question is therefore not how to eliminate the lock. It is what work the lock should aggregate. A flat-combining design aggregates requests so that the lock is acquired once per batch, the shared object is updated with locality, and the cost of coordination is amortized. That aggregation is only useful if the batch is large enough to pay for the scan, short enough to meet the latency budget, and ordered in a way that preserves the data structure's semantics. The next design question is how to keep the combiner from becoming a bottleneck when the workload changes, when the topology is non-uniform, or when a long operation arrives in the middle of a batch.

## Electing a combiner without creating a new hot spot

The combiner role is a single-writer privilege, so electing it is a consensus problem on one bit. The naive implementation is a test-and-set lock or an atomic compare-and-swap on a combiner flag. That flag becomes a hot cache line: every thread that wants to combine must read it, and every failed election invalidates the copies held by other threads. If the batch is short, the election cost can be larger than the work being combined. The election mechanism therefore has to be judged by the same standard as the data structure: what does it charge under contention?

A try-lock election is attractive because it is simple and non-blocking for losers. A thread attempts to acquire the combiner lock; if it fails, it publishes its request and waits for completion. The winner scans the records. The problem is that the lock word is still a hot spot. Under high contention, many threads attempt the lock at once, and the cache line bounces even though only one thread succeeds. Backoff helps, but backoff must be tuned. Too little backoff and the line bounces; too much and the combiner role sits idle while pending requests wait. A randomized exponential backoff with a small initial delay is often enough, but it is a heuristic, not a guarantee.

A handoff election can remove the hot spot. When the combiner finishes a batch, it selects the next combiner from the set of waiting threads and writes a token into that thread's record. The selected thread observes the token and becomes the next combiner. The lock is acquired once and then passed by explicit handoff, so the lock word is not repeatedly contended. The cost is additional latency: the handoff requires a store and a load, and the selected thread may be descheduled. A handoff also needs a policy for choosing the next combiner. Round-robin over active records is fair but may select a thread that has already left. A queue of waiting combiners preserves order but adds a shared queue. A per-thread combiner ticket avoids a central queue: each thread takes a ticket when it publishes, and the combiner processes tickets in order. The ticket counter is still a shared atomic, but it is updated once per operation rather than once per election attempt.

The election must also handle the case where the combiner is descheduled. If the combiner holds the lock and the operating system preempts it, every other thread waits. A timeout can let another thread steal the combiner role, but stealing is dangerous: the original combiner may wake up and continue applying operations, causing two combiners to mutate the shared object. A safe steal requires a lease or a generation number. The new combiner increments the generation, and the old combiner checks the generation before each operation and aborts if it has been superseded. Aborting mid-batch is complex because some operations may already be applied. The simpler approach is to keep critical sections short enough that preemption is rare, and to accept that a descheduled combiner is a latency event rather than a correctness event. If the workload cannot tolerate that, a dedicated combiner thread pinned to a core may be better, at the cost of a core that is not doing client work.

## Adaptive batching and the shape of the contention curve

A fixed batch size is a fixed point on a curve that moves. The optimal batch size depends on the arrival rate, the cost of the operations, the number of participants, and the latency budget. A batch of one is just a lock. A batch of a thousand may exceed the latency budget and hold the lock long enough to cause a convoy. The combiner should not process a fixed number of records. It should process records until one of three conditions is met: no pending records remain, a time budget expires, or a maximum count is reached. The time budget is the primary control because latency is measured in time, not in operations. The count cap is a backstop against a batch that contains many cheap operations.

The time budget should be derived from the workload. If the service-level objective is a 99th-percentile latency of 100 microseconds, the combiner cannot hold the lock for 5 milliseconds. It must leave room for queueing, publication, and the client's own work. A common approach is to set the combiner's critical-section budget to a fraction of the latency target, such as 10 to 20 percent, and to measure the actual hold time. The combiner can read a timestamp before the batch and check it periodically during the scan. Checking a timestamp is not free, but it is cheaper than applying an unbounded number of operations. The check should be done every few records, not after every record, to avoid turning the timestamp into a hot variable.

Adaptive batching also needs a low-contention mode. Flat combining has overhead: publication, election, scanning, and completion detection. If only one thread is active, those costs are pure waste. A direct fast path can perform the operation without publishing if no combiner is active and no other thread is waiting. The fast path must be ordered with the combiner path. One way is to use a version counter: the fast path attempts the operation under a short lock or a compare-and-swap, and the combiner increments the version when it starts and finishes a batch. If the fast path observes a stable version and can complete its operation atomically, it can bypass publication. If it detects a combiner, it publishes. The fast path must not violate linearizability: an operation that bypasses the combiner must still be ordered with the batch. A common technique is to have the fast path acquire the same lock for a single operation when contention is low, and switch to combining when the lock is observed to be contended. The switch should use hysteresis to avoid oscillation. A thread that fails to acquire the lock a few times can publish instead of retrying. A combiner that finds few pending records can release the lock and let the next operation try the fast path.

The shape of the contention curve is not monotonic in the number of threads. At low thread counts, a direct atomic or a simple lock may be best. At medium counts, a lock with batching can reduce coherence traffic. At high counts, flat combining can outperform both because it replaces many contended atomics with one combiner's local updates. But at very high counts, the scan of publication records grows with the number of participants, and the combiner becomes a serialization point. The throughput curve flattens and then may decline. The design should measure the curve rather than assume that more combining is always better. If the number of participants is large but the operation rate is low, a sharded structure or a read-mostly path may be better. If the operation rate is high and the operations are short, flat combining has the best chance.

## Long operations, read paths, and the limits of aggregation

Batching assumes that operations are short and roughly uniform. A long operation breaks that assumption. A range query that traverses a tree, a resize that rehashes a table, or a bulk delete that touches many nodes cannot be hidden inside a small batch. If the combiner executes it, every other thread waits behind it. If the combiner defers it, the operation's latency grows without bound. The design needs a separate path for long operations.

One approach is to split long operations into chunks. A range query can be decomposed into a sequence of node visits, each of which is short. The combiner can process a chunk, release the lock, and let other operations proceed. The query's continuation is stored in the publication record, and the combiner resumes it in a later batch. This preserves short critical sections but complicates the linearization order: the query is no longer a single atomic operation. If the query must be linearizable, it needs a versioned read or a snapshot. A read-only query can often be served from a snapshot without the combiner. A write-heavy long operation, such as a resize, is harder. It can be performed in the background while short operations use a forwarding layer, or it can acquire an exclusive lock and drain the pending batch first. Draining ensures that the resize is ordered after all previously published operations. The drain itself is a batch, so the resize's latency includes the time to complete the current batch plus the resize time. That is acceptable if resizes are rare and the latency budget can absorb them.

Read-mostly workloads should not pay the combiner tax for every read. If the data structure supports concurrent reads, a read can bypass the combiner entirely. The read path must still see a consistent state. A seqlock-style version counter is a common solution: the combiner increments a version before and after applying a batch, and readers retry if the version changes or is odd. The version counter must be published with release semantics, and readers must load it with acquire semantics. If the data structure uses pointers, the reader may also need memory reclamation support, such as epoch-based reclamation or hazard pointers, to prevent a node from being freed while it is being read. The combiner can retire nodes after a grace period. The read path then becomes the common case, and the combiner is reserved for writes. This is often the best configuration for caches, maps, and indexes where reads dominate.

The boundary between short and long operations is not always clear. An operation that is short on average can have a long tail. A hash table put is short unless it triggers a resize. A tree insert is short unless it rebalances. The design can classify operations by their worst-case cost and route the expensive cases to a slow path. The slow path can use a different lock or a different coordination strategy. The fast path remains simple and predictable. This separation is a form of contention-aware design: it recognizes that not all operations deserve the same coordination mechanism.

## Memory reclamation and the record lifecycle

Publication records are not immortal. Threads start and stop, and records must be reused or freed. The lifecycle of a record is a small concurrent protocol of its own. The owner writes a descriptor, publishes a pending state, and waits for completion. The combiner reads the descriptor, applies the operation, writes the result, and publishes a completed state. The owner consumes the result and resets the record. If the owner exits before the combiner reads the record, the record must be removed from the combiner's scan without racing with an in-progress batch.

A simple solution is to never free records. Each thread that participates gets a record from a global array indexed by a thread ID, and the record is reused when the thread ID is reused. This avoids reclamation but wastes memory and can confuse stale generations. A generation counter in each record distinguishes a new operation from a completion of an old operation. The combiner includes the generation in its completion store, and the owner checks both the state and the generation. If the generation does not match, the owner ignores the completion. The generation is incremented when the record is reused. This is the same ABA problem that appears in lock-free stacks, and it is solved the same way: by making the state a tuple of generation and phase that fits in a single atomic word.

For dynamic thread creation, a global registry of records is needed. The registry can be a lock-protected list or a lock-free list. The combiner scans the registry on each batch. If a thread unregisters, it must ensure that no combiner is currently reading its record. Epoch-based reclamation is a natural fit: the unregistering thread removes its record from the registry, then waits for all combiners that might have observed the record to finish. A simpler approach is to mark the record inactive and leave it in the registry until a quiescent period. The combiner skips inactive records. The memory is reclaimed later by a background thread. This trades memory for simplicity.

False sharing is the other lifecycle concern. The state field, the result slot, and the descriptor fields should be laid out so that a thread's completion flag does not share a cache line with another thread's completion flag. Padding each record to a cache line is the usual answer. The combiner's scan then touches one cache line per record, which is expensive if the number of records is large. A compact bitmap of active records can reduce the scan cost, but the bitmap itself must be updated atomically. The design should measure whether the scan or the false sharing dominates. In many systems, padding wins because the combiner's scan is sequential and prefetchable, while false sharing causes unpredictable invalidations.

## Hierarchical combining and topology-aware publication

On a single socket, the combiner's scan is a local memory operation. On a multi-socket or NUMA system, the publication records may be spread across nodes. A combiner on node 0 that scans records on node 1 pays a remote memory latency for every record. The publishing thread on node 1 that writes its record and then waits for completion also pays remote latency. The coordination tax now includes the interconnect. Flat combining can be extended hierarchically: each node has a local combiner that batches local requests, and a global combiner batches the node-level results. The local combiner scans only local records, so the scan is cheap. The global combiner reads a small summary from each node, such as a list of operations that need global ordering or a partial result. The hierarchy reduces remote traffic at the cost of an extra level of batching and additional latency.

The hierarchy also changes the election. The global combiner role can be passed between nodes using a token. A node holds the global token for a bounded time, combines global operations, and passes the token to the next node. The token transfer is a remote write, but it happens once per global batch rather than once per operation. The local combiners can continue to process local operations while the global token is elsewhere, as long as the operations do not require global ordering. Operations that can be partitioned by key can be combined locally and then merged. Operations that require a total order, such as a global counter or a queue, must go through the global combiner. The design must distinguish these cases; otherwise, the hierarchy adds latency without reducing contention.

Topology-aware publication also means placing records on the same node as their owners. A thread should allocate its record from node-local memory. The combiner should be scheduled on the node that holds the majority of pending records, or the global combiner should rotate. If the operating system migrates a thread, its record may become remote. Some designs pin threads to cores to keep the record local. Pinning is not always possible, but it is a common technique in high-performance systems. The design should not assume that a thread stays on one node. It should measure the remote access rate and adapt the hierarchy depth. A shallow hierarchy is better when remote traffic is low; a deeper hierarchy is better when remote traffic dominates. The right depth is a function of the topology and the workload, not a universal constant.

The remaining design space is not about choosing between flat combining and lock-free programming as ideologies. It is about deciding where the coordination tax is paid, how much of it can be amortized, and which operations are allowed to bypass the combiner. A contention-aware shared data structure exposes those decisions as explicit parameters: batch budgets, election policies, read paths, reclamation epochs, and hierarchy depth. Those parameters are not independent. A longer batch budget changes the election policy; a read fast path changes the reclamation requirements; a deeper hierarchy changes the linearization order. The engineering work is to keep the parameters consistent with the workload and to measure the result under the contention that the system actually experiences, not the contention that the microbenchmark assumes.

## Instrumenting the coordination tax before changing the design

The first temptation in a contention-heavy system is to replace the data structure. That is often the wrong first move. A flat-combining implementation can be slowed by a hot election word, a poorly padded record, or a batch budget that is too large for the tail latency target. Without instrumentation, those costs look like generic slowness and invite further redesign. The better sequence is to measure where the coordination tax is actually paid, then decide whether the fix is a different data structure, a different combiner policy, or a different placement of records.

A useful cost model separates the per-operation time into algorithmic work, publication work, election attempts, batch service, scan overhead, and waiting. The exact decomposition is less important than the discipline of attributing time to a category. A single-thread baseline gives the algorithmic component. A direct-lock version gives the cost of serialization without publication. A flat-combining version with a batch size of one gives the cost of the full protocol without batching. A sharded version gives the cost when coordination is partitioned. Comparing these variants on the same trace prevents the common error of comparing a tuned flat-combining prototype against an untuned lock.

The flat-combining implementation should expose counters that are cheap enough to leave enabled in production-like tests. Combiner occupancy is the fraction of wall time during which some thread holds the combiner role. Batch size is a histogram, not an average, because the tail matters. Batch hold time is the time from acquiring the combiner lock to releasing it. Election attempts and failed attempts show whether the combiner flag is a hot spot. Scan length shows how many records were visited to find the processed operations. Completion wait is the time a publishing thread spends after publishing until it observes completion. Read bypass rate shows whether the read fast path is doing useful work. Remote record touches show whether the topology is fighting the placement policy.

The instrumentation must also record the arrival process. A batch is not a fixed resource; it is the set of requests that happened to be pending when the combiner acquired the role. If the arrival rate is low, a combiner that holds the lock for a long time may still process only a few requests, and the lock cost is poorly amortized. If the arrival rate is high, the same hold time may process a large batch, but the queueing delay behind the combiner may exceed the service time. The relevant relationship is between the arrival rate, the batch service time, and the latency budget. A batch policy that looks excellent at a fixed thread count can behave very differently when the threads alternate between bursts of work and periods of sleep.

A practical measurement plan uses phases. Start with one thread and increase the count until the direct lock's throughput stops improving. Record the point at which failed lock attempts and cache-line transfers rise. Then enable flat combining and vary only the batch budget. Record the point at which the 99th percentile latency crosses the target. Finally, enable the read fast path or the hierarchy and repeat. The result is not a single winner. It is a map of which mechanism pays off under which contention regime. That map is more useful than a benchmark table because it tells the designer when a parameter change is likely to help.

## Scan order, commutation, and the linearization point

A combiner has to choose an order in which to apply the pending operations. The safe default is publication sequence order. The publishing thread obtains a sequence number before or after writing its descriptor, the combiner scans in increasing sequence order, and the data structure's linearization point is the combiner's update for each operation. This gives a real-time order that is easy to explain and easy to test. The cost is that the combiner may visit records in an order that has poor locality. If thread A publishes a map update for bucket 1, thread B publishes one for bucket 200, and thread C publishes one for bucket 1 again, sequence order forces the combiner to touch bucket 1, then bucket 200, then bucket 1. A locality-aware order would group the two bucket 1 operations and reduce cache traffic.

Grouping is safe only when the operations commute or when the grouping preserves the required order for conflicting operations. For a counter, increments commute, so the combiner can aggregate them. For a map with independent keys, updates to different keys commute, but updates to the same key do not. The combiner can group by hash bucket, but it must preserve sequence order within each bucket. A bucket-local queue of descriptors achieves that, but the queue is additional shared state. A simpler implementation copies descriptors into a combiner-local array, sorts by bucket, and applies them. The sort costs time, but the time is paid once per batch and can be cheaper than repeated cache misses when the batch is large. For small batches, sequence order is usually better because the sort dominates.

Commutation is not only about keys. Insert and erase can commute with lookups in some designs, but not with operations that depend on the presence of a key. A conditional update, such as compare-and-set on a map value, conflicts with any other update to the same key. A range query conflicts with structural changes that move the range. The combiner needs a conflict classification that is conservative enough to be correct. Classifying by key is straightforward for a map. Classifying by node is harder for a tree because rotations change which nodes belong to which subtree. A tree combiner can process structural operations in sequence order and batch only the lookup-like operations that do not modify structure. This is a deliberate restriction, not a failure. It keeps the fast path fast and sends the hard cases to a slower path.

The linearization point also has to be defined for reads that bypass the combiner. A read that observes a version counter can linearize at the point where it validates the version. A read that traverses a tree while the combiner is rotating nodes must either retry on a version change or use a read-copy-update discipline. The combiner must publish a new version only after all mutations in the batch are visible. If the version is incremented before the mutations, a reader can observe a new version with old data. If it is incremented after, a reader can observe old data with an old version and incorrectly accept it. The usual seqlock pattern increments before and after, marks the interval as unstable, and makes readers retry if they see an odd version or a version change. The combiner must use release semantics for the final version store and readers must use acquire semantics for their loads. A flat-combining design that gets this wrong can pass a single-threaded test and fail under a realistic scheduler.

## Designing operations for a combiner

The combiner is a serialization point, so every operation that runs inside it should be short, non-blocking, and free of arbitrary callbacks. This is a stronger requirement than it sounds. A map update that allocates a node on insertion is not combiner-friendly if allocation can take a lock, touch a global heap, or trigger a page fault. The owner thread can allocate the node before publishing the operation. The combiner then only links the preallocated node into the table. If the operation fails because the key already exists, the owner frees the node after completion. The combiner never allocates. The same pattern applies to queues, trees, and indexes: prepare in the owner, commit in the combiner.

Deletion has a similar shape. The combiner unlinks a node, but it should not free the node immediately. Other readers may still hold a reference if the read path bypasses the combiner. The combiner can append the unlinked node to a per-combiner retire list, and a later phase can reclaim it after a grace period. If the data structure does not have a read fast path and all readers go through the combiner, reclamation can be immediate after the batch, but only if no other thread can observe the old pointer. That condition is easy to violate with a stale local copy or a compiler optimization. A conservative retire list is cheaper than a rare use-after-free.

Operations that call user code are especially dangerous. A callback may block, acquire another lock, or publish another operation on the same data structure. If the callback runs inside the combiner while the combiner lock is held, it can deadlock or create a recursive combining problem. The design should either forbid callbacks inside the combiner or defer them until after the combiner lock is released. Deferred callbacks preserve the short critical section, but they change the completion semantics: the operation is not fully complete until the callback runs. The publication record can carry a post-combine action that the owner executes after observing completion. That keeps user code out of the combiner and makes the callback's thread responsible for its own latency.

Operation size also matters. A bulk insert of a thousand keys is not a single short operation just because it is one API call. The owner can split it into individual descriptors, publish them as a batch, and let the combiner process them in chunks. If the API must be atomic, the split changes the semantics. The design can provide both: a non-atomic bulk path that combines well and an atomic bulk path that drains the combiner and takes an exclusive lock. The atomic path is slower, but it is explicit. The danger is pretending that a bulk operation is short because its per-key cost is small. The combiner's latency is governed by the total work in the batch, not by the average.

## Hybrid fast paths and mode transitions

Flat combining should not be the only path in the data structure. Under low contention it is all overhead. A hybrid design starts in direct mode, where a thread performs an operation by acquiring the combiner lock for a single operation or by using a short atomic fast path. When the thread observes contention, it switches to combining mode. The switch must be safe, not just fast. If one thread is in direct mode while another is publishing, the direct-mode operation can bypass the pending batch and violate the order. The usual solution is to make the mode a property observed under the combiner lock. A direct-mode thread acquires the lock, checks that there are no pending records, and performs its operation. If it finds pending records, it drains them or releases the lock and publishes. A switch to combining mode happens only at a batch boundary, when the combiner already owns the role.

The transition policy needs hysteresis. A single failed lock acquisition is not evidence of sustained contention; it may be a scheduling artifact. A short queue may be a burst. The design can track a moving average of failed attempts or pending records and switch only after a threshold is exceeded for several batches. It can switch back when the pending count stays below a threshold for a longer period. Hysteresis prevents the data structure from oscillating between modes and paying both sets of overheads. The thresholds should be expressed in terms of the latency budget, not in terms of arbitrary counts. If the direct-mode tail latency is within budget, there is no reason to combine. If the combining scan is visible in the tail, the batch budget is the problem, not the mode.

A read-only fast path is often more valuable than a write fast path. Reads can use a version counter and retry on a concurrent batch. The read path does not need the combiner lock, but it must participate in reclamation. If the data structure uses epoch-based reclamation, a reader enters an epoch, reads, and exits. The combiner retires nodes into the current epoch and reclaims only after all readers from older epochs have left. If the data structure uses hazard pointers, the reader publishes the pointer it is about to dereference. Either scheme adds per-read overhead, but the overhead is read-only and can be made cheap in the common case. The combiner must know which scheme is in use because it controls retirement. Mixing schemes is a source of subtle bugs.

Mode transitions also interact with reclamation. If the design switches from direct mode to combining mode, readers that started in direct mode may still hold references. The combiner cannot reclaim nodes until those readers are accounted for. The safest approach is to drain the existing mode before enabling a new one. The drain is a quiescent period, not a lock acquisition. A thread that is about to enter the data structure records the current mode; a thread that changes the mode waits for all recorded entries to finish. This is more expensive than a simple flag, but it is bounded and predictable. The cost is paid only at transitions, which should be rare.

## Workload-specific calibration

A counter is the simplest case and the easiest to get wrong. If the counter only needs a final total and never returns an intermediate value, the combiner can sum all increments in a batch and add the sum once. That reduces the shared counter update to one per batch. If the counter returns the value before or after the increment, order matters. The combiner must process the increments in sequence order and return the appropriate value for each. Aggregating changes the linearization points. A design can provide two APIs: a commutative add that returns no value, and an ordered fetch-and-add that returns a value. The commutative path combines better; the ordered path is correct but slower. Making the distinction explicit prevents a fast implementation from silently breaking a caller that depends on the return value.

A map usually benefits from a read fast path and a write combiner. The map's buckets are the natural unit of locality. The combiner can group writes by bucket while preserving per-bucket sequence order. If the map uses open addressing, different keys may probe the same bucket, so the conflict unit is the probe sequence, not the key. A conservative combiner can process all writes in sequence order and rely on the fact that a single thread touching the table has good cache behavior. If the map uses separate chaining, the bucket pointer is a natural commit point. Insertions allocate nodes in the owner, and the combiner links them. Deletions unlink and retire. Resizes are long operations and should drain the batch or run as a background phase with forwarding. Range queries should not run in the combiner because their cost is proportional to the range size, not the operation count.

A queue is the hardest case for batching because its semantics are inherently ordered. Enqueues and dequeues must be linearized in a single order. A combiner can process a batch of enqueues and dequeues in sequence order, which reduces lock acquisitions but does not remove the serialization. The benefit comes from locality and from avoiding failed atomic operations on the head and tail. The risk is head-of-line blocking: a dequeue that waits behind a long batch of enqueues has latency proportional to the batch. A bounded batch budget is essential. A queue can also separate the enqueue and dequeue paths if the underlying buffer supports independent head and tail updates, but that is a different data structure, not flat combining. The design should not claim combining benefits for a queue if the queue's semantics force every operation through the same serial order.

An allocator is a useful stress case because its operations are frequent and its internal state is complex. A flat-combining allocator can let each thread allocate from a local cache and publish only cache refills and frees to the combiner. The combiner batches frees into the global free list and refills local caches in chunks. The owner prepares the free descriptors; the combiner links them. The design must handle the case where a local cache is empty and the combiner is busy. A thread can publish a refill request and wait, or it can fall back to a slower path that acquires the global lock directly. The fallback must be ordered with the combiner's batch. If the fallback bypasses pending frees, the allocator can return a block that is about to be freed, which is a correctness bug disguised as a performance optimization.

## Testing the protocol and its failure modes

The publication protocol needs tests that are more aggressive than throughput benchmarks. A linearizability checker can validate small histories against a sequential model, but it must include the combiner's batch boundaries and the read fast path. A history that passes with the combiner disabled may fail when the combiner reorders commuting operations. The checker should treat the data structure's documented linearization points as the specification, not the implementation's internal order. If the implementation claims that a read linearizes at version validation, the test must allow that. If it claims that a write linearizes when the combiner applies it, the test must not accept a write that becomes visible earlier through a fast path.

Record reuse is a common source of rare failures. A test can force a thread to publish, get preempted before observing completion, and then publish again after the combiner finishes. The generation counter must prevent the second publication from being confused with the first. A test can wrap the generation counter to its maximum value and continue, which exposes signedness and comparison bugs. A test can unregister a thread while a combiner is scanning, which exposes reclamation races. A test can deschedule the combiner in the middle of a batch, which exposes assumptions about lock hold time. These tests do not need to be probabilistic. A deterministic harness can insert delays at protocol boundaries and run many schedules.

Memory ordering deserves its own tests. A test that runs only on a strongly ordered processor can hide missing acquire or release operations. The protocol should be written with explicit atomics and documented orderings, and it should be tested with a model checker or a litmus-style harness on weakly ordered hardware if available. The record state, the result slot, and the descriptor fields are the critical set. The owner must not publish the state before the descriptor is visible. The combiner must not publish completion before the result is visible. The owner must not reuse the record before the completion is consumed. Each of these rules is a release-acquire edge or a stronger ordering. A comment that says the variable is volatile is not a substitute for a defined ordering.

Failure injection should cover allocation failure, thread exit, and combiner timeout. If the owner allocates a node and then cannot publish because the data structure is shutting down, the node must be freed. If the combiner is descheduled beyond a lease, the steal path must either abort the old combiner safely or wait for it. If the combiner encounters an operation it cannot apply, it must publish an error result rather than leaving the owner waiting forever. A timeout on the owner side is not a correctness mechanism; it is a diagnostic. The protocol should guarantee completion under the stated assumptions, and the timeout should tell the operator that an assumption has been violated.

## Bounded budgets and feedback control

The batch budget is the most important tuning knob in a flat-combining design, and it should be a control loop rather than a constant. The combiner can measure the time spent in the current batch and stop when the budget is exhausted. The budget itself can be adjusted from the observed tail latency. If the 99th percentile completion wait is above target, the budget shrinks. If the combiner is idle with pending requests, the budget grows. The adjustment should be slow and bounded. A fast controller reacts to noise and causes the batch size to oscillate. A slow controller adapts to workload phases but may be late when the workload changes abruptly.

The controller must not create a new shared hot spot. If every publishing thread reads and writes a global budget variable, the budget becomes another contended cache line. A better design keeps the budget in the combiner's local state and publishes it only at batch boundaries. Publishing threads do not need to know the exact budget; they only need to know that the combiner is active and that their requests will be served. The combiner can adjust its own budget after each batch and store it in a per-combiner field. If multiple combiners exist in a hierarchical design, each level can have its own budget. The global budget is the sum of the local budgets plus the handoff cost.

A bounded budget also bounds the damage from a long operation. If the combiner checks the budget between operations, a single operation that exceeds the budget can still blow the latency target. The design needs a per-operation cost estimate and a policy for operations that are known to be expensive. Those operations should be routed away from the combiner, split into chunks, or executed under a separate lock that does not block the short-operation path. The budget is a tool for controlling many small operations, not a substitute for classifying operations by cost. A combiner that checks the clock after every operation but still applies a resize inside the batch has not solved the latency problem.

The final constraint is that the controller must be testable. A workload generator can vary the arrival rate, the operation mix, and the thread count, and the test can assert that the tail latency stays within a bound after a settling period. The test should include abrupt changes, because a controller that is stable under a slow ramp may oscillate under a step change. It should also include a single-thread phase, because a combiner that never sees contention should not pay the full protocol cost. These tests are not microbenchmarks; they are checks that the coordination policy behaves as designed when the contention curve moves.
