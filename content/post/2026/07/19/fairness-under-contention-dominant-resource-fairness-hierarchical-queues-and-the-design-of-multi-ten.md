# Fairness Under Contention: Dominant Resource Fairness, Hierarchical Queues, and the Design of Multi-Tenant Cluster Schedulers

A cluster scheduler is a policy engine wearing a database's clothes. It presents itself as an API — submit a job, receive a placement — but everything interesting happens in the arbitration step, where demand for CPU, memory, GPUs, and I/O exceeds what the cluster can supply, and something has to decide who gets less.

Most engineering discussion of schedulers concentrates on mechanics: how to find a node that fits, how to keep the scheduler's own decision latency in the low milliseconds, how to avoid a thundering herd when a large job is admitted, how to make preemption safe. Those matter enormously. But the mechanics sit downstream of a question that is usually left implicit: what does it mean for an allocation to be *fair*, and why would you choose one definition of fairness over another?

This article is about that question, and about the specific answer that became the default in multi-tenant clusters: Dominant Resource Fairness, or DRF. DRF is the allocation policy underneath Apache Mesos, and variants of it — or explicit arguments against it — appear in YARN's capacity and fair schedulers, in Kubernetes' resource model, and in the internal schedulers of large compute fleets. Understanding it requires understanding max-min fairness first, then understanding precisely why max-min fairness in a single dimension does not generalize to a vector of resources.

## The Cluster as a Resource Vector

A job submitted to a cluster is not a request for a machine. It is a request for a bundle of resources in a fixed ratio: some number of cores, some number of bytes of memory, some number of GPUs, some amount of scratch space, and some number of network flows. The ratio is a property of the workload, not of the cluster.

The ratios differ wildly across workloads. A stateless HTTP service running on a modern runtime wants roughly one core per few hundred megabytes of resident heap. A distributed training job wants a handful of host cores and tens of gigabytes of device memory per worker. A sharded key-value store wants many cores and a modest working set. A log compaction or batch analytics job wants very few cores and an enormous amount of disk. A video transcoder wants cores and almost nothing else.

This ratio is the crux of the scheduling problem. Any scheduler that reduces a job to a single scalar — a slot, a share, a priority number, a normalized cost — has already made a normalization decision on the job's behalf, and that decision is only correct if the job mix matches the assumption baked into the normalization.

Consider the historical Hadoop slot model. A cluster was described as having *N* map slots and *M* reduce slots, and a job was scheduled by consuming slots. The slot was a proxy for a fixed fraction of a node's memory. It worked because the workloads were uniform: a map task consumed a predictable amount of memory and ran for a predictable duration. The moment you ran a job with a different memory profile — or a job that was CPU-bound but memory-light — the slot abstraction either wasted memory or overcommitted it. YARN's move to per-resource containers was, in large part, an admission that the slot was the wrong unit of account.

It is tempting to read that history as a solved problem: containers are per-resource, so we are done. But per-resource containers do not answer the fairness question. They tell you whether a *placement* is feasible. They say nothing about whether the resulting distribution across tenants is defensible. That is a separate problem, and it is the one this article addresses.

## A Taxonomy of Scheduler Responsibilities

Before narrowing to fairness, it is worth separating the jobs a cluster scheduler actually performs, because conflating them leads to confused designs.

**Admission control** decides whether a job enters the system at all. This is where quotas, priority classes, and backpressure live. A scheduler that admits every job and then fails to place it has simply moved the queue from the client to itself.

**Placement** decides which node runs which task. This is a constrained assignment problem: bin packing with anti-affinity, topology spread, taints and tolerations, device locality, and increasingly NUMA topology constraints. Placement is where most of the scheduler's CPU time goes, and it is where the interesting approximation algorithms live, because the general problem is NP-hard.

**Preemption** decides what to evict when a higher-priority or under-served tenant needs capacity that is currently occupied. Preemption is what makes a scheduler work-conserving without making it unfair; without it, a long-running low-priority job can hold a resource indefinitely.

**Fairness** decides the target distribution that placement and preemption are trying to approximate. Fairness is a *policy*, not a mechanism. It produces a vector of target allocations, and the rest of the scheduler tries to track it.

The distinction between placement and fairness matters because they have different failure modes. A placement bug produces a task stuck in Pending. A fairness bug produces a cluster that is 100% utilized and 100% useless to the tenant who cannot get capacity, which is a much harder problem to diagnose and a much more expensive one to have.

## What "Fair" Can Mean

Before defining an algorithm, it is worth being explicit about which properties a fairness policy might be expected to satisfy. The literature on fair division gives a useful checklist, and the checklist is not vacuous. No policy satisfies all of these simultaneously in all settings, and the engineering work consists largely of choosing which to sacrifice.

- **Sharing incentive.** A user should be no worse off participating in the shared cluster than running their own equal partition of it. If a user's allocation under the shared policy is less than what they would get from an exclusive slice, they have an incentive to defect — and in an organization with a procurement process, defecting means buying a separate cluster.
- **Strategy-proofness.** A user should not be able to improve their allocation by lying about their resource demands. This is a mechanism-design property, and it is the one most often violated by naive policies that reward over-reporting.
- **Envy-freeness.** No user should prefer another user's allocation to their own. This is a strong property, and in the multi-resource setting it is achievable only in a restricted sense.
- **Pareto efficiency.** The policy should not leave resources idle when there is demand that could be satisfied. If a task can be placed without displacing another task, the scheduler should place it.
- **Population monotonicity.** If a user leaves the cluster, the remaining users' allocations should not decrease. This sounds obvious and is surprisingly easy to violate with a policy that renormalizes on every scheduling cycle.

To these, practitioners add properties the theory literature tends to ignore:

**Scheduler throughput.** Can the policy be evaluated in a few milliseconds per decision, at thousands of decisions per second? A policy that requires solving a linear program per scheduling cycle is correct and unusable.

**Stability.** Does the allocation oscillate when tasks are short and the cluster is nearly full? A policy that reallocates aggressively on every task completion can produce a cluster that spends more time reshuffling than running work.

**Explainability.** Can an operator tell a user why they got what they got? This is not a soft concern. A scheduler whose decisions cannot be explained generates support tickets; support tickets generate policy exceptions; policy exceptions are how a clean algorithm becomes a pile of special cases, each of which interacts badly with the others.

## Max-Min Fairness and Progressive Filling

The single-resource version of fairness is well understood. An allocation is **max-min fair** if it is impossible to increase the allocation of any user without decreasing the allocation of a user who has less. Equivalently: the smallest allocation is as large as it can be, then the second smallest is as large as it can be given the first, and so on inductively.

The canonical algorithm is **progressive filling**. Every user starts at zero. Increase every user's allocation at the same rate until some user's demand is fully satisfied or some resource is exhausted. Freeze that user or that resource, and continue increasing the remaining users at the same rate. Repeat until nothing more can be allocated.

The result is a stair-step allocation profile. Users with small demands are fully satisfied; users with large demands are capped at a common level. The cap is the *water level*, and the process is exactly water filling: the resource is the water, the demands are containers of different heights, and the water level is the maximum allocation that leaves no container overflowing and no water unused.

Weighted max-min fairness generalizes this by assigning each user a weight $w_i$ and requiring that the *weighted* allocations $a_i / w_i$ be equalized among the users who are not saturated. A user with weight 2 receives twice the allocation of a user with weight 1, provided both are saturated. Weights are how you express *team A paid for twice the capacity of team B* without abandoning the fairness machinery entirely.

Two properties of max-min fairness explain why it survives in practice:

1. It is **work-conserving**. If there is idle capacity and a user who wants it, the allocation is not max-min fair. This is what distinguishes it from a static partition, and it is why a fair-share scheduler can beat a hard-partitioned cluster on utilization while still bounding the damage one tenant can do to another.

2. It is **strategy-proof in the single-resource case** under the standard model. Over-reporting demand does not increase your allocation, because the allocation is determined by the water level rather than by the size of your request. Under-reporting does not help either, because you receive whatever you actually consume up to the water level.

The second property is the one that breaks in the multi-resource case, and it is the reason DRF exists as a separate algorithm rather than as a straightforward generalization of progressive filling.

## Why Single-Resource Fairness Does Not Generalize

Here is a concrete counterexample. Two users, Alice and Bob. The cluster has 9 CPUs and 18 GB of RAM. Alice's tasks each require 1 CPU and 1 GB. Bob's tasks each require 1 CPU and 4 GB. Both users have effectively unlimited demand.

Suppose we normalize by CPU. Alice's task costs $1/9$ of the CPU and Bob's costs $1/9$ as well, so CPU-normalized accounting says the two tasks are identical. But Bob's task consumes $4/18 = 2/9$ of the cluster's memory, more than twice Alice's. Run max-min fairness on the CPU dimension alone and both users are entitled to 4.5 CPUs. Alice runs 4 tasks (4 CPUs, 4 GB). Bob runs 4 tasks (4 CPUs, 16 GB). Total consumption is 8 CPUs and 20 GB — the memory is overcommitted and the allocation is infeasible.

Now normalize by memory instead. Alice is entitled to 9 GB and Bob to 9 GB. Alice runs 9 tasks, consuming 9 CPUs and 9 GB. Bob runs 2 tasks, consuming 2 CPUs and 8 GB. Total consumption is 11 CPUs and 17 GB — the CPU is overcommitted and this allocation is infeasible too.

The lesson is not that one normalization is better than the other. The lesson is that *any* fixed normalization is wrong, because the binding constraint differs by user. Alice's demand vector is CPU-dense relative to the cluster; Bob's is memory-dense. A single scalar currency forces you to price both users in the same unit, and whichever unit you pick, one of them is being systematically mispriced.

The correct answer is not a fixed normalization but a *per-user* normalization derived from that user's own demand vector. That is the central idea behind Dominant Resource Fairness, and it is what the next section develops: rather than asking how much of the cluster a user is consuming, ask how much of the user's *own* bottleneck resource they are consuming, and equalize that quantity across users.

## Dominant Resource Fairness: Equalizing Bottlenecks

DRF begins with an allocation vector for each user. Let the cluster capacity be C = (C_1, ..., C_m), where each C_r is the total amount of resource r available for allocation. Let user i's current allocation be a_i = (a_i1, ..., a_im). The share of resource r held by user i is s_i,r = a_i,r / C_r. The dominant resource of user i is the resource r for which s_i,r is largest, and the dominant share is D_i = max_r s_i,r. DRF is max-min fairness applied not to CPUs, not to memory, and not to a weighted sum of the two, but to the vector of dominant shares.

The definition sounds almost too simple. It is worth pausing on what it does not do. It does not sum a user's normalized usage across resources. It does not normalize by task count. It does not compare the absolute quantities of different resources. The max is doing the work: it identifies the resource that is currently the user's bottleneck and measures the user's consumption against the cluster's supply of that resource. A user who is using 40 percent of the cluster's CPUs and 10 percent of its memory has a dominant share of 0.4. A user using 10 percent of CPUs and 40 percent of memory also has a dominant share of 0.4. DRF regards them as equally served, even though their raw usage vectors are different, because each is equally close to exhausting the resource that constrains them.

The dominant resource is not necessarily the cluster's scarcest resource globally. It is the resource that is scarcest from the user's current allocation's point of view. That distinction is important. A cluster may have plenty of free memory and almost no free CPU, but a memory-heavy user may still be constrained by memory if that user's task shape demands four gigabytes for every core. The scheduler should not compare that user's memory pressure to another user's CPU pressure directly. It should compare how far each user has traveled toward its own limit.

Return to the 9-CPU, 18-GB cluster and the two task shapes used earlier: Alice's tasks require 1 CPU and 1 GB, Bob's require 1 CPU and 4 GB. For Alice, one task consumes 1/9 of the CPU capacity and 1/18 of the memory capacity, so her dominant resource is CPU and her dominant share per task is 1/9. For Bob, one task consumes 1/9 of the CPU capacity and 4/18, or 2/9, of the memory capacity, so his dominant resource is memory and his dominant share per task is 2/9. If Alice runs x tasks and Bob runs y tasks, their dominant shares are x/9 and 2y/9. Equalizing those shares gives x = 2y. The feasibility constraints are x + y <= 9 CPUs and x + 4y <= 18 GB. Substituting x = 2y gives 3y <= 9 and 6y <= 18, so both constraints allow y = 3 and x = 6. The resulting allocation is 6 CPUs and 6 GB for Alice, 3 CPUs and 12 GB for Bob. Both users have a dominant share of 2/3, and the cluster is fully allocated.

This is the concrete answer to the normalization problem from the previous section. Alice gets twice as many tasks as Bob, but she does not get twice the fairness entitlement. Each of Bob's tasks consumes twice Alice's share of his own bottleneck resource, so Bob needs fewer tasks to reach the same dominant share. An operator can explain the outcome without appealing to an arbitrary exchange rate between CPU and memory: each user is receiving the same fraction of the resource that limits that user's workload. That explanation is not merely cosmetic. It is the difference between a policy that survives contact with users and one that generates a stream of exceptions.

DRF can be implemented as progressive filling in dominant-share space. Maintain each user's current allocation and dominant share. At each scheduling decision, choose the user with the smallest dominant share among those with pending demand whose next task fits in the remaining capacity. Allocate that task, update the user's dominant share, and repeat. If the smallest user cannot fit, consider the next smallest, and so on. If no pending task fits anywhere, the allocation is maximal for the current capacity. In the continuous version, this is water filling with a single water level D: every active user's allocation is increased until its dominant share reaches D, subject to resource constraints. In the discrete version, the same idea is applied task by task, with granularity deciding how close the final allocation gets to the ideal water level.

A priority queue keyed by D_i makes the accounting cheap. Each decision requires the user's dominant resource index and a max over m resources, not a linear program. In practice, m is small — CPU, memory, GPU, maybe disk and network — so the max is a handful of divisions. The expensive part of a cluster scheduler remains placement and fit checking, not the fairness calculation. This is a large part of DRF's appeal: it replaces a contested policy question with a compact piece of arithmetic that can run inside the scheduling loop.

The policy has several properties that are worth naming precisely, because they are often repeated more strongly than the underlying model supports. When there is only one resource, D_i reduces to that resource's share, and DRF reduces to ordinary max-min fairness. In the idealized model with divisible resources and a fixed demand vector per user, DRF is Pareto efficient: it does not leave capacity idle when a pending demand could use it without displacing a user with a lower dominant share. It provides a sharing incentive: no user is worse off than under an equal partition of the cluster, measured by the number of its own tasks that can be satisfied. It is envy-free in the model's utility sense: no user prefers another user's allocation bundle. And it is strategy-proof in the same idealized setting: over-reporting demand does not improve a user's dominant share, because the allocation is set by the water level rather than by the size of the request.

Those guarantees are conditional, and the conditions matter in production. With indivisible tasks, the greedy algorithm can leave a user slightly below the exact water level because the next task does not fit. With multiple task shapes per user, the dominant resource can change as the workload mix changes, so the scheduler needs either a stable estimate of each user's demand vector or a policy for re-evaluating the dominant resource as jobs arrive. With admission order, preemption, and queue-level borrowing, the discrete outcome can differ from the continuous ideal. DRF is still useful in those settings because it provides a principled target, not because it makes every edge case disappear. The engineering work is in deciding how far the system is allowed to deviate from that target before it takes corrective action.

Weighted DRF extends the same machinery by assigning each user a weight w_i and equalizing D_i / w_i instead of D_i. A team with weight 2 can reach twice the dominant share of a team with weight 1 before the two are considered equally served. This is how DRF expresses relative entitlement without abandoning the bottleneck-based definition of fairness. The weights are policy; the dominant share is the accounting. Keeping those two concepts separate is important, because it lets an operator change entitlement without changing the meaning of fairness for a single user.

## Hierarchical Queues: The Organization Chart as Policy

Flat DRF assumes that the unit of fairness is a user. Real clusters rarely have a flat user list. They have an organization: a platform group, several product teams, services within each team, and jobs within each service. The scheduler is expected to enforce fairness between teams at the top, between services within a team, and between jobs within a service. A single flat weight per job cannot express that structure without collapsing it into a number, and the number will be wrong whenever the organization changes.

Hierarchical queues make the tree explicit. Each internal node represents a group, and each leaf represents a queue of work. Every node can carry a weight, a minimum guarantee, and a maximum cap. The weight governs competition among siblings. The minimum guarantee protects a queue that has been idle but is entitled to capacity when it becomes active again. The maximum cap prevents a queue from borrowing the entire cluster simply because its siblings are idle. The tree is a policy language: it says that two teams are equal at the top level even if one team has a hundred engineers and the other has two, and it says that within a team, services divide their team's entitlement according to a second set of weights.

A common implementation applies max-min fairness recursively. At a parent node with an allocation A_p, divide A_p among the active children in proportion to their weights. For child c, the nominal target is A_p * w_c / sum over active siblings of w_j. Then recurse into each child. If every branch is active and no caps bind, a leaf's share is the product of the normalized weights along its path from the root. If a branch is inactive, work-conserving hierarchical scheduling lets its siblings borrow the unused share, subject to their maximum caps. That borrowing is where the policy becomes operationally interesting. Without borrowing, hierarchical queues are static partitions with extra steps. With borrowing but without reclaim, a temporarily idle subtree can lose its entitlement permanently to a busy sibling. With borrowing and reclaim, the scheduler needs preemption or reservation to restore the guaranteed share when the idle subtree returns.

DRF fits into this structure by changing what is equalized at each level. Instead of tracking a single scalar fair share per queue, the scheduler tracks an allocation vector and a dominant share for each node. A node's allocation is the sum of the allocations of its descendants. Its dominant share is the maximum over resources of that aggregate allocation divided by cluster capacity. At each level, DRF is applied across siblings using those aggregate dominant shares. When a leaf receives a task, the scheduler updates the dominant shares of every ancestor on the path to the root. This is still cheap: the tree is shallow, and the update touches one path per scheduling decision. The state is larger than flat DRF, but it is still small compared with the state needed for placement.

There is an important subtlety here. A parent can look balanced in aggregate while one of its children starves. Suppose a subtree contains one CPU-heavy child and one memory-heavy child. The subtree's aggregate usage may be evenly split between CPU and memory, so its dominant share looks healthy relative to its siblings. Inside the subtree, however, the CPU-heavy child may be consuming most of the available CPU while the memory-heavy child cannot get the memory it needs. If the scheduler enforces fairness only at the root, that local starvation is invisible. If it enforces fairness at every level, the leaf's effective share is constrained by every ancestor, and local fairness can conflict with global work-conservation. The design must choose which level dominates, how often to rebalance, and what happens when a local guarantee cannot be met without violating a global cap.

This is not a detail that can be left to the implementation. Root-level fairness maximizes global utilization and prevents any single tenant from taking the whole cluster. Leaf-level fairness protects users inside a team and prevents a large service from monopolizing its team's share. Strict leaf-level fairness can leave capacity idle when one team's leaves are idle and another team's leaves are active but capped by an ancestor. Work-conserving hierarchical schedulers resolve this by allowing a queue to exceed its nominal share when its siblings are inactive, while marking the excess as reclaimable. When the inactive sibling returns, the scheduler either preempts the borrower or waits for the borrower's tasks to finish, depending on the queue's preemption policy. That choice determines whether the hierarchy behaves like a fair scheduler or like a partition with a borrowing grace period.

A hierarchical implementation that computes a single scalar fair share per node has quietly reintroduced the single-resource normalization problem at every level. If the scalar is a weighted sum of resource shares, the weights encode an implicit exchange rate between CPU and memory, and that exchange rate will be wrong for some workload. If the scalar is one resource, the scheduler ignores the others. The vector-aware version stores a dominant resource indicator and a dominant share per node, and applies DRF to siblings. That is more state, but it keeps the fairness definition consistent from the root to the leaf. The alternative is a hierarchy that is fair in the accounting system and unfair in the resources that actually constrain the workload.

Hierarchical queues also introduce a stability problem that flat DRF can avoid. When the active set changes on every task completion, nominal targets jump. A queue that was inactive for a few seconds can return to find its share already borrowed, and the scheduler must decide whether to reclaim immediately or wait for a rebalancing window. Aggressive reclaim produces oscillation: tasks are preempted, queues change state, and the scheduler spends more time reshuffling than running work. Passive reclaim produces slow drift: a queue's guarantee erodes because it was idle during the wrong interval. Production systems usually split the difference with hysteresis — recomputing shares on a fixed cadence, requiring an imbalance to exceed a threshold before preempting, or using queue-level demand estimates rather than instantaneous usage. Those mechanisms are not part of the fairness definition, but they determine whether the definition is observable in practice.

The result is that hierarchical DRF is best understood as a family of policies rather than a single algorithm. The tree defines entitlement. The weights define sibling competition. The minimum guarantees define protection. The maximum caps define the blast radius of borrowing. DRF defines how a vector of resources is converted into a comparable share at each level. Preemption defines whether the target can be restored after it has been violated. Each of those choices is visible to users, and each of them can turn a clean theoretical property into an operational tradeoff.

## Preemption as the Enforcement Arm of Fairness

A fairness policy that cannot reclaim capacity is a suggestion. Hierarchical weights and dominant shares describe a target allocation, but targets drift as workloads arrive, finish, and change shape. Preemption is the mechanism that pulls the actual allocation back toward the target when drift becomes material. Without it, a tenant that borrows idle capacity during a quiet period can hold that capacity indefinitely, and the guarantee owed to the returning tenant becomes unenforceable.

The first design question is what counts as a violation. A scheduler can treat any positive deviation from the nominal share as a violation, or it can tolerate a band. Treating every deviation as a violation produces constant eviction; treating none as a violation produces permanent erosion of guarantees. Production systems typically define a reclaim threshold: preemption is considered only when a tenant's dominant share exceeds its weighted target by some margin, or when an under-served tenant has been waiting longer than a configured interval. The threshold converts fairness from a per-decision invariant into a control loop with hysteresis.

The second question is victim selection. Preempting the first task that fits is cheap and usually wrong. A good victim is a task from an over-served tenant, preferably one whose removal brings that tenant closest to its target without overshooting, and preferably one that is cheap to restart. Long-running tasks with expensive checkpoints are poor victims; short tasks that will finish soon are also poor victims because preemption saves little capacity for long. The scheduler may prefer tasks that are already at a natural boundary, or tasks whose queue has explicitly marked them as preemptible. Priority classes interact here: a high-priority task in an over-served tenant should not be immune to fairness preemption merely because of its priority, but preempting it may violate a different contract. The policy must state which dimension dominates when priority and fairness conflict.

Preemption also has a cost model that is easy to ignore. Killing a process loses in-memory state, invalidates caches, and may trigger a retry storm. Suspending a task may free CPU but not memory, which does not help if memory is the dominant resource. Draining a node is gentler but slower and can strand capacity. A scheduler that does not account for these costs will choose victims that are cheap to identify and expensive to lose. Rate limiting preemption is therefore as important as choosing victims: a cluster that preempts hundreds of tasks in a burst can spend more time restarting than running, and the resulting instability makes the fairness target harder to track, not easier.

Finally, preemption must be bounded by guarantees. A tenant should not be preempted below its minimum entitlement, even if another tenant is under-served, unless the cluster is overcommitted and the policy explicitly allows it. This is the difference between reclaiming borrowed capacity and seizing protected capacity. The former enforces fairness; the latter destroys the sharing incentive. Operators need a clear answer to the question: if every tenant simultaneously demanded its full guarantee, would the cluster be able to satisfy them? If not, the guarantees are overcommitted, and preemption policy is really an admission-control problem wearing a scheduling costume.

## Reservations, Quotas, and the Difference Between Entitlement and Usage

Quotas and reservations are often used interchangeably in conversation and are distinct in mechanism. A quota is an upper bound: tenant A may not consume more than 40 percent of the cluster. A reservation is a lower bound: tenant B is guaranteed 20 percent even when the cluster is busy. DRF is neither. It is an allocation rule that decides how elastic capacity is divided among active tenants. A complete policy usually combines all three: reservations for protected capacity, DRF for the elastic remainder, and quotas to cap the blast radius of a tenant that would otherwise absorb every idle resource.

The combination is not additive. If reservations are expressed as fixed quantities per resource, they can fragment the cluster. A reservation of 100 CPUs and 200 GB is not useful if the scheduler cannot place a task that needs 8 CPUs and 64 GB because the remaining free memory is split across nodes. Reservations should be expressed in the same resource vector as the workloads, and the admission controller should verify that the reserved capacity is placeable, not merely accounted for. Otherwise the guarantee exists on paper and not in the cluster.

Quotas have the opposite failure mode. A quota that is enforced only at admission can be exceeded at runtime if the scheduler overcommits resources. A quota enforced only at runtime can reject a job that would have fit within the tenant's fair share because another tenant is temporarily borrowing capacity. The clean model separates admission from allocation: the quota bounds the tenant's maximum concurrent allocation, and DRF determines the tenant's share of the currently available capacity. When the two disagree, the tighter constraint wins, and the scheduler should record which one bound the decision.

Entitlement and usage are also not the same thing. A tenant may be entitled to 25 percent of the cluster and use 5 percent because its workload is bursty. Another tenant may be entitled to 25 percent and use 40 percent by borrowing the first tenant's idle share. That borrowing is desirable if it is reclaimable. It becomes a problem when the first tenant returns and the scheduler cannot distinguish between capacity that was borrowed and capacity that was permanently granted. The fix is to track reclaimable allocations separately from guaranteed allocations, and to expose that distinction in the scheduler's API and in its metrics. A tenant should be able to answer: how much of my current allocation is protected, and how much can be reclaimed if a sibling becomes active?

Weighted DRF gives a natural way to express entitlement without hard reservations. A weight is a relative claim on elastic capacity, not a promise of a fixed quantity. If the cluster is fully utilized, weights determine how the shortfall is distributed. If the cluster is idle, weights do not prevent a single tenant from using everything. That is the correct behavior for a work-conserving system, and it is why weights are a better fit than static partitions for clusters with variable demand. The cost is that weights alone cannot protect a tenant from starvation during a sustained overload. For that, the system needs either a reservation or a minimum guarantee that is enforced by preemption.

## Resource Shapes, Gang Scheduling, and the Limits of Greedy DRF

Greedy DRF makes a strong assumption: tasks are independent, and the scheduler can admit them one at a time. Many distributed workloads violate that assumption. A distributed training job needs all of its workers running simultaneously or none at all. A sharded service needs a quorum. A batch pipeline may have a barrier between stages. These are gang-scheduled workloads, and a scheduler that applies DRF task by task can admit half a gang, consume capacity, and then discover that the remaining half does not fit. The result is a deadlock or a long idle period while the first half holds resources that no one else can use.

The standard remedy is all-or-nothing admission for gangs, combined with a reservation of the gang's full resource vector before any task is placed. That reservation changes the fairness calculation. A gang that holds a reservation but has not started is consuming entitlement without consuming resources. If the scheduler counts the reservation against the tenant's dominant share, the tenant may be throttled even though the cluster is idle. If it does not count the reservation, two gangs can each reserve the same capacity and neither can start. The practical solution is a two-phase commit: reserve capacity in the scheduler's accounting, place the tasks atomically, and release the reservation if placement fails. The fairness target should be computed on committed allocations, with pending reservations treated as a separate demand signal.

Multiple task shapes within a single tenant create a related problem. A tenant may run a CPU-heavy batch job and a memory-heavy serving job at the same time. The tenant's dominant resource depends on the mix, and the mix changes as jobs arrive and finish. If the scheduler recomputes the dominant resource on every decision, the tenant's effective share can oscillate: the scheduler sees CPU as dominant, throttles the tenant, then sees memory as dominant, and throttles it again. The tenant is penalized twice for having a diverse workload. A more stable approach is to compute the dominant resource over a window, or to use the maximum over the tenant's active jobs' dominant resources rather than the dominant resource of the aggregate. The latter is conservative but predictable, and predictability is a feature in a multi-tenant system.

Gang scheduling also exposes the difference between user-level and job-level fairness. DRF is usually defined over users, but a user with one large gang and a user with many small jobs are not symmetric. The large gang may be unable to fit even when the user's fair share is nominally sufficient, because the free capacity is fragmented. The small jobs may be able to fill the gaps and consume more than the large gang's tenant. If the policy equalizes dominant shares at the user level, the large-gang user can be starved by fragmentation while the small-job user is over-served. The scheduler needs a fragmentation-aware metric: not just how much capacity a tenant is entitled to, but whether that entitlement is placeable given the current node shapes and the tenant's task shapes.

This is where DRF stops being a complete answer and becomes one component of a larger policy. DRF decides how much. Placement decides whether the how much can be realized. Admission decides whether the request should enter the system at all. Preemption decides what happens when the realization drifts. A cluster scheduler that implements DRF but ignores fragmentation will be fair in accounting and unfair in practice.

## GPU and Accelerator Scheduling: The New Dominant Resource

Accelerators break the assumption that resources are fungible. Two GPUs of different generations may have different memory capacities, different compute throughputs, and different interconnect topologies. A task that needs 40 GB of device memory cannot run on a 16 GB GPU, even if the scheduler has ten of them free. A task that needs NVLink-connected GPUs cannot be spread across PCIe islands without a large performance penalty. Treating all GPUs as a single resource pool is the same mistake as treating all CPUs as interchangeable across architectures: it produces feasible-looking allocations that are useless to the workload.

The correct model is a vector of device attributes, not a scalar count. Each accelerator has a memory capacity, a compute capability, a topology group, and possibly a mode such as MIG partitioning. A task's request is a predicate over those attributes, not just a number. DRF can still be applied, but the resource dimension for GPUs should be the attribute that binds the task: device memory for a large model, GPU count for a data-parallel job, interconnect bandwidth for a communication-heavy job. The dominant resource may be GPU memory even when the cluster has free GPU count, because the free GPUs are too small.

Heterogeneity also complicates fairness across tenants. If tenant A can only use the newest GPUs and tenant B can use any GPU, a policy that equalizes GPU count gives tenant A a smaller effective share, because the resources it can use are a subset of the pool. A fairer approach is to define the resource pool per tenant as the set of devices that satisfy the tenant's constraints, and to apply DRF within that pool. The scheduler then needs to decide how to allocate shared devices between tenants with overlapping but non-identical eligibility. This is a matching problem as much as a fairness problem, and it can produce allocations that are envy-free within each eligibility class but not across classes.

Fractional GPUs and MIG make the accounting more granular but also more fragile. A MIG slice is not a smaller version of the whole GPU; it has its own memory bandwidth and cache characteristics. A task that fits in a slice may perform poorly if its working set exceeds the slice's cache. The scheduler can expose slices as separate resource types, but then the fairness calculation must handle many more dimensions. The alternative is to schedule at the whole-GPU level and let the runtime partition, which simplifies fairness and wastes capacity. Most production systems choose a hybrid: whole GPUs for training, slices for inference, and a policy that keeps the two classes separate so that a slice cannot consume the dominant share of a training-oriented tenant.

## Elasticity, Overcommitment, and Fairness Under Uncertainty

Every fairness policy assumes something about demand. DRF assumes that each user has a demand vector and that the scheduler can observe it. In practice, demand is uncertain, requests are inflated, and usage is a noisy signal. A user who requests 100 CPUs and uses 10 is not the same as a user who requests 10 and uses 10, but a scheduler that counts requests treats them as different, and a scheduler that counts usage treats them as the same. Neither is correct for all purposes.

Request-based accounting protects the cluster from overcommitment. It ensures that the sum of admitted requests does not exceed capacity, which makes placement predictable and preemption rare. Its weakness is that it rewards over-requesting. A tenant that inflates its requests can reserve capacity it does not use, and the scheduler will treat that capacity as unavailable to others. Usage-based accounting rewards actual consumption, which improves utilization, but it can allow a tenant to consume more than its fair share by running many small tasks that individually look harmless. It also makes the fairness target unstable, because usage fluctuates with workload phase.

A common compromise is two-level accounting. Allocation decisions use requests, so the scheduler knows what it has committed. Fairness metrics use a blend of requests and observed usage, or they use requests for admission and usage for reclaim. For example, a tenant's dominant share can be computed from its allocated resources, while its reclaimable share is computed from the difference between allocation and recent usage. A tenant that holds 100 CPUs and uses 10 has a large reclaimable buffer; the scheduler can lend that buffer to another tenant and preempt the borrower when the holder's usage rises. This is how overcommitment and fairness can coexist: the guarantee is on allocation, the efficiency comes from lending idle usage, and the reclaim path is triggered by a change in usage.

The uncertainty also affects the choice of time scale. A scheduler that recomputes fairness every few milliseconds will chase noise. A scheduler that recomputes every few minutes will respond slowly to a tenant that has been starved for an hour. The right cadence depends on the workload: interactive services need fast reaction to latency-sensitive demand, while batch jobs can tolerate slower rebalancing. A single global cadence is a compromise that is wrong for at least one class of workload. Production systems often use multiple scheduling loops: a fast loop for placement and preemption of high-priority tasks, and a slower loop for rebalancing fair shares across queues. The two loops must share state carefully, or the fast loop will make decisions that the slow loop immediately undoes.

## Measuring Fairness: Metrics and Operational Signals

Fairness is invisible unless it is measured. A cluster can be 95 percent utilized and still have a tenant that has received zero capacity for an hour. Utilization says nothing about distribution. The scheduler needs per-tenant metrics that are tied to the fairness policy, not just generic resource counters.

The most direct metric is the dominant share over time, broken down by tenant and by queue. Plotting dominant share alongside the tenant's weight or target shows whether the policy is tracking its intent. A tenant whose dominant share is consistently below its target while it has pending demand is being starved. A tenant whose dominant share is consistently above its target is either borrowing idle capacity or receiving more than its entitlement. The distinction matters: borrowing is healthy, over-entitlement is a policy bug.

Wait time is the second essential signal. A tenant can have a reasonable average allocation and still have unacceptable tail latency if its tasks wait behind a backlog. The scheduler should track the distribution of queue wait time per tenant, not just the mean. A p99 wait time that grows while utilization is stable usually indicates that a large tenant is monopolizing the placement path, or that fragmentation is preventing the waiting tenant's tasks from fitting. The fairness policy may be correct in accounting while the placement policy is starving the tenant in practice.

Preemption rate is the third signal. A high preemption rate for a tenant means its allocation is unstable. That may be the intended consequence of borrowing reclaimable capacity, but it should be visible and attributable. If a tenant is preempted repeatedly because another tenant's reservations keep activating, the cluster is overcommitted at the guarantee level, and the fix is admission control or more capacity, not a different victim-selection heuristic. If a tenant is preempted because its own usage is bursty and the scheduler is reacting to noise, the fix is hysteresis or a longer averaging window.

Finally, the scheduler should expose allocation error: the difference between the target share and the achieved share, integrated over time. This turns fairness into a control-system metric. A small, bounded error is normal in a discrete system with indivisible tasks. A growing error means the scheduler is drifting. An oscillating error means the control loop is unstable. Operators can set thresholds on allocation error and alert when a tenant's error exceeds its historical band. That is more actionable than a raw utilization graph, because it directly answers the question the fairness policy was designed to answer: who is getting less, and is that the intended outcome?

## Priority Is Not Fairness, and Fairness Is Not Priority

Priority and fairness are often conflated because both answer the question of who goes first. But priority is ordinal and fairness is cardinal. Priority says class A outranks class B; fairness says tenant A and tenant B are entitled to shares that sum to capacity. A scheduler that uses priority to encode entitlement forces operators to express that team A gets twice team B as a rank ordering, which loses the ratio. A scheduler that uses fairness to encode priority forces operators to express that this job must run before that one as a weight, which loses the hard ordering. The two mechanisms should be separate.

A workable composition is priority within fair share. The fairness layer computes each tenant's target dominant share. The priority layer orders tasks within a tenant's queue. When the cluster is contended, the scheduler first admits tasks from tenants below their target, using priority to choose among that tenant's tasks. A tenant above target is served only when no under-target tenant has a runnable task, or when its task is in a priority class that the policy explicitly exempts from fairness. The exemption is the dangerous part. If high-priority tasks are always admitted regardless of tenant share, a tenant can escape fairness by marking everything high priority. The policy needs a bounded exemption: high-priority tasks may borrow from the tenant's future share, but the borrowing is recorded and reclaimed.

Priority also changes victim selection for preemption. A fairness-driven preemption should prefer tasks from over-served tenants, but a priority-driven preemption should prefer low-priority tasks. When both conditions point at different tasks, the scheduler needs a lexicographic rule. One common rule is: never preempt a task below its tenant's guaranteed share for fairness reasons; never preempt a task above its priority class for priority reasons; otherwise choose the task whose removal reduces the largest weighted violation. This is a policy decision, not an algorithmic one. It should be documented and exposed, because users will encounter it at the worst possible moment.

Priority inversion is the operational symptom of a bad composition. A low-priority task holds a resource that a high-priority task needs, and the scheduler cannot preempt the holder because the holder is under its tenant's fair share. The high-priority task waits. If this happens repeatedly, the priority class has become decorative. The fix is either to make the high-priority class preemptible at a cost, or to reserve capacity for it, or to admit fewer high-priority tasks. Each fix moves the problem somewhere else. The scheduler's job is to make the tradeoff explicit rather than to hide it behind a single numeric priority.

## The Queueing View: Latency, Head-of-Line Blocking, and Fair Queueing

DRF answers how much capacity a tenant should receive over time. It does not answer how long a task waits before it receives that capacity. Those are different questions, and a cluster can be perfectly fair in allocation while being unacceptable in latency. A tenant with a correct dominant share can still see p99 wait times in the hours if its tasks sit behind a backlog of larger tasks from the same tenant or from a tenant that is borrowing capacity.

The queueing discipline determines service order. A single global FIFO queue produces head-of-line blocking: a large task at the head prevents smaller tasks behind it from using free capacity, even when those smaller tasks would fit. Per-tenant queues remove cross-tenant head-of-line blocking, but they introduce the question of how to multiplex the queues onto the cluster. Weighted fair queueing and deficit round robin are the classical answers. Each queue receives a weight, and the scheduler services queues in proportion to their weights while accounting for the size of the task being dispatched. The result is fairness in service order, not just in aggregate allocation.

DRF and fair queueing are complementary but not identical. DRF operates on resource vectors and dominant shares. Fair queueing usually operates on a scalar cost, such as task count or estimated duration. A scheduler can use DRF to set each tenant's target dominant share and then use a fair queueing discipline to order tasks within that target. The combination gives both vector-aware allocation and bounded latency. The scalar cost in the queueing discipline should be chosen to reflect the resource that is actually scarce. If CPU is the bottleneck, cost in CPU-seconds; if memory is the bottleneck, cost in memory-byte-seconds; if the bottleneck changes, the cost function should change with it or the queueing discipline will misprice tasks.

Backpressure is the other half of the queueing problem. A tenant that submits more work than its share can absorb will accumulate a queue. If the scheduler accepts unlimited queue depth, the queue becomes a latency buffer that hides overload and makes wait-time metrics meaningless. Admission control should bound the queue per tenant, and the bound should be expressed in the same resource vector as the fairness policy. A tenant with a large queue but a small share is not being served fairly; it is being stored fairly. The scheduler should reject or delay submissions once the tenant's queued demand exceeds a threshold, and it should tell the tenant why.

Aging is the simplest remedy for starvation within a queue. A task that has waited longer than a threshold gains priority until it is served. Aging is crude, but it is predictable and easy to explain. It also interacts with fairness: if an over-served tenant's tasks age into high priority, they can defeat the fairness layer. The aging threshold should therefore be applied within the tenant's fair share, not across tenants. The tenant's queue orders its own tasks; the fairness layer orders the tenants.

## Isolation Is More Than Allocation: Interference and the Extended Resource Vector

Allocation fairness assumes that a unit of resource delivered to a tenant is a unit of resource the tenant can use. That assumption fails on shared hardware. Two tasks on the same node compete for last-level cache, memory bandwidth, network queues, and disk I/O. The scheduler can give each tenant a fair share of cores and memory and still produce a cluster where one tenant's workload is slowed by another tenant's cache thrashing. The allocation is fair; the performance is not.

The resource vector should therefore include interference dimensions when the scheduler can observe them. Cache occupancy, memory bandwidth, and network bandwidth are not always allocatable, but they are measurable. A scheduler can use them as placement constraints: avoid co-locating two memory-bandwidth-sensitive tasks on the same node, or spread a tenant's tasks across NUMA domains. It can also use them as fairness dimensions: if a tenant's dominant resource is memory bandwidth, its dominant share should be computed against the cluster's memory bandwidth, not just its core count. This is an extension of DRF, not a replacement. The dominant resource is whatever constrains the tenant's workload, and on modern hardware that is increasingly a shared microarchitectural resource rather than a core.

Isolation mechanisms sit below the scheduler. cgroups limit CPU and memory; CPU pinning and NUMA binding reduce migration and remote-memory traffic; network QoS limits bandwidth; device plugins partition accelerators. These mechanisms are necessary but not sufficient. They bound the damage a tenant can do, but they do not make the scheduler's allocation fair. A tenant can be capped at 10 percent of CPU and still be starved if its tasks are placed on nodes where the other 90 percent is consumed by a cache-polluting neighbor. The scheduler and the isolation layer must agree on the resource model. If the scheduler thinks in cores and the isolation layer thinks in shares, the two will diverge under contention.

The practical consequence is that fairness metrics should include performance signals, not just allocation counters. A tenant's dominant share can be correct while its tasks' stall time is high. The scheduler should track per-tenant slowdown relative to a solo run, or a proxy such as CPU steal time, cache miss rate, or network retransmits. When slowdown diverges from allocation, the problem is isolation or placement, not fairness. Treating it as a fairness problem leads to over-allocating the tenant; treating it as a placement problem leads to better co-location decisions. The distinction is important because the remedies are different.

## Operationalizing Fairness: State, Reconciliation, and Policy Change

A fairness policy is only as good as the state that implements it. The scheduler needs to know each tenant's allocation, each queue's target, each node's capacity, and each task's resource vector. That state changes on every admission, placement, completion, and preemption. If the state is maintained in multiple places, the fairness calculation will eventually use a stale view. The standard remedy is a single authoritative allocation store, updated transactionally with each scheduling decision, and reconciled periodically against the actual state of the cluster.

The fairness calculation should be deterministic and cheap. Given the same allocation state and the same pending demand, it should produce the same target. This makes the scheduler testable and makes incidents reproducible. Randomization can be useful for breaking ties, but it should be seeded and recorded. A scheduler that makes different fairness decisions on identical inputs is impossible to debug.

Policy change is a fairness event. Changing a weight, adding a reservation, or moving a queue in the hierarchy changes the target for every tenant that shares an ancestor. The scheduler should version the policy, record which version was active for each decision, and provide a dry-run mode that computes the new targets without enforcing them. Operators can then compare the old and new distributions before committing. Without this, a weight change intended to help one team can silently starve another, and the resulting incident will be attributed to the scheduler rather than to the policy change.

Reconciliation is the safety net. The scheduler's allocation counters will drift from reality because of crashes, missed events, or bugs in placement. A periodic reconciliation should compare the counters with the actual tasks running on nodes and correct the difference. The correction should be logged and attributed. A drift that recurs is a bug in the event path; a drift that appears once is a crash. Both need to be visible. Fairness under contention is a control loop, and a control loop without reconciliation is an open-loop system that will eventually diverge.

Testing fairness policies requires more than unit tests for the dominant-share function. It requires simulation with adversarial demand: tenants that over-report, tenants that under-report, tenants that arrive in bursts, tenants that leave and return, and tenants whose resource shapes change over time. The invariants are simple to state: no tenant below its guarantee while capacity is idle; no tenant above its cap while a sibling is starved; the sum of allocations never exceeds capacity; and a tenant's allocation does not decrease when another tenant leaves. Property-based tests can generate random demand vectors and check those invariants. Deterministic replay can reproduce production incidents from recorded scheduling events. Both are more valuable than a benchmark that measures average utilization on a friendly workload.
