## The problem with 'perfectly parallel'

There is a class of performance problem that resists the usual diagnostic reflexes. The kernel is embarrassingly parallel: no locks, no shared mutable state, no reduction, no cross-thread communication except at the very beginning and the very end. The work is uniform, the trip counts are known at compile time, and every thread executes the same instruction stream over different data. And yet, past a certain core count, throughput flattens and then sags.

The standard explanations are contention, load imbalance, and serial sections. When those are ruled out — the profile shows no lock acquisition, per-thread timings agree to within a few percent, and the serial fraction is negligible — the remaining explanation is usually data movement. The threads are not waiting on each other. They are waiting on the memory hierarchy, and adding threads makes that hierarchy *worse*, because aggregate demand for cache lines, DRAM banks, and interconnect bandwidth grows with the thread count while supply stays fixed.

This is the domain of communication-avoiding algorithms. The sharp definition is this: a communication-avoiding algorithm has been restructured so that it moves asymptotically fewer words, or sends asymptotically fewer messages, between levels of the memory hierarchy than the standard formulation — at the cost of extra computation, extra storage, or both. *Avoiding* is chosen deliberately, and it is not the same as *hiding*.

The distinction matters because hiding is what most engineers reach for first, and hiding has hard limits. This article is about the other approach: changing the algorithm so that the data movement is not there to hide in the first place. We will work through the machine models that make the problem precise, the lower bounds that say how much movement is unavoidable, the specific algorithms that attain those bounds — 2.5D matrix multiplication, tournament-pivoting LU, Tall-Skinny QR, s-step Krylov methods — and the practical costs that determine whether any of it is worth doing on your hardware.

## Two currencies: words and messages

Any model of data movement has to distinguish two costs, because they scale differently and are optimized by different means.

The first is **bandwidth**: the number of words transferred. On a single node this is the cost of pulling cache lines from L2, L3, or DRAM. On a cluster it is the cost of pushing bytes through the network interface. Bandwidth costs are, to first order, proportional to the volume of data moved, and they are amortized by moving more data at once.

The second is **latency**: the number of messages or transactions. A cache miss is a message. An MPI point-to-point send is a message. A DRAM row activation is arguably a message. Latency costs are proportional to the *count* of discrete operations, and they are amortized by making each one carry more payload.

These two are related by a simple identity: if you move W words in m messages, the average message carries W/m words. An algorithm that minimizes W may use many small messages; an algorithm that minimizes m may move far more words than necessary. The product of per-message latency and per-link bandwidth — the latency-bandwidth product — tells you roughly how many bytes must be in flight to saturate a link. On a modern high-speed interconnect, per-message latency is on the order of a microsecond and per-link bandwidth is on the order of tens of gigabytes per second, so saturating one link requires on the order of tens of kilobytes in flight. That number determines whether a latency-optimized algorithm can actually reach the bandwidth it is theoretically entitled to.

The practical consequence is that *reduce communication* is an under-specified goal. You must say which currency you are spending. A broadcast of a matrix block to all processors costs one message per receiver but many words. A ring-based pipeline of the same block costs many messages but the same total words. Which is better depends entirely on the ratio of your latency to your bandwidth, and that ratio differs by orders of magnitude between a shared-memory node and a wide-area cluster.

There is a third currency that is increasingly the binding one: **energy**. Moving a double from DRAM to a register and performing one double-precision multiply-add on it are not remotely comparable in joules; the movement is commonly estimated to cost two to three orders of magnitude more. This is why the same algorithmic restructuring that reduces words moved also tends to reduce power, and why communication-avoiding designs have outlived the specific machines they were invented for.

## Hiding is not avoiding

The conventional response to data movement is to overlap it with computation. Double-buffering in a stencil kernel, software prefetching in a pointer-chasing traversal, asynchronous MPI with computation in the middle, `cp.async` on a GPU — all of these are attempts to make the latency of a transfer disappear behind useful work.

Hiding works, and it is often the right answer. But it has three structural limits.

First, hiding requires concurrency. To hide a latency L behind work that takes time T per unit, you need roughly L/T independent units in flight. If your kernel has limited parallelism — a triangular solve, a sparse matrix with a long dependency chain, a sequential recurrence — there may not be enough independent work to fill the pipe. Amdahl's law has a memory-hierarchy analogue: the fraction of time that cannot be overlapped bounds the achievable speedup.

Second, hiding requires resources. Prefetch buffers, registers, and shared memory are all finite. A kernel that hides latency by keeping many loads in flight consumes registers and occupancy to do it, and on a GPU that trade can cost more than it saves. On a CPU, out-of-order execution already does a great deal of hiding for free, up to the size of the reorder buffer and the load queue; beyond that, the programmer must supply the concurrency explicitly.

Third, and most importantly, hiding does not reduce the total volume of traffic. If an algorithm must move a certain number of words through a level of the hierarchy, overlapping those transfers with computation makes them cheaper in wall-clock time but does nothing about the fact that they consume bandwidth, energy, and interconnect capacity that other parts of the system also need. On a machine where bandwidth is genuinely scarce — a multi-socket node with a saturated memory controller, a cluster with an oversubscribed fat-tree — hiding just moves the bottleneck around.

Avoiding is the complementary strategy. If the algorithm's inherent data movement is reduced, then both the latency and the bandwidth costs drop, and the overlap problem becomes easier because there is less to overlap. The two techniques compose: a communication-avoiding algorithm that is also well-overlapped is strictly better than either alone.

## The red-blue pebble game

To reason about how much movement is *unavoidable*, we need a model. The standard one for sequential memory hierarchies is the red-blue pebble game, introduced by Hong and Kung in 1981.

The model is a two-level memory: a fast memory of size M (registers, cache, shared memory, or local memory) and a slow memory of unbounded size (DRAM, or the aggregate memory of other processors). Computation can only occur on values in fast memory. The game is played on a data dependency graph — for matrix multiplication, the graph whose vertices are the input and output elements and whose edges represent the multiply-add operations. Two kinds of pebbles are placed on vertices: red pebbles represent values resident in fast memory, blue pebbles represent values resident in slow memory. At most M red pebbles may be on the board at once. A red pebble may be placed on a vertex whose predecessors all carry red pebbles (that is, a computation); a red pebble may be removed and replaced by a blue pebble (a write-back); a blue pebble may be replaced by a red pebble (a read). The cost of the game is the number of red-blue pebble exchanges — the number of words moved between the two levels.

For matrix multiplication of n×n matrices, the result is that any algorithm requires Ω(n³/√M) word transfers between slow and fast memory, provided M is at most on the order of n². This is the Hong-Kung bound. It is tight: the standard blocked algorithm with blocks of size √M × √M attains it.

The bound is remarkable because it is independent of the order in which the computation is performed. You cannot out-clever the dependency structure. Any schedule of the n³ multiply-adds that respects the data dependencies and has only M words of fast memory must move at least that many words.

The result was extended in two directions that matter for practice. Irony, Toledo, and Tiskin showed in 2004 that the same style of argument applies to distributed-memory matrix multiplication, giving a per-processor bound in terms of the local memory size. Ballard, Demmel, Holtz, and Schwartz generalized it in 2011 to a broad class of *direct* linear algebra algorithms — those whose dependency structure can be described by a set of nested loops with restricted access patterns — and gave bounds for matrix multiplication, LU, QR, Cholesky, and others in terms of the number of scalar multiplications rather than n alone. The generalization is what makes the theory actionable: it tells you, for a specific factorization on a specific machine, how much communication you are stuck with.

The general shape of the result is worth stating plainly. For a broad class of dense linear algebra problems, the minimum number of words that must cross a boundary between memory of size M and memory of unbounded size is on the order of

```
(number of scalar multiplications) / sqrt(M)
```

and the minimum number of messages is on the order of

```
(number of scalar multiplications) / M^(3/2)
```

The second expression follows from the first plus the observation that a message can carry at most M words, though for distributed algorithms the tight bound is derived more carefully from the geometry of the dependency graph. Both expressions have the same structure: communication scales with the work, divided by a power of the fast memory size. Doubling the size of your cache reduces the mandatory traffic by a factor of √2 for bandwidth and roughly 2.83 for latency.

That structure is the central fact of the field, and it explains why the practical questions are always about M: how big is the fastest level you can actually use, and how much of your working set fits in it.

## What the bound does and does not say

Three caveats before we start designing algorithms around it.

The bound is **asymptotic**. It says nothing about constants, and the constants matter enormously at the sizes most people run. A bound that only bites at n = 100,000 is irrelevant if your matrices are 2,000 × 2,000 and fit comfortably in a large last-level cache. The whole communication-avoiding program is a bet that your problem is large enough for the asymptotics to dominate; when it is not, a well-tuned conventional algorithm usually wins.

The bound is about a **two-level model**. Real machines have four to six levels, each with its own M, and the binding constraint is often not the one you expect. A kernel can be perfectly blocked for L2 and still thrash the TLB. It can be perfectly blocked for DRAM and still lose to false sharing at L1. Choosing M correctly — that is, choosing which boundary you are actually optimizing — is a prerequisite for using any of this.

The bound assumes the algorithm is *direct* in the sense of having a dependency graph that can be analyzed this way. Iterative methods, sparse factorizations, and algorithms with data-dependent control flow fall outside the cleanest form of the theorem, and for those the bounds are either weaker or require a different argument. This is precisely where a great deal of the interesting research lives, and it is why the s-step Krylov methods we will examine later needed their own line of analysis rather than inheriting the dense-case result.

With those caveats in place, the design space is clear. For each of the canonical dense linear algebra problems — matrix multiplication, LU, QR, Cholesky, and the eigenvalue and Krylov problems built on top of them — there is a known lower bound, a family of algorithms that attains it up to constants, and a set of costs (extra flops, extra memory, numerical stability, load imbalance) that you pay to get there. The rest of this article walks that design space one problem at a time, starting with the one where the intuition is clearest: matrix multiplication, where the choice is not whether to block but *how many copies of the data to keep alive at once*.

## 2.5D matrix multiplication: buying bandwidth with memory

The standard 2D blocked algorithm for C = A B on P processors partitions the matrices into a √P × √P grid. Each processor (i, j) computes a block C_{i,j} of size n/√P × n/√P, and to do so it needs the entire row i of A and the entire column j of B. In the usual broadcast formulation, each processor initially holds one block of A and one block of B, and then the blocks are broadcast along rows and columns. The communication cost per processor is O(n²/√P) words and O(√P) messages. The total words moved across the machine is O(n² √P), and the total number of messages is O(P √P) in the worst case, though pipelining can reduce this.

The 2.5D algorithm, introduced by Solomonik and Demmel, asks a simple question: if you have extra memory, can you use it to reduce communication? The answer is yes, and the mechanism is replication. Instead of storing one copy of A and B, we store c copies of each, where c is an integer between 1 and P. We then arrange the P processors in a three-dimensional grid of size P1 × P2 × c, where P1 = P2 = √(P/c). Each processor is indexed by (i, j, k) with i, j ∈ [0, √(P/c)) and k ∈ [0, c).

The matrices A and B are replicated c times along the k dimension. For a fixed k, the processors (i, j, k) form a 2D grid of size √(P/c) × √(P/c). This grid is responsible for computing a block of C of size n/√(P/c) × n/√(P/c) — that is, a block that is √c times larger in each dimension than in the 2D algorithm. The inner dimension of the multiplication is split into c parts, one for each layer. Processor (i, j, k) computes a partial sum over the k-th part of the inner dimension, and the c partial sums are then reduced across the k dimension to form the final block.

The communication cost per processor drops to O(n² / √(c P)) words and O(√(P/c)) messages. The total words moved across the machine is O(n² √(P/c)), a factor of √c better than the 2D algorithm. The computation per processor remains O(n³/P), so the extra work is only in the reduction, which is O(n² c / P) words per processor — still smaller than the input communication for c < P. The memory per processor increases by a factor of c, because each processor must hold its share of the replicated inputs. Specifically, the input storage per processor is O(n² c / P), plus O(n²/P) for the output block.

The trade-off is stark: you spend memory to buy bandwidth. The optimal c is the largest value that fits in memory. If M is the memory per processor, then c is bounded by c ≤ M P / n². The algorithm is communication-optimal given that constraint. In practice, c is chosen as a power of two that divides P, and the replication is managed by having each processor load the appropriate blocks from a shared file system or by broadcasting them once at the start. The 2.5D algorithm is particularly attractive on machines with large memory per node but a relatively slow interconnect, such as clusters with many-core nodes. It is implemented in libraries like Elemental and has been used in high-performance LINPACK benchmarks.

## Tournament pivoting: communication-avoiding LU

LU factorization with partial pivoting is the workhorse of dense linear algebra. The standard algorithm proceeds column by column: at step k, find the element of largest magnitude in column k below the diagonal, swap rows, then update the trailing submatrix. On a distributed-memory machine, finding the pivot requires a global reduction at every step, and the row swap requires communication. The result is that standard LU with partial pivoting requires O(n) messages per processor, which is far from the lower bound of O(n³/M^(3/2)) messages. The pivoting is the bottleneck.

Tournament pivoting, developed by Grigori, Demmel, and Xiang, restructures the pivoting to be communication-efficient. The idea is to select a block of b pivots at once, rather than one at a time, using a tournament among the rows. The algorithm partitions the rows of the current panel into blocks. Each block computes its own local pivots by performing a small LU factorization with partial pivoting on its own rows. Then the local pivot rows are compared in a reduction tree: pairs of blocks are merged, and the better pivots are selected. The winner of the tournament provides the b pivot rows for the entire panel. These rows are then swapped into place, and the update proceeds.

The effect is that the number of global reductions is reduced by a factor of b. Instead of O(n) reductions, we have O(n/b) reductions. The communication cost for the pivoting becomes O(n²/b) words and O(n/b) messages, which is negligible compared to the O(n³/√M) words and O(n³/M^(3/2)) messages required for the update. The overall algorithm attains the lower bound for LU communication, up to constants. The extra computation is O(n³) but with a larger constant; the tournament itself requires O(n² b) comparisons and some extra flops, but this is asymptotically smaller than the O(n³) update.

The numerical stability of tournament pivoting is a subtle point. It is not identical to partial pivoting, but extensive testing has shown it to be stable in practice. The growth factor is bounded by a function that is slightly worse than partial pivoting, but still acceptable for most applications. The algorithm is used in the Elemental library and in communication-avoiding versions of ScaLAPACK. It is a key example of how a seemingly sequential operation — pivoting — can be restructured to expose parallelism and reduce communication.

## Tall-Skinny QR: reducing messages in QR

QR factorization is used in least squares, orthogonalization, and Krylov methods. For a tall-skinny matrix — one with many more rows than columns, say m × n with m ≫ n — the standard Householder QR algorithm applies a sequence of n Householder reflectors. Each reflector is a rank-one update that must be applied to the remaining matrix. On a distributed-memory machine, this requires a broadcast of the reflector (or at least its essential parts) to all processors, followed by a local update. The result is O(n) messages and O(m n) words moved per processor, which is a latency bottleneck when n is large.

Tall-Skinny QR (TSQR) restructures the computation to reduce the number of messages. The algorithm partitions the rows of the matrix into P blocks, one per processor. Each processor computes a local QR factorization of its block, producing a local R factor of size n × n and an implicit Q factor. The local R factors are then combined in a binary tree: at each level, pairs of R factors are stacked and a QR factorization is computed on the stacked matrix. The final R factor is the product of all the local R factors (up to orthogonal transformations), and the Q factor can be reconstructed if needed.

The communication cost is dramatically lower. Each processor sends its R factor (n² words) up the tree. The tree has log P levels, so the total words sent per processor is O(n² log P). The number of messages is O(log P), independent of n. Compare this to the O(n) messages of standard QR: for n = 1000 and P = 1024, TSQR uses about 10 messages instead of 1000. The flops are the same asymptotically, though the constant is higher because of the tree reduction and the fact that the local QR factorizations are done on smaller matrices. The algorithm is communication-avoiding because it reduces the number of messages asymptotically, and it is particularly effective when m is very large and n is moderate.

TSQR is used in many applications. In MapReduce and Spark, it is used for distributed least squares and principal component analysis. In Krylov methods, it is used to orthogonalize a set of s basis vectors at once, which is a key component of s-step methods. The main limitation is that the Q factor is not formed explicitly; if Q is needed, it must be reconstructed, which costs additional communication. But for many applications, only R or the ability to apply Q^T is needed, and TSQR provides that efficiently.

## s-step Krylov methods: reducing synchronization

Krylov subspace methods — conjugate gradient (CG), GMRES, and their variants — are the standard iterative solvers for sparse linear systems. Each iteration typically involves a sparse matrix-vector product (SpMV) and one or more inner products (dot products). The inner products require a global reduction, which is a synchronization point across all processors. On a large machine, the latency of a global reduction can be on the order of microseconds, while the SpMV may take only a few microseconds per iteration. As a result, the reductions dominate the runtime, and the algorithm becomes latency-bound rather than bandwidth-bound.

s-step Krylov methods break this bottleneck by computing s basis vectors at once. Instead of performing one SpMV and one reduction per iteration, the algorithm performs s SpMVs to build a basis for the Krylov subspace of dimension s, and then performs a single reduction to compute all the inner products needed to orthogonalize the basis and update the solution. The number of global reductions is reduced by a factor of s. For CG, the s-step algorithm is known as s-step CG; for GMRES, it is s-step GMRES. The basis is typically constructed using a monomial, Newton, or Chebyshev basis, and the small s × s system is solved locally on each processor.

The communication cost is reduced in terms of messages, not necessarily words. The inner products now involve s² dot products, which can be computed in one reduction of s² words per processor. The SpMV communication is unchanged. The total words moved may increase slightly because of the larger basis, but the number of messages drops by a factor of s. On a machine where latency is the dominant cost, this is a substantial win. The optimal s depends on the ratio of latency to bandwidth and on the numerical properties of the problem.

The main challenge is numerical stability. As s increases, the basis vectors become nearly linearly dependent, and the algorithm loses orthogonality. This can lead to slow convergence or even divergence. To mitigate this, s-step methods often use a more stable basis (such as a Chebyshev basis) and may require reorthogonalization. The extra computation is O(s² n) flops per step, which is negligible if s is small (typically s ≤ 10). The algorithms are implemented in libraries like PETSc and Trilinos, and they are particularly effective on machines with high latency interconnects, such as Blue Gene or large GPU clusters.

These four algorithms — 2.5D matrix multiplication, tournament-pivoting LU, TSQR, and s-step Krylov methods — illustrate the range of communication-avoiding techniques. They all trade something (memory, extra computation, numerical stability) for reduced data movement. The choice of which to use depends on the problem, the machine, and the relative costs of bandwidth, latency, and flops. The next section will examine the practical costs in more detail and discuss how to decide whether communication-avoiding restructuring is worth the effort.

## The arithmetic of the trade

The algorithms above are existence proofs before they are recipes. They show that the lower bound can be approached, not that approaching it is profitable on a particular machine. Turning the theory into a decision requires writing down a cost model and being honest about which terms dominate. A useful first approximation is

`T = α F + β W + γ Q + δ D`

where `F` is scalar work, `W` is words moved across the boundary being optimized, `Q` is messages, and `D` is the critical path through dependent transfers. The coefficients are not machine constants so much as effective rates: `α` is the reciprocal of sustained flop throughput, `β` is the reciprocal of sustainable bandwidth for the access pattern in question, and `γ` includes protocol overhead, software stack costs, and the portion of latency that cannot be overlapped. The last term, `D`, is the one most often omitted from back-of-the-envelope analysis and the one most likely to invalidate it.

A communication-avoiding restructuring replaces a baseline tuple `(F, W, Q, D)` with `(F + ΔF, W - ΔW, Q - ΔQ, D')`. It wins only if the saved transfer terms exceed the added computation, added storage pressure, and any increase in dependency depth. That inequality is not universal. It depends on the ratio `γ/β`, on the achievable arithmetic intensity, and on how close the baseline already is to the lower bound. If the baseline moves twice as many words as the bound requires, the maximum possible bandwidth saving is a factor of two. If the restructuring adds twenty percent more flops and the kernel is already flop-bound, the net result can be a loss even though the communication count improved. The bound tells you the size of the prize; the cost model tells you whether you can collect it.

### The memory budget is not a free parameter

The 2.5D algorithm is often described as buying bandwidth with memory, and that description is accurate but incomplete. The replication factor `c` consumes capacity at the level where the replicas reside. It also consumes workspace for partial reductions, communication buffers, and the output block. If `M` is the nominal capacity per processor, the usable `M` is smaller: cache associativity, TLB reach, page granularity, and reserved memory all take a cut. A plan that requires `c` copies to exactly fill memory is a plan that will spill, page, or lose occupancy under real workloads.

The more important subtlety is that replication reduces interprocessor communication, not local memory traffic. Each processor still reads its replicated panels from DRAM into cache, and those reads may become the new bottleneck. The lower bound itself changes when the fast-memory size changes. If the boundary being optimized is the network, then `M` is local memory and the 2.5D layout can be communication-optimal. If the boundary is last-level cache, then replication may increase local traffic and evict useful data, and the algorithm can be worse than a simpler blocked implementation. The same restructuring can be a win at one level of the hierarchy and a loss at another. Choosing the boundary is therefore part of the algorithm design, not an implementation detail.

There is also a scheduling cost. The three-dimensional grid requires `P/c` to be a perfect square for a clean layout, or it requires a rectangular grid and a more complex mapping. The reduction across the `c` layers can be done as a tree or as a linear chain. A tree has depth `log c` and better latency, but it leaves processors idle at the upper levels. A chain has depth `c` and keeps every layer busy but pays a longer dependency chain. Neither is free. The total message count may be small while the critical path is not.

### Messages, packets, and the cost of being small

Message count is a useful abstraction only when messages are large enough to amortize their fixed costs. A small message and a large message do not have the same effective `γ`. On an interconnect, latency has a fixed component and a size-dependent component; on a GPU, a kernel launch or a synchronization has a fixed cost that dwarfs the payload for small transfers. TSQR illustrates the issue. It reduces the number of messages from `O(n)` to `O(log P)`, but each message carries an `n × n` triangular factor. When `n` is small, that factor may be below the latency-bandwidth product, so the message is latency-dominated and the asymptotic message reduction is less valuable than it appears. When `n` is large, the same message is bandwidth-dominated, and the advantage shifts toward total words moved rather than message count.

s-step Krylov methods have the same crossover. The baseline performs one reduction per iteration. The s-step variant performs one reduction per `s` iterations, but that reduction carries `s²` inner products. A crude model for the reduction cost over `s` baseline iterations is

`baseline: s γ + s β_r`

` s-step: γ + s² β_r`

where `β_r` is the per-word cost of the reduction payload and `γ` is the fixed reduction cost. The s-step variant pays off when `(s - 1) γ > s² β_r`, which means `s` must be smaller than roughly the ratio of fixed latency to per-word cost. If the latency-bandwidth product is large, a larger `s` can help; if the reduction is already bandwidth-bound, increasing `s` mainly increases the payload. The optimal `s` is therefore a property of the machine and the matrix, not a universal constant.

### The critical path survives

Reducing the total number of messages does not automatically reduce the longest chain of dependent messages. A tournament pivot selection has a reduction tree of depth logarithmic in the panel width; TSQR has a binary tree over processors; 2.5D has a reduction over the replication layers. These trees are shallow, but they are not free. At each level, a processor may wait for a partner, and at the top of the tree most processors are idle. If the machine is latency-bound and only a few transfers can be in flight, the depth of the tree sets a floor on time even when the total message count is small.

The same issue appears in load balance. A tree reduction is balanced in work but not in waiting. A replicated 2.5D layout is symmetric only if the matrix dimensions divide evenly across the grid and if every processor has the same memory bandwidth. In practice, some nodes are slower, some links are oversubscribed, and the reduction completes when the slowest contributor finishes. Communication-avoiding algorithms often increase the granularity of synchronization: fewer, larger reductions can create longer straggler tails than many small ones. The relevant metric is not the average message cost but the tail latency of the slowest path.

### Where the model stops being predictive

The two-level model is a simplification, and the simplification is load-bearing. Real caches are set-associative, so a block that fits in `M` bytes may conflict-miss in a smaller effective capacity. TLB reach can be the binding limit for large replicated panels, especially when the replication changes the stride pattern. On multi-socket nodes, local memory and remote memory have different latencies and bandwidths; a communication-avoiding layout that increases remote accesses can lose even if it reduces total words. On GPUs, shared memory is small, bank conflicts are data-dependent, and global memory bandwidth is shared across all resident blocks. The model also ignores coherence traffic and false sharing, both of which can turn an apparently local update into a network transaction.

A practical analysis therefore needs measurements at the intended boundary. Cache counters, memory-controller counters, interconnect counters, and occupancy are more informative than a single wall-clock number. If the counters show that the baseline is already at the bandwidth ceiling for the relevant level, then reducing words helps. If they show that the baseline is stalled on dependency latency with unused bandwidth, then reducing messages may help more. If they show that the baseline is compute-bound, neither helps without a corresponding reduction in flops.

### Numerical behavior is part of the cost model

For direct factorizations, the numerical cost is usually a stability margin. Tournament pivoting has a different growth factor from partial pivoting; it is stable in practice, but the constant is not identical. 2.5D changes the order of summation in the reduction, so bitwise results differ and reproducibility across processor counts is harder. TSQR is stable, but reconstructing `Q` costs additional communication and may be less accurate than applying the local reflectors. These are not reasons to reject the methods; they are reasons to include numerical behavior in the objective function rather than treating it as an afterthought.

For iterative methods, the numerical cost can dominate. Time to solution is time per iteration multiplied by the number of iterations. An s-step method that reduces reductions by a factor of four but increases iteration count by fifty percent has a net message saving of only about 2.7, and it may lose more if the larger basis increases SpMV traffic. A tournament-pivoting LU that requires more careful scaling may need extra work in the triangular solve. A 2.5D reduction that changes rounding may shift convergence for an ill-conditioned system. The correct comparison is end-to-end time and accuracy on representative problems, not the asymptotic message count of an inner kernel.

### A decision procedure

A defensible workflow has seven steps.

First, measure the baseline in all three currencies. Count flops, words moved across the candidate boundary, and messages, and record the critical path where it can be isolated. Do not rely on a single bandwidth number; fit `β` and `γ` for the message sizes and access patterns that the algorithm actually uses.

Second, identify the dominant term. If the baseline is compute-bound at the chosen boundary, communication-avoiding restructuring is unlikely to pay unless it also reduces flops or exposes more parallelism. If it is bandwidth-bound, estimate the ratio of baseline words to the lower bound. If that ratio is small, the remaining headroom is small.

Third, check capacity. For 2.5D, compute the largest `c` that fits with headroom and still divides the processor grid cleanly. For tournament pivoting, compute the panel width `b` that the memory and reduction tree can support. For s-step methods, compute the largest `s` whose basis and workspace fit without spilling. The algorithm is only communication-avoiding if the required state actually stays resident.

Fourth, model the critical path, not just the total count. A reduction tree of depth `d` imposes at least `d` dependent latencies. A replicated layout imposes a reduction over `c` layers. A panel factorization imposes a tree over `b` row blocks. If the depth is comparable to the baseline message count, the asymptotic win may not appear at realistic scale.

Fifth, account for load imbalance. Replication and tree reductions assume that all participants contribute on the same timescale. Sparse matrices, irregular panels, and heterogeneous nodes violate that assumption. The tail, not the mean, determines completion time.

Sixth, prototype the smallest interesting variant and validate numerical behavior on matrices that resemble the target workload. Check convergence, growth factors, and reproducibility. A method that is stable on random matrices can fail on the structured matrices that arise in practice.

Seventh, retune the block sizes after the restructuring. The optimal tile size changes when the algorithm moves from many small messages to fewer large ones, and the optimal `c`, `b`, or `s` changes with the problem size and processor count. A communication-avoiding implementation tuned with the baseline parameters is not a fair test.

The practical question is therefore not whether avoidance is theoretically superior. It is whether the saved transfer terms exceed the added computation, storage pressure, dependency depth, and numerical risk at the scale and boundary where the code actually runs. That question has a different answer for a cache-resident kernel, a DRAM-bound node, and a latency-bound cluster, and the same algorithm can be right for one and wrong for the others.

## Sparse and irregular problems: the bound becomes graph-dependent

The dense lower bounds are clean because the dependency graph is regular. A sparse matrix-vector product has a dependency graph that is the matrix graph, and the communication cost is governed by how that graph is partitioned. For a matrix with a symmetric sparsity pattern, the standard 1D row partitioning assigns rows to processors. Each processor needs the vector entries corresponding to the columns of its nonzeros. The communication volume is the sum over processors of the number of off-processor columns touched. For a 2D grid graph, a 1D partitioning has a boundary of size O(√n), so each SpMV requires O(√n) words per processor. A 2D partitioning, which assigns nonzeros to a 2D grid of processors, reduces the boundary to O(n^{1/4}) for the same graph. Hypergraph partitioning generalizes this: it partitions the nonzeros so that the cut is minimized, and the communication volume is the number of cut hyperedges. This is a communication-avoiding technique in the sense that it restructures the data layout to reduce words moved, but it does not change the arithmetic. The lower bound for SpMV is related to the edge expansion of the graph: if the graph has a small separator, you can partition it to reduce communication; if it is an expander, every partition has a large boundary, and communication is unavoidable. This is a different kind of lower bound: it depends on the graph, not just on the number of flops and M. The practical consequence is that for sparse problems, the best you can do is often determined by the graph's separator structure. Graph partitioning is therefore a first-class communication-avoiding technique, not a preprocessing afterthought. For sparse direct factorization, the situation is similar. Multifrontal and supernodal methods use a separator tree; the communication cost is related to the size of the separators and the amount of fill. Nested dissection reduces both fill and communication by choosing separators recursively. The lower bounds for sparse Cholesky are expressed in terms of the separator tree and the number of nonzeros, not simply n^3/√M. This is why sparse solvers are often communication-bound in a way that dense solvers are not: the arithmetic is sparse, so the ratio of flops to words is low, and the graph structure dominates. s-step Krylov methods help with the inner products, but the SpMV communication remains. To reduce that, you need a different data distribution, a different ordering, or a preconditioner that is cheaper to apply. This is an active area where the lower bounds are less clean and the algorithms are more heuristic.

## FFT and tensor contractions: when the dependency graph is not a matrix

The fast Fourier transform is another canonical communication-avoiding problem. The standard Cooley-Tukey algorithm has a dependency graph that is a butterfly network. The red-blue pebble game applies, and the lower bound for FFT on a two-level memory is Ω(n log n / log M) words, where n is the transform size and M is the fast memory size. The standard six-step FFT attains this bound up to constants. The six-step algorithm reshapes the data into a matrix, transposes it, performs batched FFTs on the rows, multiplies by twiddle factors, transposes again, and performs batched FFTs on the columns. The transposes are the communication steps. By choosing the shape of the matrix so that the transposes are cache-blocked, the number of words moved is reduced. The log M in the denominator is weaker than the √M in matrix multiplication, so the payoff from increasing fast memory is logarithmic rather than square-root. This is why FFT is often considered less communication-avoiding than matrix multiplication: the bound is still there, but the gains are smaller. Nevertheless, on modern machines with deep memory hierarchies, a well-blocked FFT can be several times faster than a naive implementation, and the algorithmic restructuring is essential.

Tensor contractions generalize matrix multiplication and FFT. A tensor contraction is a sum over shared indices between two or more tensors. The communication lower bounds for tensor contractions depend on the number of indices, their dimensions, and the assignment of indices to processors. For a contraction that can be mapped to matrix multiplication, the n^3/√M bound applies. For higher-order contractions, the exponent changes. The general theory uses the red-blue pebble game on the dependency graph and derives bounds in terms of the number of scalar multiplications and the fast memory size. The algorithmic restructurings are similar: block the tensors, replicate them if memory allows, and use a reduction tree for the contracted indices. The 2.5D idea extends to tensor contractions: you can replicate the inputs along a third dimension and reduce the communication by a factor of √c, at the cost of memory. This is used in quantum chemistry and machine learning workloads, where tensor contractions dominate. The practical challenge is that the optimal layout depends on the contraction order and the dimensions, and the search space is large. Autotuning is often necessary.

## The hardware curve: why the case strengthens

The case against moving data is not a static argument. It has strengthened over time because compute throughput has grown faster than memory bandwidth. The roofline model makes this concrete: a kernel with arithmetic intensity I flops per byte is limited by min(peak flops, I × bandwidth). As peak flops increase and bandwidth stagnates, the ridge point moves to the right, and more kernels become bandwidth-bound. Communication-avoiding algorithms increase arithmetic intensity by reusing data in fast memory. They move the kernel to the right on the roofline, where the compute roof is higher. This is why they are increasingly important for large-scale machine learning, scientific computing, and graph analytics.

The energy argument reinforces the bandwidth argument. Moving a word from DRAM to a register costs orders of magnitude more energy than performing a floating-point operation on it. The exact ratio depends on the technology, but the qualitative fact is robust: data movement dominates energy consumption in many workloads. Communication-avoiding algorithms reduce both time and energy. This is why they have outlived the specific machines they were invented for. The trend toward 3D-stacked memory, HBM, and chiplets is an architectural response to the same problem: bring memory closer to compute. But the lower bounds tell us that even with perfect memory, there is a minimum amount of movement between levels. Algorithms must be designed to respect that minimum.

On GPUs, the fast memory is shared memory and registers. The red-blue pebble game applies with M equal to the size of shared memory plus the register file. The 2.5D matrix multiplication can be mapped to a cluster of GPUs by replicating data across nodes and using the GPU's shared memory for the inner blocking. s-step Krylov methods reduce the number of global synchronizations, which are expensive on GPUs because they require kernel launches or cooperative groups. TSQR is used to orthogonalize blocks of vectors on GPUs, reducing the number of small messages. The same principles apply, but the constants are different: GPU memory bandwidth is high, but latency is also high, and the latency-bandwidth product is large. This shifts the optimal parameters toward larger messages and more aggressive replication.

## Programming models and implementation realities

Expressing communication-avoiding algorithms requires more than calling a library function. It requires control over data distribution, replication, and reduction trees. MPI is the most flexible low-level model: you can implement 2.5D by creating communicators for the layers and using allreduce or reduce-scatter for the reduction. But MPI does not manage memory automatically; you must allocate the replicas and keep track of which processor owns which block. PGAS languages like UPC and Chapel provide a global address space, which simplifies the expression of replicated arrays, but they do not automatically choose the replication factor or the reduction tree. Task-based runtimes like Legion, Regent, and StarPU allow you to express the data dependencies and let the runtime schedule the tasks. They can discover parallelism and communication, but they need to be told about the algorithm's structure. For example, a task-based 2.5D algorithm would create tasks for each block multiplication and a reduction tree for the partial sums. The runtime can then overlap communication and computation.

Autotuning is essential. The parameters c, b, s, and the block sizes are not universal. They depend on the machine's memory hierarchy, interconnect, and the problem dimensions. A communication-avoiding implementation that is not tuned can be slower than a well-tuned conventional one. Libraries like Elemental, SLATE, and DPLASMA implement some of these algorithms and provide autotuning. But portability remains a challenge: a layout that is optimal for a fat-tree interconnect may be poor for a torus, and a replication factor that fits in memory on one node may not fit on another. The algorithms must be parameterized and the parameters must be chosen at runtime or install time.

Fault tolerance is another consideration. Communication-avoiding algorithms often hold more state in memory and have longer dependency chains. A 2.5D algorithm replicates data, so a single node failure may lose a replica, but the redundancy can also be used for recovery. However, checkpointing more data costs more communication, which partially offsets the savings. s-step methods have longer phases between synchronizations, so a failure during a phase loses more work. These are practical costs that are not captured by the lower bounds.

## Mixed precision as communication avoidance

Reducing the precision of data movement is a complementary way to reduce communication. If you send a message in FP16 instead of FP64, you move half the bytes. The number of messages is unchanged, but the bandwidth cost is halved. This is not an algorithmic restructuring in the same sense as 2.5D, but it is a communication-avoiding technique: it reduces the words moved per unit of computation. Mixed precision iterative refinement combines this with algorithmic restructuring. The idea is to solve the system in low precision, compute the residual in high precision, and use the residual to correct the solution. The bulk of the work — the factorization and the triangular solves — is done in low precision, so the communication is reduced. The high-precision residual computation is cheaper because it is done less frequently. For dense linear algebra, mixed precision LU with iterative refinement can achieve FP64 accuracy while doing most of the flops in FP32 or FP16. This reduces both computation and communication. The lower bound applies to the precision used for the bulk: if you use FP16, the effective M in bytes is the same, but you can fit more elements, so the bound in terms of elements changes. The communication in bytes is halved. This is multiplicative with algorithmic restructuring: you can use 2.5D to reduce messages and mixed precision to reduce bytes per message. The combination can be very effective on machines with high bandwidth but limited memory capacity.

## The next boundary: where the lower bounds are still open

The cleanest lower bounds are for dense direct linear algebra. The next frontier is iterative and randomized algorithms. For Krylov methods, the lower bounds are less developed because the number of iterations is problem-dependent and the communication per iteration is dominated by SpMV. s-step methods reduce the number of reductions, but the optimal s depends on the convergence rate, which is not known a priori. Randomized algorithms, such as randomized least squares and randomized sketching, have different communication patterns. They may move less data because they can use smaller sketches, but the lower bounds are not yet fully understood. For graph algorithms, the communication lower bounds are often expressed in terms of the graph's expansion and the number of rounds. For machine learning, the communication-avoiding techniques are being applied to distributed training, where the bottleneck is often the allreduce of gradients. Techniques like gradient compression, local SGD, and federated averaging are communication-avoiding in the sense that they reduce the number of messages or the number of words. The lower bounds for these problems are an active area of research. The case against moving data is strongest when the data movement is asymptotically larger than the computation. As long as that remains true, the algorithms will continue to evolve.

## Case study: tuning a communication-avoiding kernel

A lower bound is a target, not a tuning recipe. To make the trade concrete, consider a dense matrix multiplication on a cluster where each node has a fixed amount of memory and a network that is fast but not infinitely so. The baseline is a 2D blocked algorithm with a square processor grid. It broadcasts panels along rows and columns and reaches a per-processor word count proportional to n²/√P and a message count proportional to √P. The lower bound says that with fast memory M per node, the word count can be as low as n³/(P√M) and the message count as low as n³/(P M^{3/2}) for the distributed case, up to constants and assuming M is not larger than the local output block. The gap between the baseline and the bound is the prize.

The first step is to measure which term is actually binding. If the network counters show that the baseline is already near the link's sustainable bandwidth, then reducing words is the goal, and replication along a third grid dimension is the natural lever. If the counters show low link utilization but long stalls at collective operations, then message count and critical path are the problem, and a deeper reduction tree or a larger panel width may help more. The two diagnoses lead to different parameters even for the same algorithm.

Suppose the diagnosis is bandwidth. The 2.5D layout adds a third dimension of size c. The processor grid becomes P1 × P2 × c, with P1 = P2 = √(P/c). Each processor computes a partial product over a slice of the inner dimension. The partial results are reduced across the c layers. The per-processor word count for input communication drops roughly as 1/√c, while the reduction adds a term proportional to the output block size times c. The reduction term is usually small when the output block is small, but it is not zero. If c is too large, the reduction traffic can exceed the input traffic it was meant to replace.

The memory budget sets the upper limit on c. Replication consumes capacity for the input panels, for communication buffers, for the reduction workspace, and for the output block. A useful rule is to compute the largest c that leaves at least twenty percent headroom at the level being optimized. Headroom is not waste; it is what prevents the allocator from spilling, the cache from conflict-missing, and the runtime from serializing on a single buffer. If the nominal capacity is M and the algorithm's static footprint is S(c), the constraint is S(c) ≤ 0.8 M. The factor is a starting point, not a law; it should be tuned with counters.

The grid shape adds a divisibility constraint. P/c should be a perfect square for the clean 3D layout, or the implementation must handle a rectangular grid with a more complex mapping. A rectangular grid changes the block sizes, the reduction pattern, and the load balance. It is often better to choose c as a power of two that divides P and leaves a square P/c than to choose the theoretically optimal c that forces an irregular decomposition. The difference between c = 8 and c = 9 is rarely worth an irregular communication pattern.

The reduction across the c layers can be implemented as a tree or a chain. A tree has depth log c and uses fewer dependent steps, but it leaves processors idle at the upper levels. A chain has depth c and keeps every layer busy, but it lengthens the critical path. On a latency-bound machine, the tree is usually better; on a bandwidth-bound machine with large messages, the chain can be better because it overlaps more transfers. The choice should be made with the same counter data that diagnosed the bottleneck. If the reduction messages are large, the chain's extra depth may be hidden by bandwidth; if they are small, the tree's shorter depth matters more.

Finally, the block sizes inside each processor must be retuned. The 2.5D layout changes the shape of the local panels. A tile size that was optimal for the 2D algorithm may cause bank conflicts, TLB misses, or poor register reuse in the 3D layout. The inner kernel should be autotuned again, not inherited. The same applies to the reduction: the buffer size, the number of outstanding messages, and the order of the reduction all affect the achieved bandwidth. A communication-avoiding algorithm that is not retuned is a communication-avoiding algorithm that is not yet a win.

## Replication, capacity, and the effective fast-memory size

The lower bounds are expressed in terms of M, the size of the fast memory. Replication changes the relationship between the nominal capacity and the effective M. If a processor holds c copies of an input panel, those copies occupy space that could otherwise hold a larger tile of the output or a deeper pipeline of the inner dimension. The effective fast memory for the computation is not the total capacity; it is the capacity left after the replicas, the buffers, and the metadata are placed. The bound still holds, but it holds with a smaller M. A plan that ignores this substitution can overestimate the benefit of replication.

The effect is easiest to see in a cache. Suppose a node has a last-level cache of size M. A 2D blocked algorithm uses the entire cache for a single copy of the working set. A 2.5D algorithm with replication factor c uses a fraction of the cache for replicas, leaving less room for the output tile and the streaming inputs. If the working set was already close to the cache size, the replicas can push it over the edge, and the algorithm begins to miss where the baseline hit. The result is that the algorithm with fewer network words can have more local words, and the total time can increase. The relevant counter is not just network traffic but the sum of local and remote traffic weighted by their respective costs.

Power and energy follow the same logic. Replication increases the number of memory cells that must be written and refreshed. It also increases the number of bytes that must be read from local memory to feed the replicated panels into the compute units. If the energy cost of a local DRAM read is a significant fraction of the energy cost of a network transfer, then replication can reduce energy even when it increases local traffic, because network transfers are usually more expensive per byte. But if the local memory is already the dominant energy consumer, replication can increase total energy. The trade is machine-specific and should be measured, not assumed.

There is also a capacity-driven scheduling cost. Replication makes the memory footprint larger, which reduces the number of concurrent tasks that can be resident. On a GPU, this can lower occupancy and reduce the ability to hide latency. On a CPU, it can reduce the number of threads that fit in the last-level cache. The communication-avoiding algorithm may reduce messages but lose the latency hiding that the baseline enjoyed from having more resident work. The lower bound says nothing about occupancy; it assumes that the fast memory is used optimally. In practice, the fast memory must be shared with the runtime, the operating system, and the other tenants of the node.

The practical conclusion is that replication is not a free parameter even when memory is technically available. It consumes capacity, power, and scheduling freedom. The optimal replication factor is the one that maximizes end-to-end throughput after all of these costs are included. The lower bound tells you the direction of the trade; the machine tells you how far to go.
