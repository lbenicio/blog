Consider two workloads that look nothing alike.

In the first, a finite element simulation has discretized a car body into tens of millions of tetrahedra, and the solver runs across 512 MPI ranks. Each rank owns a subset of the elements. Every element that shares a face with an element owned by a different rank forces an exchange of boundary data — on every solver iteration, for the entire run. In the second, a graph analytics job runs PageRank over a web-scale graph on a few thousand machines, and every edge that crosses a machine boundary becomes a network message on every superstep.

Different physics, different data structures, different runtimes, and the same combinatorial problem underneath. You have a graph. You have a fixed number of parts. You must assign each vertex to a part so that few edges cross part boundaries, and so that no part is much larger than any other. That is graph partitioning, and it is one of the quiet load-bearing walls of parallel and distributed computing. It rarely headlines a paper, but it decides whether a cluster spends its time computing or waiting on an interconnect.

This article is about how production-grade partitioners actually work. The short version is that they do not solve the problem you stated. They solve a sequence of much easier problems whose solutions compose into a good answer for the hard one. That trick — the multilevel paradigm — is the single most important idea in the field, and it is worth understanding in detail because it generalizes: the same coarsen-solve-refine structure shows up in sparse matrix ordering, in graph drawing, in mesh generation, and in a surprising number of other combinatorial optimization problems.

We will work through the objective functions and why they disagree with each other, the two local search algorithms that do the actual polishing, the coarsening machinery that makes those algorithms effective, and the parallel and streaming variants you reach for when the graph no longer fits in one machine's memory. Along the way we will look at where the standard formulation breaks down entirely — power-law graphs, where minimizing edge cut is close to the wrong thing to do — and what replaces it.

## The Problem, Stated Precisely

Let `G = (V, E)` be an undirected graph. Each vertex `v` carries a weight `w(v) > 0` (the amount of work it represents, or the number of nonzeros it owns, or its memory footprint). Each edge `e` carries a weight `c(e) > 0` (the cost of communicating across it, or simply 1).

A `k`-way partition assigns every vertex to exactly one of `k` parts. Write `P(v)` for the part containing `v`. The partition is **balanced** if, for every part `i`,

```
W(i) = sum of w(v) over v with P(v) = i  <=  (1 + epsilon) * W_total / k
```

for some imbalance tolerance `epsilon >= 0`. The **edge cut** is the total weight of edges whose endpoints land in different parts:

```
cut(P) = sum of c(e) over e = (u, v) with P(u) != P(v)
```

The optimization problem is: minimize `cut(P)` subject to the balance constraint. Finding the optimal partition is NP-hard, and not in a soft way — even the simplest interesting case, partitioning into two balanced parts, is NP-hard. There is no polynomial-time approximation scheme for general graphs unless P = NP.

Two structural choices matter more than they first appear.

**Recursive bisection versus direct k-way.** One way to build a `k`-way partition is to split the graph in two, then split each half in two, and so on, until you have `k` parts. This is recursive bisection, and it is what METIS does internally. The alternative is to compute all `k` parts simultaneously, which is what KaHIP, Scotch, and the `k`-way mode of METIS do. Direct `k`-way is generally better because recursive bisection optimizes the wrong thing at each level: a bisection that looks good at the top of the recursion tree may force a bad split three levels down. The gap is real but usually modest, and recursive bisection parallelizes more easily because each subtree is independent.

**Graphs versus hypergraphs.** A finite element mesh is not naturally a graph. It is a hypergraph: each element is a hyperedge connecting all the nodes it touches, and each node belongs to many elements. The natural cut metric for hypergraphs is not the number of cut edges but the **connectivity-1** metric — for each hyperedge, count the number of distinct parts its vertices touch, and subtract one. A hyperedge spanning three parts contributes 2 to the cost, not 3, because a message sent to three destinations costs roughly the same as a message sent to two if you are doing one collective broadcast per hyperedge.

This distinction is not academic. Converting a hypergraph to a clique graph — replacing each hyperedge of size `s` with `s(s-1)/2` edges — changes the objective, inflates the graph, and produces partitions that are measurably worse for the applications that motivated the problem. Real partitioners for VLSI and for finite element meshes operate on hypergraphs directly, and the algorithms below have hypergraph analogues that are more complicated but not fundamentally different.

## Three Objectives That Are Not The Same Objective

Every partitioner reports a number. Almost none of them report the number you care about.

The number you care about is wall-clock time of your application. The numbers partitioners optimize are proxies, and the proxies disagree with each other in ways that matter.

**Edge cut** is the classic objective. It is easy to compute, easy to reason about, and it is what most algorithms are designed to reduce. Its appeal is that it is local: the contribution of each edge depends only on the two vertices it connects.

**Communication volume** is different. For a vertex `v`, count the number of distinct parts among its neighbors. The total communication volume is the sum of those counts over all vertices. Consider a vertex with 100 neighbors, all in a single remote part: it contributes 1 to communication volume but 100 to the edge cut. A vertex with 2 neighbors in 2 different remote parts contributes 2 to both. If your application does one message per remote part per vertex, communication volume is the right metric and edge cut over-weights high-degree vertices. If your application does one message per edge, edge cut is right. Most real applications are somewhere in between, and the partitioner usually cannot know which.

**Balance** is a constraint, not an objective, but the tolerance you choose changes the answer dramatically. A tight `epsilon` (say 1%) forces the partitioner to spend cut quality to satisfy it. A loose `epsilon` (say 10%) gives the optimizer room to find much better cuts, at the cost of leaving some ranks idle. The right value depends on whether your application is compute-bound or communication-bound, and on whether the slowest rank determines the iteration time. In most bulk-synchronous applications it does.

There is a fourth factor that partitioners usually ignore entirely: **the shape of the subdomains**. Two partitions with identical edge cut can have very different performance if one produces long, stringy subdomains with high surface-to-volume ratio and the other produces compact, blob-like ones. Compact subdomains mean fewer boundary vertices, better cache reuse within a rank, and — critically for distributed-memory codes — fewer distinct neighbors, which reduces the number of messages even when the total bytes are the same. Message count, not message volume, is often the binding constraint on a high-latency interconnect, and edge cut says nothing about it.

Finally there is the cost of partitioning itself. For a graph with a hundred billion edges, the partitioner is a distributed computation in its own right, and its runtime is part of your job's runtime. A partitioner that produces a 3% better cut but takes 40 minutes longer than the alternative has made your application slower, not faster, unless the job runs long enough to amortize it. This is why streaming partitioners — which see each vertex once, in arbitrary order, and never revisit a decision — exist at all despite producing noticeably worse cuts than multilevel methods.

## Why The Obvious Approaches Fall Short

Before the multilevel idea, it is worth seeing why the direct approaches fail, because the failure mode is instructive.

**Random assignment** gives a cut fraction of roughly `1 - 1/k`. For `k = 512`, that means 99.8% of edges are cut. It is a useful sanity check on your measurement harness and nothing else.

**Breadth-first search from a seed** grows a connected region outward until it hits the balance limit, then starts a new seed in the unvisited remainder. On meshes with geometric coordinates this is respectable. On general graphs it is fragile: the growth front can be enormous, the region shape is determined entirely by the seed, and the final region can be a thin tendril wrapping around the whole graph. Greedy graph growing — the same idea but choosing the next vertex by a gain function rather than by BFS order — is better and is still used as an initial partitioning heuristic inside multilevel partitioners, but only on graphs that have already been coarsened to a few hundred vertices.

**Spectral bisection** computes the eigenvector associated with the second-smallest eigenvalue of the graph Laplacian (the Fiedler vector), sorts vertices by their value in that vector, and splits at the median. The quality is excellent — spectral methods capture global structure that local methods cannot see — but the cost is prohibitive. Computing an eigenvector of a sparse matrix is iterative, requires several sparse matrix-vector products per iteration, converges slowly when the graph has a small spectral gap, and is difficult to parallelize efficiently. Spectral methods survive in practice as one option among several for the initial partitioning step at the coarsest level, where the graph has a few hundred vertices and the eigenvector computation is trivial.

**Local search from a random start** is the most instructive failure. Take the Kernighan-Lin algorithm, which we will examine in detail later. Given a partition, it repeatedly finds the pair of vertices — one from each side — whose swap most reduces the cut, performs the swap, and continues. It is guaranteed to find a locally optimal partition with respect to single swaps. The problem is that the space of partitions has an astronomical number of local optima, and a random start lands in one of the bad ones. Local search is excellent at polishing a good partition and nearly useless at escaping a bad one. Its search radius is one swap; it cannot see that a globally better configuration requires moving fifty vertices through an intermediate state that is worse.

The multilevel paradigm resolves exactly this tension. It gives local search a good starting point by making the initial problem small enough that global structure is visible, then progressively restores detail while local search cleans up the damage. The next sections work through the two local search algorithms that do the polishing, and then the coarsening machinery that makes them effective at scale.

## Kernighan-Lin: Pair Swaps And The Best Prefix

Kernighan-Lin (KL) is the canonical refinement algorithm for balanced bisection. It starts with a partition of `V` into two sides `A` and `B`, both already within the balance tolerance. For a vertex `v` in `A`, define its gain `g(v)` as the total weight of edges from `v` to `B` minus the total weight of edges from `v` to `A`. For a vertex in `B`, define the gain symmetrically: weight to `A` minus weight to `B`. A positive gain means that moving `v` across the boundary would reduce the cut by that amount. Moving `v` alone changes the cut by `-g(v)`.

The algorithm does not move vertices one at a time. It moves them in pairs. For `u` in `A` and `v` in `B`, the gain of swapping the two vertices is

```
gain(u, v) = g(u) + g(v) - 2 * c(u, v)
```

where `c(u, v)` is the edge weight if `u` and `v` are adjacent and zero otherwise. The subtraction term corrects a double count. Moving `u` alone would remove the edge `(u, v)` from the cut, and moving `v` alone would remove it again, but swapping both leaves the edge cut because the endpoints have merely exchanged sides. The `-2 * c(u, v)` term restores the truth.

A KL pass works as follows. All vertices start unlocked. At each step, examine every unlocked pair consisting of one vertex in `A` and one in `B`, compute the swap gain, and choose the pair with the largest gain. The chosen pair is swapped and both vertices are locked. Locking prevents a vertex from moving more than once in the same pass, which is what keeps the pass from cycling. After the swap, the gains of the neighbors of the two moved vertices change, so those gains are recomputed. The pass continues until every vertex is locked or until no remaining pair satisfies the balance constraint.

The important detail is what happens to the sequence of swaps. The pass records the cumulative gain after each swap. That cumulative gain rises and falls as the pass proceeds. The algorithm does not necessarily keep the final configuration. It keeps the prefix of the swap sequence with the highest cumulative gain, then reverts every swap after that prefix. This is the best-prefix rule, and it is why KL can escape a local optimum. A pass may accept a negative-gain swap if later swaps recover more than was lost. The best prefix captures the recovery without forcing the algorithm to end in a worse state.

KL is a local search, but its neighborhood is larger than single-vertex moves. It can make coordinated changes: a vertex that is bad to move alone may be good to move together with a partner. That coordination is valuable near a boundary where two clusters are interleaved. The cost is that the neighborhood is quadratic in the size of the boundary. A naive pass scans all pairs at every step, which is `O(n^2)` for a bisection and worse when gains must be recomputed incrementally. KL is therefore rarely used directly on large graphs. It survives as a refinement step at the coarsest levels of a multilevel partitioner, where the graph has a few hundred vertices, and as a conceptual baseline for understanding what refinement is trying to do.

KL also has structural limitations. It is defined for two-way partitioning. Extending it to `k` parts requires either recursive bisection or pair swaps between arbitrary pairs of parts, and the latter becomes expensive quickly. It assumes that the balance constraint can be maintained by pair swaps, which is not always true when vertex weights are uneven. And its best-prefix rule depends on a single pass having a meaningful cumulative-gain trajectory; on very irregular graphs the trajectory can be noisy and the best prefix may be only marginally better than the starting point. Those limitations are why production partitioners use a different refinement algorithm as their workhorse.

## Fiduccia-Mattheyses: Single Moves, Buckets, And k-Way Refinement

Fiduccia-Mattheyses (FM) refinement generalizes the same best-prefix idea to `k`-way partitioning and to hypergraphs. Instead of swapping pairs, FM moves one vertex at a time. For a vertex `v` in part `A`, consider moving it to part `B`. In the graph case, the gain of that move is the total weight of edges from `v` to `B` minus the total weight of edges from `v` to `A`. Edges to other parts remain cut either way and do not affect the gain. The move changes the cut by `-gain`. In the hypergraph case, the gain is computed from the connectivity metric: for each hyperedge containing `v`, moving `v` can remove one part from the hyperedge's part set, add one part, or do neither. The contribution is positive when the move removes a part and does not add one, negative when it adds a part and does not remove one, and zero otherwise.

FM maintains a priority queue of possible moves keyed by gain. In the `k`-way version, each vertex has up to `k - 1` candidate destinations, so the queue contains move records rather than just vertices. The highest-gain move that respects the balance constraint is selected. The vertex is moved, locked, and the gains of its neighbors are updated. The pass continues until all vertices are locked or no valid move remains. As in KL, the algorithm records the cumulative gain and reverts to the best prefix at the end of the pass. Negative-gain moves are allowed during the pass because they may lead to a better prefix later.

The balance constraint is handled explicitly. A move is valid only if it does not push the destination part above the upper weight bound or the source part below the lower bound. In practice, partitioners often relax this slightly: they allow a move that violates balance by the smallest amount if no strictly valid move exists, then repair the imbalance in a later pass. The part weights are maintained incrementally, and the queue is often implemented as an array of buckets indexed by integer gain. If edge weights are integers and bounded, bucket queues make each pass `O(E)` rather than `O(E log V)`. That constant-factor difference is why FM is the default refinement kernel in METIS, Scotch, and KaHIP.

FM is weaker than KL in one sense: it cannot move two vertices together in a single step. A pair swap that is beneficial may require two separate moves, and the intermediate state may have a worse cut. The best-prefix rule can sometimes traverse that intermediate state, but not always. FM compensates by being much cheaper and by handling `k`-way partitions and hypergraphs directly. It is also easier to parallelize because each vertex's gain depends only on its local neighborhood. The usual pattern is to run several FM passes, stop when the best prefix shows no improvement, and rely on the multilevel structure to provide a starting partition that is close enough for single-vertex moves to be effective.

Neither KL nor FM is an initial partitioner. Given a random assignment, both converge to a local optimum that is usually poor. Their strength is polishing a partition that already has the right global structure. That is the gap the multilevel paradigm fills: it produces a coarse problem small enough that a good initial partition is cheap to find, then uses FM or KL to repair the projection at each finer level. The coarsening step that makes this possible is the next piece of the machinery.

## Heavy-Edge Matching: Coarsening That Preserves Structure

Coarsening takes a graph `G_i` and produces a smaller graph `G_{i+1}` by contracting disjoint edges. A matching `M` is a set of edges with no shared endpoints. For every edge in `M`, its two endpoints are merged into a single supervertex. Vertices not in `M` carry over unchanged. The weight of a supervertex is the sum of the weights of the vertices it contains. The weight of an edge between two supervertices is the sum of all original edge weights between their constituent vertices. Edges that become self-loops are discarded.

The key invariant is that a partition of the coarse graph induces a partition of the fine graph with the same cut and the same balance. Each fine vertex inherits the part of its supervertex. An edge between two supervertices represents all fine edges between the two clusters, so the cut contribution is preserved exactly. This is why coarsening is safe: solving the coarse problem is not an approximation of a different problem. It is a compression of the same problem, with the fine-level decisions temporarily hidden.

A matching should be maximal, meaning no additional edge can be added without sharing an endpoint. It need not be maximum. Maximal matchings are cheap to compute greedily, and a maximal matching already reduces the vertex count by a constant factor. The coarsening ratio is the ratio of coarse vertices to fine vertices. A ratio near 0.5 means each contraction removes about half the vertices. Coarsening stops when the graph is small enough for an expensive initial partitioner, typically a few hundred vertices, or when the ratio becomes too close to 1 because the graph has many isolated or unmatched vertices.

Heavy-edge matching (HEM) is the standard greedy matching heuristic. Vertices are visited in random order. If a vertex is still unmatched, it examines its unmatched neighbors and selects the one connected by the heaviest edge. The two are matched. The random order matters because it prevents the matching from being biased by vertex numbering or by the order in which the graph was stored. The heaviest-edge rule matters because edge weight is a proxy for coupling. Contracting a heavy edge merges vertices that are strongly connected, which tends to keep clusters intact and preserve the community structure that the partitioner will later exploit. Contracting a light edge merges vertices that barely interact, which blurs cluster boundaries and makes the coarse graph a worse guide.

On an unweighted original graph, every edge initially has weight 1, so the first level of HEM is mostly a random maximal matching with tie-breaking. The heavy-edge advantage appears after the first contraction. Once supervertices exist, an edge between two supervertices has weight equal to the number of original edges between the two clusters. Heavy edges now identify pairs of clusters that are densely connected. Contracting those pairs is exactly what preserves the large-scale structure while shrinking the graph. HEM is `O(E)` per level, simple to implement, and surprisingly effective. It is not guaranteed to minimize any objective, but it produces coarse graphs on which FM refinement behaves well.

HEM has failure modes. On power-law graphs, a high-degree hub has many neighbors, and the greedy rule may match the hub to a leaf because the edge weight is high relative to the leaf's other options. The resulting supervertex can have enormous weight, which distorts balance and makes later refinement harder. Degree-aware matching, vertex-weight penalties, and normalized heavy-edge scores are common fixes. On hypergraphs, coarsening is more delicate: matching two vertices merges their incident hyperedges, hyperedges can shrink, and parallel hyperedges must be merged with summed weights. The same principle applies, but the bookkeeping is heavier. Alternatives such as random matching, light-edge matching for specific objectives, and algebraic-distance matching exist, but HEM remains the default because its cost is low and its structural bias is usually helpful.

With coarsening and refinement in place, the multilevel loop becomes concrete. The graph is contracted level by level until it is small. An initial partition is computed there. The partition is projected back one level at a time, and FM or KL refines each projection. Most vertices are already in a reasonable part after projection, so refinement only has to fix local damage near the new boundaries. That is the economy of the multilevel idea: expensive global search happens once on a tiny graph, and cheap local search happens many times on large graphs.

## Initial Partitioning: The Coarsest Level Matters More Than You Think

The multilevel loop's final cut is bounded by the quality of the initial partition. If the coarsest graph is split badly, refinement at finer levels can only repair so much. The coarsest graph is typically a few hundred vertices, which is small enough that algorithms considered impractical on the full graph become affordable. Most partitioners run a portfolio of initial partitioning heuristics and keep the best result.

The simplest is random assignment, which is used mainly as a baseline. Greedy graph growing (GGP) is the workhorse. It picks a random seed vertex, then repeatedly adds the vertex with the highest gain — the number of edges to the growing region minus the number of edges to the unassigned remainder — until the region reaches its target weight. A new seed is chosen in the unassigned vertices, and the process repeats until all vertices are assigned. GGP is fast and produces connected, compact regions on meshes, but it is sensitive to the seed and can leave thin tendrils on irregular graphs. Running it many times with different seeds and keeping the best partition is standard.

Spectral bisection is the other classical option. It computes the Fiedler vector — the eigenvector associated with the second-smallest eigenvalue of the graph Laplacian — and splits vertices at the median. On a coarsest graph of a few hundred vertices, the eigenvector computation is trivial, and the resulting partition often captures global structure better than GGP. The catch is that spectral methods are sensitive to vertex weights and can produce unbalanced parts when weights vary widely. Some partitioners use spectral bisection only for the first few levels of recursive bisection, then switch to GGP.

For `k`-way initial partitioning, there are two families. Recursive bisection applies a bisection heuristic repeatedly, splitting each part in two until `k` parts exist. Direct `k`-way methods assign vertices to all `k` parts simultaneously, often by growing `k` regions in parallel. Direct methods are usually better because they avoid the myopic decisions of recursive bisection, but they are harder to implement and can be slower on the coarsest graph. On very small graphs, exact methods such as branch-and-bound or integer programming become viable, and a few partitioners use them when `k` is small. The gain from an exact initial partition is real but modest — typically a fraction of a percent in final cut — because refinement at finer levels washes out most of the difference.

The coarsest graph is also where the balance constraint is easiest to satisfy. Vertex weights on the coarsest graph are sums of many fine-level weights, so they are large and the relative imbalance introduced by a single move is small. This is why initial partitioners can afford to be aggressive about cut minimization and leave balance repair to refinement.

## Parallel Multilevel Partitioning

When the graph no longer fits in one machine's memory, the multilevel loop must be distributed. Every stage changes character. Parallel coarsening computes a matching across processors. Each processor matches its local vertices greedily, but edges that cross processor boundaries require communication. A common pattern is to let each processor propose matches for its boundary vertices, exchange proposals, and resolve conflicts with a deterministic rule such as lowest global vertex ID. The resulting matching is maximal within each processor but may leave some boundary vertices unmatched. That is acceptable: a few unmatched vertices do not hurt the coarsening ratio much, and forcing a perfect matching would require expensive global synchronization.

Parallel initial partitioning is usually easy because the coarsest graph is small enough to gather onto a single processor. If the coarsest graph is still too large — which can happen when the original graph is enormous and the coarsening ratio is conservative — the partitioner falls back to parallel recursive bisection or a distributed greedy heuristic. The coarsest graph is also where communication is cheapest, so gathering is often the pragmatic choice.

Parallel refinement is the hard part. FM refinement is inherently sequential: it locks vertices, selects the globally best move, and reverts to the best prefix. Parallelizing FM directly requires either speculative execution — each processor proposes moves, then a global reduction picks the best non-conflicting subset — or relaxed locking, where multiple moves proceed concurrently and conflicts are repaired afterward. Both approaches add synchronization and can degrade cut quality. A common alternative is to use a refinement algorithm that is naturally parallel, such as label propagation or diffusion. Label propagation assigns each vertex the most common label among its neighbors, subject to balance constraints. It is fast, local, and easy to distribute, but it can oscillate and does not have the best-prefix guarantee of FM. Diffusion moves vertices along boundaries in small steps, using only local information. It converges more slowly than FM but parallelizes cleanly.

Production parallel partitioners — ParMETIS, PT-Scotch, and the parallel mode of KaHIP — combine these ideas. They use parallel heavy-edge matching for coarsening, a serial or semi-serial initial partitioner on the coarsest graph, and a parallel refinement that is a hybrid of FM and label propagation. The cut quality is typically 5–15% worse than the best serial partitioner on the same graph, but the parallel version can handle graphs with billions of edges that would never fit on one node. The trade-off is worth it when partitioning time is a significant fraction of total job time, which is common for very large graphs.

## Streaming Graph Partitioning

Streaming partitioning abandons the multilevel paradigm entirely. Vertices arrive one at a time, in an arbitrary order, and each must be assigned to a part immediately. The algorithm never revisits a decision, and it cannot store the full graph. Memory is limited to the part weights and a small amount of state per vertex. The objective is still to minimize edge cut subject to balance, but the constraints are severe: one pass, `O(1)` or `O(degree)` work per vertex, and no global view.

The simplest streaming algorithm is random assignment: hash the vertex ID to a part. It is perfectly balanced and `O(1)`, but the cut is close to the worst case — roughly `1 - 1/k` of all edges are cut. Greedy assignment improves on this. For each arriving vertex, count how many of its already-assigned neighbors are in each part, and assign it to the part with the most neighbors, subject to a balance constraint. This is the streaming analogue of greedy graph growing, and it produces cuts that are dramatically better than random. The weakness is that high-degree vertices — hubs — can dominate the count and pull many of their neighbors into the same part, creating imbalance. A balance penalty is needed.

Linear Deterministic Greedy (LDG) adds that penalty. The score for assigning vertex `v` to part `i` is

```
score(v, i) = |N(v) ∩ part i| - penalty * W(i)
```

where `W(i)` is the current weight of part `i` and `penalty` is a tunable parameter. The vertex is assigned to the part with the highest score. The penalty term grows as a part fills, which prevents any part from becoming too large. LDG is simple, fast, and surprisingly effective. Fennel is a refinement of the same idea with a different penalty function:

```
score(v, i) = |N(v) ∩ part i| - gamma * (W(i))^(alpha - 1)
```

where `gamma` and `alpha` control the balance–cut trade-off. Fennel’s penalty is derived from a theoretical analysis of the expected cut, and it often outperforms LDG on power-law graphs. Both algorithms require only the part weights and the neighbor counts, so memory is `O(k)` plus the graph stream.

Streaming partitioners produce cuts that are typically 1.5–2× worse than offline multilevel partitioners on the same graph. That gap is the price of one-pass, bounded-memory operation. For graphs that are too large to store, or for applications where partitioning must happen on the fly — dynamic graphs, continuous ingestion pipelines, or distributed graph processing systems that receive edges as a stream — the trade-off is often acceptable. Some systems use a hybrid approach: stream the graph to get a rough partition, then run a limited number of offline refinement passes on a sampled subgraph. Restreaming algorithms make a second pass over the vertices, using the first pass’s partition as a starting point, and can improve cut quality by 10–20% at the cost of a second pass.

## Partitioning Power-Law Graphs

Power-law graphs break the standard edge-cut objective. A single hub vertex can have degree in the millions, and every edge incident to that hub that crosses a part boundary contributes to the cut. Minimizing edge cut tends to place hubs in one part, which overloads that part and forces other vertices to be distributed unevenly. The result is a partition with a low edge cut but terrible balance, or a balanced partition with a huge cut. Neither is good.

The root cause is that edge cut weights every edge equally, but in a power-law graph the edges incident to a hub are not equally important. If the application communicates per vertex — for example, a vertex-centric graph processing system where each vertex sends one message per superstep — then the right metric is communication volume, not edge cut. Communication volume counts the number of distinct remote parts a vertex communicates with, not the number of edges. A hub with a million neighbors in one remote part contributes 1 to communication volume, not a million. Minimizing communication volume instead of edge cut changes the optimal partition completely.

An alternative is vertex-cut partitioning. Instead of assigning vertices to parts, assign edges to parts. A vertex is replicated to every part that contains one of its edges. The cost is the total number of vertex replicas, or the maximum replication factor. Vertex-cut is the model used by GraphX, PowerGraph, and other distributed graph processing systems. It handles power-law graphs gracefully because a hub can be replicated across many parts without forcing all its edges into one part. The trade-off is that vertex-cut requires more storage for replicas and more complex communication patterns, but for power-law graphs it is often the only way to get good balance and low communication.

For edge-cut partitioners, degree-aware matching and refinement can help. Heavy-edge matching on a power-law graph may match a hub to a leaf, creating a supervertex with enormous weight. Degree-aware matching penalizes matches that involve high-degree vertices, or normalizes the edge weight by the degrees of the endpoints. Refinement can use a gain function that discounts edges incident to hubs, or it can move hubs to separate parts and then balance the remaining vertices. These fixes are ad hoc but effective in practice. The deeper lesson is that the objective function must match the application’s communication pattern. Edge cut is the right objective for some applications and the wrong one for others, and no amount of algorithmic sophistication can fix a mismatched objective.

## Dynamic and Incremental Partitioning

Real graphs change. Edges are added and removed, vertices appear and disappear, and the optimal partition drifts. Repartitioning from scratch after every change is prohibitively expensive, and it causes massive data migration: every vertex that changes parts must be moved across the network. Incremental partitioning tries to adjust the partition locally, moving only vertices near the changes.

The simplest incremental approach is to leave the partition alone and let it degrade. This is what many systems do in practice: partition once, then run with the same partition until the imbalance or cut becomes unacceptable. The degradation is usually slow for graphs with stable structure, but it can be fast for graphs with bursty updates. A better approach is to run a local refinement pass after a batch of changes. The refinement uses the same FM or label propagation machinery as the multilevel loop, but it is restricted to vertices within a few hops of the changed edges. This keeps the migration cost low while repairing the worst damage.

A more principled approach is to formulate incremental partitioning as a multi-objective problem: minimize cut plus a migration cost. The migration cost is the total weight of vertices that change parts, or the number of edges that cross a part boundary before and after. The trade-off is controlled by a parameter that says how much cut improvement is worth a unit of migration. When the graph changes slowly, the optimal policy is to repartition rarely and tolerate some cut degradation. When the graph changes rapidly, more frequent repartitioning is worth the migration cost. The optimal frequency depends on the rate of change, the cost of migration, and the sensitivity of the application to cut quality. There is no universal answer, which is why most systems expose a tuning knob.

Incremental partitioning is especially important for streaming graph processing systems, where the graph is never fully materialized and updates arrive continuously. In that setting, the partitioner and the application run concurrently, and the partitioner must keep up with the update rate. This is an active area of research, and the algorithms are still evolving.

## Inside the Multilevel Control Loop

A production multilevel partitioner is less a single algorithm than a small scheduler with a hierarchy of representations. The coarsening phase produces an array of graphs `G_0, G_1, ..., G_L`, where `G_0` is the input and `G_L` is the coarsest graph. The partitioner keeps every level in memory, or recomputes levels if memory is tight, because the uncoarsening phase needs the exact contraction mapping that produced each level. That mapping is usually stored as a parent array: each fine vertex records the coarse vertex that absorbed it. Projecting a partition upward is then a single gather, and the cost is linear in the number of fine vertices.

The stopping rule is worth tuning explicitly. A common default is to stop when the coarse graph has fewer than a few hundred vertices, or when the contraction ratio falls below about 1.1. The first condition bounds the cost of the initial partitioner. The second prevents wasted work on levels that barely shrink the graph. On irregular graphs, a maximal matching can leave many vertices unmatched, so the ratio may stall well above 1.5 while the vertex count is still large. A good scheduler detects that and switches to a different matching strategy or accepts a smaller contraction and continues. Stopping too early leaves the initial partitioner with a graph that is too large for a global heuristic. Stopping too late produces a coarsest graph so small that its structure is an artifact of the matching rather than a summary of the input.

Most partitioners also use more than one initial partition. The coarsest graph is cheap enough that running three to ten trials of greedy graph growing, spectral bisection, or randomized greedy is affordable. Each trial is projected upward independently, refined, and scored. Keeping the best final partition is a form of portfolio selection, and it reduces the variance of the output substantially. The trials should differ in more than their random seed: varying the initial partitioner, the balance penalty, and the order in which regions are grown gives a more diverse set of starting points than repeated runs of the same heuristic.

The uncoarsening phase is not a straight line. A useful extension is the V-cycle, borrowed from multigrid methods. After refining a level, the partitioner optionally re-coarsens that level with the current partition as a constraint, solves the smaller problem again, and projects back. The re-coarsening is restricted so that it does not merge vertices from different parts, which preserves the partition while exposing new local structure. A V-cycle can repair mistakes made at a coarse level that plain refinement cannot see because the fine-level graph no longer contains the relevant adjacency. The cost is additional coarsening and refinement work, so production partitioners use V-cycles sparingly, often only at the coarsest few levels.

Refinement scheduling matters as much as the refinement algorithm. Running FM until it converges at every level is wasteful, because the early levels contain most of the vertices and most of the damage from projection is local. A common schedule is to run a small fixed number of FM passes at each level, check the improvement, and stop early if the best prefix does not beat the previous best by a meaningful margin. The last few levels, closest to the original graph, get more passes because errors there are most expensive. Some partitioners also interleave refinement with balance repair: after a refinement pass, they move vertices from overweight parts to underweight parts using a restricted FM pass that only considers moves that improve balance, then run a normal pass to recover cut quality.

Balance repair deserves its own pass because the balance constraint is not a soft penalty in most production settings. If a part exceeds its capacity, the application may fail outright rather than merely slow down. A partitioner that reports a beautiful cut and a 12% imbalance when the tolerance was 3% has produced an invalid answer. The repair pass should be explicit, bounded, and verified before the result is returned. A useful implementation detail is to maintain part weights in a fixed-width integer type with a known total, so that comparisons against the capacity bound are exact and do not drift with floating-point accumulation.

The control loop also needs an off-ramp for pathological inputs. A graph with many connected components, isolated vertices, or vertices whose individual weight exceeds a part's capacity cannot be partitioned under the stated constraints. The partitioner should detect infeasible vertex weights before coarsening, because otherwise the matching will produce supervertices that are impossible to place and refinement will thrash without converging. Similarly, a graph with zero-weight edges or negative weights violates the assumptions of most gain calculations and should be normalized or rejected at the API boundary.

## Hypergraph Partitioning Without the Clique Expansion

Hypergraph partitioners share the multilevel skeleton but differ in every data structure. A hypergraph is stored as a list of pins for each hyperedge and a list of incident hyperedges for each vertex. The connectivity metric is maintained as a per-hyperedge part set, usually a small array of part identifiers with a count. When a vertex moves, the partitioner updates only the hyperedges incident to that vertex. For each such hyperedge, it removes the old part if the vertex was the last pin in that part, adds the new part if the vertex is the first pin there, and recomputes the gain contribution. The gain of a move is the sum over incident hyperedges of the change in connectivity cost. A hyperedge that currently touches two parts and will touch one after the move contributes a gain of one. A hyperedge that touches one part and will touch two contributes a loss of one. Hyperedges that touch three or more parts often contribute zero because the move merely swaps which parts are present without changing the count.

This local update structure is why hypergraph FM is practical. The expensive part is finding the best move, not evaluating a single move. Bucket queues indexed by integer gain work the same way as in the graph case, but the number of candidate moves per vertex is larger because a vertex can move to any part currently present in one of its incident hyperedges. In a `k`-way partition with large `k`, that candidate list can be long. Implementations prune it by considering only parts that appear in the vertex's neighborhood, and by using a lazy priority queue that skips stale entries rather than updating them eagerly.

Coarsening a hypergraph is more delicate than coarsening a graph. Matching two vertices merges their incident hyperedges. If both vertices belong to the same hyperedge, that hyperedge loses a pin and may shrink from size three to size two, or from size two to a self-loop that is discarded. If they belong to different hyperedges, those hyperedges may merge if they now have the same pin set. The coarse hypergraph therefore needs a canonicalization step: duplicate hyperedges are merged with summed weights, singleton hyperedges are dropped, and pins are deduplicated. The heavy-edge score for a pair of vertices is usually the sum of the weights of the hyperedges that contain both vertices, normalized by hyperedge size so that large hyperedges do not dominate the matching. Without that normalization, a single huge hyperedge would make every pair of its vertices look strongly connected, and the matching would collapse the entire hyperedge into one supervertex.

Fixed vertices are another hypergraph-specific concern. Many applications require certain vertices to remain in designated parts: boundary conditions in a mesh, pinned operators in a computation graph, or vertices with external storage affinity. A fixed vertex cannot move during refinement, and coarsening must not merge two fixed vertices that belong to different parts. The matching therefore treats fixed vertices as constraints, and the initial partitioner must respect them. Feasibility can fail even when every individual weight is small, because the fixed vertices may already consume more than the capacity of a part. A robust partitioner checks this before spending time on coarsening.

The connectivity metric also changes how tie-breaking should work. Two moves with the same gain can have very different effects on the future. A move that empties a part from a hyperedge reduces the part set permanently; a move that merely replaces one part with another leaves the connectivity count unchanged. Preferring moves that reduce the number of distinct parts, rather than merely preserving the count, tends to produce cleaner boundaries. Some implementations add a small secondary score based on the number of hyperedges that become single-part, so that the refinement favors moves that simplify the cut structure even when the immediate gain is zero.

## Balance Is a Vector, Not a Scalar

Most descriptions of partitioning collapse balance to a single number: the weight of a part divided by the average part weight. Real applications rarely have a single weight. A vertex may consume memory, compute time, communication bandwidth, and accelerator capacity in different proportions. A part that is under the memory bound may be over the compute bound, and a partition that is balanced in one dimension can be badly unbalanced in another.

Multi-constraint partitioning treats each vertex as a weight vector and each part as a capacity vector. A partition is feasible only if every component of every part's load is below its corresponding capacity. The feasible region is a multidimensional box intersected with the discrete assignment constraints, and it can be empty even when a single-constraint relaxation would be feasible. This is not a corner case. Heterogeneous clusters, mixed CPU-GPU ranks, and applications with per-rank memory limits all produce capacity vectors that differ across parts. A partitioner that assumes identical capacities will either violate a constraint or leave capacity unused.

The algorithmic consequence is that gain comparisons are no longer total orders. A move can improve one constraint while worsening another, and the notion of a best move depends on which constraint is currently binding. Production multi-constraint partitioners use a scalarized score for the priority queue, often a weighted sum of normalized gains, and then repair the constraints explicitly. Normalization is essential: memory measured in gigabytes and compute measured in milliseconds cannot be added without scaling. A common approach is to divide each component by its part capacity, so that a move that consumes 1% of the remaining memory is comparable to a move that consumes 1% of the remaining compute budget.

Scalarization has a known failure mode. A move that is slightly positive in the weighted score but pushes a nearly full part over capacity should be rejected, not merely penalized. The queue must therefore support hard feasibility filters, not just soft penalties. When no feasible move exists, the partitioner should relax the least important constraint, record that it did so, and continue. Reporting which constraint was relaxed is valuable diagnostics: it tells the user that the requested balance vector was infeasible and that the result is a best-effort compromise rather than a silent violation.

Balance also interacts with the cut objective in a way that single-number summaries hide. Two partitions can have the same maximum part weight but very different distributions. One may have all parts near the limit, leaving no room for refinement; another may have one part at the limit and the rest comfortably below. The first is fragile because any subsequent vertex weight increase makes it infeasible. The second has slack that can be traded for cut quality. A partition quality report should include the full sorted vector of part weights, not just the maximum and the mean, because the shape of that vector predicts how much the partition can tolerate growth.

## Reproducibility, Determinism, and Parallel Nondeterminism

Partitioning is often used as a preprocessing step whose output is cached, compared across runs, and used to reproduce performance results. Nondeterminism is therefore a practical problem, not an academic one. A partitioner that produces a different partition on every run makes it impossible to tell whether a performance change came from the application or from a luckier cut. It also makes debugging distributed applications miserable, because the communication pattern changes between runs.

Serial partitioners can be made deterministic relatively easily. The matching order should be derived from a hash of stable vertex identifiers rather than from the order in which vertices happen to be stored. Tie-breaking in the priority queue should use the vertex identifier as a secondary key. Random seeds should be explicit parameters, not drawn from the clock. Floating-point gain calculations should be avoided where integer weights are available, because the order of additions can change the result even in a single-threaded program. With those rules, the same input and the same parameters produce the same partition on the same binary.

Parallel partitioners are harder. The matching phase may propose conflicting matches from different processors, and the resolution rule must be deterministic. A common rule is to prefer the match with the lowest global vertex identifier, or the highest edge weight followed by the lowest identifier. The refinement phase is worse: parallel FM selects moves concurrently, and the set of moves that are accepted can depend on thread scheduling. Relaxed locking makes this explicit, but it also means that the final partition depends on timing. Deterministic parallel refinement usually requires a fixed reduction order, such as rounds in which each processor proposes one move and a global coordinator selects a non-conflicting subset. That adds synchronization but restores reproducibility.

There is a real trade-off here. Deterministic parallel partitioning is slower and may produce slightly worse cuts because it cannot exploit opportunistic concurrent moves. Some systems expose a deterministic mode for benchmarking and a fast mode for production. Others report the distribution of cut quality over several runs rather than a single number, which is the honest thing to do when nondeterminism is unavoidable. If a paper or a vendor reports a single cut value for a parallel partitioner, it is worth asking how many runs that value represents and whether the variance was measured.

Reproducibility also depends on stable identifiers. If the graph is relabeled between runs, even a deterministic partitioner will produce a different partition because the tie-breaking and matching order change. Relabeling can happen when a graph is loaded from a distributed file system, when vertices are renumbered by a preprocessing step, or when a streaming system assigns IDs from a hash. The partitioner cannot fix this on its own. Applications that need comparable partitions across runs should persist the vertex labeling or use a canonical labeling step before partitioning.

## Quality Metrics That Travel With the Graph

A cut number without context is not a quality report. The same cut value can represent a good partition for one application and a bad one for another, depending on which edges are cut, how the cut is distributed across parts, and what the application does with a boundary vertex. A useful partition report includes several metrics that travel with the graph and can be compared across partitioners.

The first is the normalized cut: total cut weight divided by total edge weight. It is easy to compare across graphs of different sizes, but it hides the balance constraint. A partitioner that ignores balance can always lower the normalized cut by putting most vertices in one part. The report should therefore pair normalized cut with the maximum part weight as a fraction of capacity, and with the number of parts that are within tolerance. A partition that is 2% better in cut but violates balance on three parts is not an improvement.

The second is the distribution of cut contributions across parts. A partition with a low total cut but one part that accounts for half of all cut edges is fragile: that part is a communication hotspot, and the application's slowest rank will dominate. The per-part cut vector reveals this. It can also reveal imbalance in the boundary structure even when the part weights are balanced. A part with few vertices but many boundary edges may be computationally cheap and communication-expensive.

The third is boundary size. Count the number of vertices that have at least one neighbor in another part. That number predicts the size of the halo or ghost region in a distributed computation, which in turn predicts memory overhead and the cost of exchanging boundary data. Two partitions with the same edge cut can have very different boundary vertex counts, especially on graphs with high-degree vertices. A partition that cuts many edges incident to a few hubs may have a small boundary vertex set; a partition that cuts one edge from each of many low-degree vertices may have a much larger one.

The fourth is the part adjacency graph. Build a graph whose vertices are parts and whose edges connect parts that share at least one cut edge, weighted by the number of shared edges. The density and degree distribution of this graph predict how many distinct messages each part must send per iteration. A sparse part adjacency graph with low maximum degree is often better than a lower-cut partition with a dense adjacency graph, because message count and synchronization cost depend on the number of neighbors, not just the total bytes. This metric is especially important for bulk-synchronous applications, where every part must wait for all of its neighbors at every superstep.

The fifth is the migration footprint, relevant whenever the partition replaces an existing assignment. Count the number of vertices that change parts and the total weight moved. A new partition with a better cut but a huge migration cost may be worse for a long-running job than a slightly worse partition that moves almost nothing. The right comparison is not cut versus cut but total cost, including the one-time migration and the recurring communication savings. That comparison requires knowing how long the job will run, which the partitioner usually does not know. The report should provide the migration numbers and let the application decide.

Finally, quality reports should include the partitioner's own runtime and memory peak. A partition that is 5% better but takes an hour longer to compute is not obviously better for a job that runs for two hours. Including partitioner cost in the same report as cut quality makes the trade-off visible and prevents the common mistake of optimizing the preprocessing step in isolation.

## Tuning Knobs and Failure Modes

Most partitioner tuning is about recognizing a small set of failure modes. The first is matching collapse: coarsening produces a few supervertices with enormous weight and many tiny ones. The symptom is a coarsest graph whose weight distribution is extremely skewed, followed by refinement that cannot satisfy balance without destroying the cut. The cause is usually a matching rule that over-rewards heavy edges without normalizing by vertex weight or degree. The fix is to cap supervertex weight, penalize matches that would create a vertex above a threshold, or switch to a degree-aware matching rule on the offending levels.

The second is refinement thrash: FM passes keep accepting moves, the cumulative gain oscillates, and the best prefix improves by almost nothing. This often happens when the balance constraint is very tight and the graph is nearly full. Each move to improve cut pushes a part over capacity, and the repair move undoes the gain. The fix is to relax the balance tolerance slightly, run refinement on a subset of boundary vertices, or separate cut improvement from balance repair into distinct passes with different acceptance criteria.

The third is hidden over-refinement. Running many FM passes at every level can produce a slightly better cut but a much longer partitioner runtime, and the extra passes may move hubs in ways that increase boundary vertex count without reducing cut. Monitoring both cut and boundary size catches this. If additional passes do not improve either metric, stop them.

The fourth is an initial partition that is too good for the wrong graph. A spectral initial partitioner on the coarsest graph can produce a split that looks excellent in cut but is geometrically arbitrary, with parts that are interleaved at fine levels. Refinement then spends many passes untangling the boundary. Greedy graph growing often produces less impressive coarse cuts but more coherent regions that refine faster. The best initial partitioner depends on the graph family, which is why portfolios exist.

The fifth is capacity infeasibility that is only discovered late. If the coarsest graph contains a vertex heavier than a part's capacity, no amount of refinement will fix it. Check individual vertex weights before coarsening, check supervertex weights during coarsening, and fail early with a clear message. Silent best-effort partitioning in this situation produces a result that looks valid in the cut report but violates the application's hard constraint.

A practical tuning strategy is to start with the partitioner's defaults, measure the full quality report rather than just the cut, and change one knob at a time. The imbalance tolerance has the largest effect on cut quality and should be set from the application's actual tolerance, not from a desire for a prettier number. The coarsening threshold and number of initial partition trials have the next largest effect. Refinement parameters usually matter less, provided the partitioner is allowed enough passes to repair projection damage. Matching parameters matter most on irregular graphs and least on meshes with uniform degree. Keeping the quality report, the parameter set, and the partitioner runtime together turns tuning from guesswork into a controlled experiment.

When a partitioner reports only a cut number, ask what else it measured. The answer usually tells you whether the result is relevant to your application or merely a locally optimal artifact of the wrong objective.
