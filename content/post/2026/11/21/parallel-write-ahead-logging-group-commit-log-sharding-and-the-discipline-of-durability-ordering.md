# Parallel Write-Ahead Logging: Group Commit, Log Sharding, and the Discipline of Durability Ordering

A write-ahead log is usually introduced as a simple idea: append a description of a change before applying it, then replay that description after a crash. The simplicity is deceptive. In a transactional system, the WAL is also a serialization point, a durability boundary, a recovery format, and often the highest-contention component in the write path. When a system needs more write throughput, engineers naturally ask whether the log can be parallelized. The answer is yes, but only along specific axes, and each axis introduces a new failure mode.

This article examines parallel WAL design for storage engines and replicated databases. It starts with single-node mechanics, then extends the same reasoning to sharded logs and distributed commit. The assumptions are conventional: crash-stop failures, a local block device or filesystem that honors flush operations, checksummed records, and a recovery process that tolerates torn writes at the tail. We are not assuming Byzantine faults, and we are not assuming that `write()` is durable. We are assuming that the durability contract is explicit and testable.

The central claim is that a WAL can be parallelized in its data path, but not in its ordering contract. Producers may format records concurrently, writers may issue I/O concurrently, and flushers may batch many transactions behind one device flush. What cannot be parallelized away is the need for a total order of log records, a contiguous durable prefix, and a commit visibility rule that never acknowledges a transaction whose commit record is not durably ordered before the data it protects.

## What Parallelism Can and Cannot Change in a WAL

It helps to separate four independent axes. They are often conflated under the phrase 'parallel WAL', which is why designs that appear similar can have very different correctness properties.

The first axis is record formation. A transaction can compute its redo records, checksums, and commit metadata on multiple threads before any ordering is imposed. This is the easiest form of parallelism because it does not touch the log order. It can reduce CPU cost on large records or checksummed payloads, but it does not reduce the cost of the append point or the flush.

The second axis is reservation. A log needs a total order, so each record needs a position or log sequence number. That position can be assigned by an atomic counter, by a dedicated sequencer thread, or by a per-shard counter when the log is partitioned. Reservation is a small critical section, but it defines the order that recovery will replay. If reservation order and physical write order diverge, the system must track completion so that it never publishes a durable position beyond a hole.

The third axis is physical write. Once positions are assigned, different writers can write different records to different offsets, or to different files, concurrently. This is where most throughput gains live on modern storage. It is also where subtle bugs appear: two threads writing into the same block, checksum fields published before payloads, or a flush issued while a later write is still in flight.

The fourth axis is flush and acknowledgment. A commit is visible only after its commit record is durable, and durability usually means a device or filesystem flush. Parallelism here is mostly batching: many commit records can share one flush. That is group commit. It trades latency for throughput and changes the shape of the tail latency distribution, but it does not remove the need for a durable prefix.

These axes are not equivalent. Formatting records in parallel is safe by construction because it precedes ordering. Reserving positions in parallel requires a total order. Writing in parallel requires completion tracking. Flushing in parallel requires a barrier discipline. A design that accelerates the first three but leaves the fourth as a single synchronous `fsync` per transaction will still be limited by the flush. A design that batches flushes but lets writers publish out of order may acknowledge transactions whose predecessors are not durable, which is a correctness failure, not a performance trade-off.

## The Durable Prefix Is the Real Contract

A WAL has a logical order. Every record carries an LSN, and recovery replays records in increasing LSN order. The log also has a physical layout: segments, blocks, offsets, and checksums. The two orders do not have to match instant by instant, but the system must be able to identify a contiguous prefix of records that is known durable. That prefix is the authority for recovery and for commit visibility.

Suppose a producer reserves LSN 100, then stalls before writing. Another producer reserves LSN 101 and completes its write immediately. The device may now contain record 101 but not record 100. A naive flusher that asks the device to flush everything and then advances a durable position to 101 creates a hole: recovery sees 101 without its predecessor. If record 100 was part of the same transaction, or if 101's commit depends on 100's page change, the log is no longer a faithful history. The correct response is to track completion and advance the durable frontier only to the largest LSN such that every record up to that LSN is present and flushed.

A completion bitmap is one practical mechanism. Each reserved slot has a state: empty, written, or durable. Writers mark slots written after publishing the record bytes and checksum. The flusher scans the bitmap from the current frontier, waiting for gaps to fill, then issues a flush that covers the contiguous prefix. After the flush completes, it marks those slots durable and publishes the new frontier. The scan must be cheap; a monotonic frontier plus a small out-of-order completion set is usually enough.

The memory ordering matters. A writer must publish the record payload and checksum before marking the slot written, otherwise a flusher could observe the written bit and flush uninitialized bytes. In languages with explicit memory models, that means a release store for the slot state and an acquire load in the flusher, or an equivalent lock. The LSN reservation counter also needs a defined order; it is a logical sequence, not a timestamp. It should not be compared across independent shards without a separate global ordering mechanism.

Another distinction is between write completion and durability. A successful `write()` or `pwrite()` usually means the bytes reached the kernel page cache, not stable storage. A successful `fsync()` or `fdatasync()` means the filesystem has flushed the relevant data and metadata according to its contract. Direct I/O and device-level flush semantics change the details, but the principle is the same: the WAL must have an explicit operation that establishes durability, and the frontier must advance only after that operation returns successfully. If the flush fails, the frontier must not move, and the affected transactions must fail or retry according to the system's error policy.

Recovery is the other half of the contract. A log reader starts at the last checkpoint, scans forward, validates checksums and record framing, and stops at the first invalid or incomplete record. It must tolerate a torn tail because a crash can interrupt a write mid-record. The durable frontier is a hint, not a substitute for validation: after a crash, the reader must still verify that records are complete and that commit records are present before replaying their effects. Recovery should be idempotent or should use a redo position that makes repeated replay safe.

## Group Commit as a Coordination Problem

Group commit is the most common form of WAL parallelism, and it is often described too casually. The usual story is that multiple transactions put their commit records into a batch, one thread calls `fsync`, and everyone waits. The real design problem is coordinating arrivals, leadership, failure, and latency policy without acknowledging a transaction too early.

A simple implementation uses a commit queue and a leader election among waiting committers. When a transaction is ready to commit, it appends its commit record and joins the current batch. One thread becomes the leader for that batch, writes the batch if needed, issues the flush, and then wakes the followers. The followers do not return success until the leader reports that the flush covered their commit records. If the leader fails before the flush, another thread must take over or the transactions must fail; they must not be acknowledged on the assumption that someone else will flush later.

The batching policy is a latency-throughput trade-off. A size-based policy waits until a batch reaches a target number of bytes or transactions, which improves throughput but can add latency when arrivals are sparse. A time-based policy waits for a short interval, which bounds added latency but may flush small batches under load. Adaptive policies switch between the two based on queue depth and recent service time. None of these policies changes the correctness rule: the acknowledgment boundary is the flush completion, not the enqueue into the batch.

Group commit also interacts with the durable prefix. If commit records are written out of order, the flusher cannot simply flush the highest LSN in the batch. It must flush through the highest contiguous LSN that includes every commit record in the batch, or it must maintain a separate commit frontier. In practice, many systems write commit records in reservation order within a batch, which simplifies the accounting but can serialize the write path. A more concurrent design uses separate write completion tracking and lets the flusher compute the prefix after writes finish.

The tail latency behavior deserves attention. Batching reduces the number of flushes, but a transaction that arrives just after a batch closes waits for the next batch. Under bursty load, the queue can grow, and the time spent waiting for a batch can dominate the flush itself. Systems often combine group commit with admission control or write coalescing to keep the queue bounded. The goal is not to maximize batch size; it is to keep flush utilization high while keeping the waiting-time distribution acceptable for the workload's durability contract.

Finally, group commit does not eliminate crash recovery concerns. A batch may contain multiple commit records, and a crash may leave a prefix of the batch durable. Recovery must replay the durable prefix and discard incomplete records. If commit records are atomic units, the format must make that atomicity detectable through length, checksum, and sequence fields. A commit record that is partially written must never be treated as a commit.

## Sharding Changes the Frontier from a Scalar to a Vector

A single log has a scalar durable frontier. A sharded log has one frontier per shard, or a vector of frontiers if the system needs to reason about cross-shard ordering. Sharding can remove a global append bottleneck, but it moves complexity into commit coordination and recovery.

If transactions are partitioned so that each transaction touches exactly one shard, per-shard logs are straightforward: each shard has its own LSN space, its own flush discipline, and its own recovery. The system still needs a routing layer and a way to map a transaction to a shard, but there is no cross-shard commit problem. This is the simplest and often the most effective use of log sharding.

Cross-shard transactions break that simplicity. A transaction that updates two shards needs a commit decision that is durable with respect to both. One option is two-phase commit with prepare records in each shard log and a commit decision recorded in a separate global log. Another is a single global commit log that orders cross-shard decisions while per-shard logs carry the data. Both options add at least one additional durability boundary per cross-shard transaction, and both make recovery depend on reconciling multiple logs. The system must be able to tell whether a prepared transaction committed, aborted, or is still in doubt, and it must do so after arbitrary crash points.

A vector frontier is not a total order. Two shards can each be durable to different points, and there may be no meaningful comparison between their LSNs unless the system has a global commit timestamp or a transaction status table. Engineers sometimes assume that a per-shard LSN can be compared globally because both counters started at zero. That assumption is false in general. Cross-shard visibility requires an explicit ordering mechanism, not an accident of identical counter initialization.

The next section turns to the concrete mechanics of a group-commit coordinator, including how to bound queue growth, how to handle flush failures, and how to test the durable-prefix invariant under injected crashes.

## Inside a Group-Commit Coordinator

A group-commit coordinator is a small state machine, but its correctness depends on every transition being explicit. The coordinator should not be a thread that merely calls fsync; it should own the mapping from reserved LSNs to flush epochs and the publication of the durable frontier. Producers interact with it through three operations: reserve, publish, and wait-for-durable. The reserve operation returns an LSN and a slot. The publish operation marks the slot as written after the record bytes and checksum are visible. The wait-for-durable operation blocks until the slot's LSN is at or below the durable frontier, or returns an error if the flush that covered it failed.

A batch descriptor is the unit of coordination. It contains the first and last LSN in the batch, the set of commit LSNs that must be covered, the byte ranges that need flushing, and a completion object. The coordinator builds batches by atomically claiming a range of reserved slots. A simple approach is a ticket counter: each producer that wants to commit increments a batch-arrival counter and reads the current batch. The producer that moves the counter from zero to one becomes the leader for that batch. The leader waits until the batch is full, a timer expires, or the queue is drained, then writes any remaining records, issues the flush, and publishes completion.

The leader must not assume that all records in the batch are already written. Some producers may have reserved LSNs but not yet published their bytes. The leader can either wait for publication, which risks head-of-line blocking, or split the batch at the highest contiguous published LSN. The second option is usually better: it allows the flush to cover a prefix while later records continue to be written. The batch descriptor then records the flush range, not the full reservation range. Followers whose LSNs fall outside the flush range remain in the next batch.

Bounded queues are essential. If producers can reserve LSNs faster than the flusher can drain them, memory grows and latency explodes. Backpressure can be applied at reservation time: a producer that cannot reserve within a bounded window waits until the durable frontier advances. This couples admission to flush progress and prevents the log from becoming an unbounded buffer. The wait must be interruptible and must not hold locks that the flusher needs. A condition variable on the frontier, or an eventfd, is usually sufficient.

Flush failure handling is where many implementations become unsafe. If fsync returns an error, the coordinator must mark the batch failed and must not advance the frontier. Followers must receive the error and must not report commit success. The system may retry the flush, but it must be careful: a retry after a partial write may leave duplicate or torn records. The WAL format should make replay idempotent or should use a new flush epoch that supersedes the failed one. If the failure is permanent, the affected transactions must abort, and the log may need to be truncated to the last known good frontier. Truncation must not remove records that recovery still needs for undo or for prepared transactions.

Timeouts in the coordinator are dangerous. A leader that appears stuck may be waiting on a slow device, not dead. Stealing leadership and issuing a second flush for the same range can create concurrent flushes with different completion orders. If the system allows concurrent flushes, it needs a fencing token or a flush epoch so that a late completion from an old flush cannot advance the frontier past a newer failure. In a single-process system, a simpler rule is that only one flush per log shard may be in flight at a time. That serializes the device flush but still allows many writes and commits to be batched behind it.

## Completion Tracking and Frontier Advancement

The durable frontier is the largest LSN such that every record up to that LSN is written and covered by a successful flush. Computing it efficiently is a data structure problem. The naive approach scans a bitmap from the current frontier after every flush. That is acceptable if the frontier advances by large steps, but it can become quadratic if many small out-of-order completions occur. A better approach maintains a set of completed intervals and a min-heap of gaps. When a slot completes, the tracker merges adjacent intervals. The frontier advances while the interval starting at frontier plus one is complete. The heap is only needed if the tracker wants to know the next expected gap for scheduling, not for correctness.

Memory ordering is part of the data structure. A slot has a payload and a state. The writer must store the payload, then store the checksum, then perform a release store on the state. The flusher must perform an acquire load on the state before reading the payload. If the state is a single word, the release/acquire pair is enough. If the state is a bit in a bitmap, the writer must ensure that the bitmap update does not race with the flusher's read. Atomic bit operations with appropriate fences, or a per-slot state byte, are safer than a shared bitmap without synchronization.

The flush operation itself must cover the right bytes. On a block device, a flush may be a barrier that orders all prior writes to the device. On a filesystem, fsync flushes data and metadata for a file, while fdatasync may skip metadata that does not affect data retrieval. The coordinator should know which operation it is using and what guarantees it provides. If the log is written with O_DIRECT, the write may bypass the page cache, but the device may still have a volatile write cache. A flush is still required unless the device is known to be power-safe and the filesystem contract says otherwise. The frontier should advance only after the strongest flush that the durability contract requires.

Commit records need special treatment. There are two common formats. In the first, each transaction writes its own commit record. The durable prefix rule then applies directly: a transaction is committed when its commit record is at or below the frontier. In the second, a batch writes a single commit record containing a list of transaction IDs or LSNs. That record is atomic if it is checksummed and length-framed. When it becomes durable, all transactions in the list are committed. The second format can reduce log volume and make group commit more explicit, but it requires the coordinator to build the list before writing the record. If the list is built after some transactions have already written their own commit records, recovery must reconcile both formats or the design must use only one.

The commit visibility rule must be enforced at every layer. A transaction's changes must not be visible to other transactions until its commit record is durable. In an MVCC system, that means the transaction's commit timestamp or commit LSN must not be published to the snapshot manager before the frontier advances. In a lock-based system, it means locks must not be released until the commit is durable. In a replicated system, it means the commit must not be acknowledged to the client until the local durable frontier and any required replication acknowledgments are satisfied. These are separate conditions, and the WAL frontier is only one of them.

## Shard Topologies and Cross-Shard Commit

Sharding a WAL is not just partitioning a file. The shard boundary determines which transactions can commit locally and which need coordination. The simplest topology is one shard per independent transaction domain. If the workload can be partitioned so that most transactions touch a single shard, per-shard group commit gives near-linear scaling with the number of shards, limited only by device bandwidth and CPU. The routing layer must be stable: if a transaction can move between shards, the system needs a way to serialize its accesses or to use a cross-shard protocol.

A common topology is a shared-nothing database with a coordinator per shard. Each shard has its own WAL, its own buffer pool, and its own recovery. Cross-shard transactions use two-phase commit. In the prepare phase, the coordinator sends prepare messages to the participants. Each participant writes a prepare record to its shard log and flushes it. The prepare record contains the transaction ID, the participant's vote, and enough information to redo or undo the transaction's changes. The coordinator then decides commit or abort and writes a commit decision to a global log or to one of the participant logs. The decision must be durable before the coordinator acknowledges the client.

The global log can be a bottleneck if every cross-shard transaction writes to it. One way to reduce contention is to shard the global log by transaction ID or by coordinator. Another is to use a single global log only for decisions, while data records stay in per-shard logs. The global log then has a small record per cross-shard transaction, which is easier to batch. Its durable frontier is a global commit point, but it does not need to contain the full redo payload. Recovery reads the global log to resolve prepared transactions, then applies redo from the shard logs.

A vector frontier is the natural representation of durability across shards. Each shard has a frontier, and the system may also maintain a global commit point. A read that spans shards needs a snapshot that is consistent with the global commit point. If the read uses per-shard frontiers directly, it may see a transaction committed on shard A but not yet durable on shard B. That is not necessarily a correctness violation if the transaction is not yet acknowledged, but it becomes one if the read observes the transaction's effects on A and then reads B without the corresponding effects. MVCC with a global commit timestamp solves this by making visibility depend on the commit timestamp, not on the local durable frontier. The local frontier is then only a recovery and acknowledgment mechanism.

Cross-shard commit also affects group commit. A prepare record must be durable before the participant votes, so each participant needs its own flush. The coordinator's decision record needs another flush. This adds at least two durability boundaries per cross-shard transaction, which is why cross-shard transactions are expensive even when the WAL is parallelized. Batching can help: multiple prepare records can share a flush, and multiple decisions can share a global log flush. But the ordering constraints are stricter. A participant must not vote before its prepare record is durable. The coordinator must not decide before all votes are durable. The decision must not be acknowledged before the decision record is durable. These are not optional.

## Recovery with Sharded Logs

Recovery in a sharded system must reconcile multiple logs without assuming a global order. The first phase is shard-local analysis. Each shard scans its log from the last checkpoint, validates records, and builds a transaction table. It identifies prepared transactions, committed transactions, and losers. Because the shard log is contiguous up to its durable frontier, the scan stops at the first invalid record. The shard-local analysis does not need to know about other shards yet.

The second phase resolves cross-shard decisions. The recovery coordinator reads the global log or the decision records in the participant logs. For each prepared transaction, it determines whether a commit decision is durable. If yes, the transaction is a winner and must be redone. If no, and if the system guarantees that a missing decision means abort, the transaction is a loser and must be undone. If the decision is in doubt, recovery must wait or must choose a policy. A blocking two-phase commit cannot unilaterally abort a prepared transaction after a crash if the coordinator may have committed it elsewhere. That is the fundamental blocking property of two-phase commit. Systems that cannot tolerate blocking use a consensus protocol for the decision, which adds another log and another durability boundary.

The third phase is redo and undo. In an ARIES-style recovery, redo is performed for all transactions, including losers, to restore the database to the state it had at the crash. Undo then rolls back the losers. The redo phase needs the page LSN to avoid reapplying changes that are already in the page. In a sharded system, redo can be parallelized across shards because each shard has its own pages and its own log. Cross-shard dependencies are handled by the transaction status: a loser's changes are undone on every shard, and a winner's changes are kept. The undo phase may need to follow the transaction's log records across shards, which requires a transaction ID that is globally unique and a way to locate the participant logs.

Checkpoints must also be coordinated. A fuzzy checkpoint records the active transaction table, the dirty page table, and the current durable frontier for each shard. In a sharded system, the checkpoint coordinator must ensure that the set of checkpoints is consistent enough for recovery. It does not need a global stop-the-world, but it must not record a transaction as committed in one shard's checkpoint if the decision is not durable. A common approach is to write a global checkpoint record that references per-shard checkpoint LSNs. Recovery starts from the oldest referenced checkpoint and scans forward.

Torn tails and partial batches are the normal case after a crash. The recovery reader must treat a partial batch commit record as if the entire batch never committed, unless the format allows individual commit records to be durable independently. If the batch record is atomic, its checksum and length must be validated before any transaction in the batch is considered committed. If the batch record is written after the data records, a crash may leave the data records durable but the batch record missing. Recovery must then undo those data records or treat the transactions as losers. This is why the commit record must be the last thing written for a transaction, and why the durable frontier must not advance past a missing commit record.

## Testing the Invariants

Parallel WAL code is difficult to review because the bugs are often timing-dependent. Testing must target the invariants directly. A simulated device is the most valuable tool. It should accept writes and flushes as separate operations, allow writes to complete out of order, allow flushes to fail, and allow a crash to discard all writes that were not covered by a successful flush. It should also be able to tear a write at any byte boundary, so that recovery's checksum and framing logic is exercised. With such a device, a test harness can run thousands of transactions, inject crashes at random points, and verify that every acknowledged transaction is present after recovery and that no unacknowledged transaction is visible.

The durable-prefix invariant can be checked continuously with assertions. After every frontier update, the system can assert that every LSN up to the frontier is marked written and durable. It can assert that no commit acknowledgment was sent for an LSN above the frontier. It can assert that the recovery reader, when run on the simulated device state, finds exactly the acknowledged transactions. These assertions are cheap enough to run in debug builds and in stress tests.

Concurrency tests should include adversarial scheduling. One thread can reserve LSNs and then stall before publishing. Another can publish and flush. The flusher should never advance the frontier past the stalled LSN. A test can deliberately pause a writer after reservation, run flushes, and verify that the frontier stops at the gap. Another test can pause a flusher after issuing a flush but before processing completion, then crash and verify that recovery does not acknowledge transactions whose flush never completed. These tests are more valuable than throughput benchmarks for finding correctness bugs.

Performance testing should measure the right things. Flush utilization is the fraction of time the device is doing useful flush work. Batch size distribution shows whether group commit is actually batching or degenerating into one transaction per flush. Commit latency percentiles show the cost of waiting for a batch. Recovery time shows whether the log format and checkpoint policy are practical. Write amplification shows whether parallel writes are causing extra metadata or checksum overhead. A design that improves throughput but doubles recovery time may not be acceptable for a system with a strict recovery objective.

## Durability Ordering Below the WAL

A WAL's ordering discipline does not end at the log buffer. It extends through every layer that can reorder, coalesce, or discard writes. The application writes bytes to a file descriptor. The kernel page cache may hold dirty pages. The block layer may merge adjacent writes and reorder them. The device may have a volatile write cache. The media may perform garbage collection or internal wear leveling. A successful `write` or `pwrite` only means the kernel accepted the bytes. It says nothing about stable storage. A successful `fsync` or `fdatasync` says the filesystem has flushed the file's data and, depending on the call, metadata. On some systems, `sync_file_range` only starts writeback and does not wait for durability. `O_DSYNC` and `O_SYNC` change the write path, but their exact guarantees depend on the filesystem and device. Direct I/O bypasses the page cache, but it does not automatically bypass the device cache. If the device has a volatile write cache, a flush command is still required unless the device is known to be power-safe or the cache is battery-backed and the contract says so.

Parallel WAL implementations often use multiple files or multiple shards. Each file has its own durability boundary. Flushing shard A does not make shard B durable, even if both files live on the same device. The filesystem may issue a single device flush that covers both, but the WAL cannot assume that from the file-level API. It must either issue a flush per shard or use a coordinated flush that covers all shards and then advances each shard's frontier together. The latter can reduce flush overhead, but it creates a global barrier. If one shard is slow or has a gap, the coordinated flush must not advance the other shards past their own gaps. A vector frontier is still required; the coordinated flush only changes how the flush is issued, not how completion is tracked.

Write barriers and flush epochs are useful when the WAL rotates segments. A segment may be sealed and flushed while a new segment receives writes. The durable frontier can advance across the sealed segment only after its flush completes. If the new segment has a lower LSN than some unflushed record in the old segment, the frontier must stop at the gap. Segment rotation must therefore be ordered with LSN reservation. A common bug is to rotate segments based on file size without considering that a slow writer may still hold an earlier LSN in the old segment. The rotation logic must either wait for the old segment to be fully published or keep both segments open and track completion across them.

Torn writes are another layer-specific concern. Many devices guarantee atomicity only at sector granularity, often 512 bytes or 4096 bytes, and some storage stacks can tear even within a sector under power loss. The WAL format must detect a torn record through length, checksum, and sequence fields. A checksum that covers the header and payload is necessary but not sufficient if the length field itself is torn. The reader must validate the length against the remaining bytes and against a maximum record size. A commit record that is partially written must never be treated as a commit, even if its checksum happens to validate over a truncated payload. This is why commit records should be self-describing and should include a transaction count or a list length that recovery can cross-check.

## Record Framing for Concurrent Appenders

Concurrent appenders do not change the need for a total order, but they do change the log format. A record must be self-contained enough that recovery can validate it without reading neighboring records. It must also be writable without read-modify-write cycles that would serialize the write path. Fixed-size headers help. A typical header contains a magic number, format version, record type, flags, LSN, transaction ID, payload length, and checksum. The checksum should cover the header with the checksum field zeroed, plus the payload. If the record is compressed, the checksum should cover the compressed bytes or the uncompressed bytes consistently; mixing the two across record types invites subtle corruption.

Variable-length records need alignment. If the log is written in blocks, each block can have its own header with a first LSN, a record count, and a block checksum. Writers reserve slots within a block and fill them independently. The block header is written last, or its checksum is updated after all records in the block are published. Writing the block header last is safer because a crash leaves the block invalid until the header is durable. Recovery then ignores the block and stops at the previous valid block. This is a form of atomicity at block granularity. It works well when records are small and many writers share a block.

Commit records deserve a separate format. A per-transaction commit record can be a small record with the transaction ID and commit LSN. A batch commit record can contain a list of transaction IDs or a range of LSNs. The batch record is atomic if it is length-framed and checksummed. When it becomes durable, every transaction in the list is committed. The coordinator must build the list before writing the record. If some transactions in the batch have not yet published their data records, the batch commit record must not include them. Including them would acknowledge a transaction whose redo records are not durable. The safe rule is that the batch commit record covers only transactions whose data records are already written and whose LSNs are within the flush range.

Checksum computation can be parallelized. Each producer can compute its record checksum before reservation, or after reservation if the LSN is part of the checksum. If the LSN is part of the checksum, the producer must reserve first, then compute, then publish. That adds a dependency between reservation and checksum, but it is still off the flush critical path. Some systems use a checksum that does not include the LSN and store the LSN in a separate field protected by a second checksum. That allows checksum computation before reservation, but it complicates validation. The simpler rule is to reserve, compute, publish. The cost is CPU, not ordering.

Recovery must handle gaps in the physical layout. If writers reserve LSNs and write out of order, the file may contain record 101 before record 100. Recovery scans in LSN order, not file order. It can use an index from LSN to offset, or it can scan the file and build a map. If it encounters a gap, it must stop at the gap unless it has a way to know that the missing record will never appear. The durable frontier is that way. If the frontier is below the gap, the missing record may still appear after a crash? No, after a crash, no more writes occur. The frontier is the authority. Recovery should replay only up to the durable frontier and should treat any record beyond it as suspect. But it must still validate checksums because the frontier itself may be stale if the crash occurred during a frontier update. The frontier update should be part of the log or should be reconstructible from the log. A common approach is to write a checkpoint or a flush marker record that indicates a durable prefix. Recovery scans forward until it finds the last valid flush marker, then stops.

## Checkpointing and Safe Truncation Under Parallel Flush

Checkpointing is the mechanism that bounds recovery time and allows log truncation. In a parallel WAL, a checkpoint must capture a consistent cut without stopping all writers. A fuzzy checkpoint begins by recording a checkpoint begin record with a checkpoint LSN. It then collects the active transaction table, the dirty page table, and the durable frontier for each shard. It finishes with a checkpoint end record. Recovery starts from the begin record and uses the collected state. The checkpoint does not need to be a point-in-time snapshot of the database, but it must be a consistent description of what recovery needs.

The active transaction table contains every transaction that has not yet committed or aborted. For each transaction, it records the first LSN, the last LSN, and the status. In a sharded system, a transaction may be active on multiple shards. The checkpoint coordinator must gather the per-shard tables and merge them by transaction ID. A transaction that is prepared but not yet decided must be recorded as prepared, not as committed. If the checkpoint records it as committed before the decision record is durable, recovery may redo a transaction that should be undone. That is a correctness failure.

The dirty page table records pages that have been modified in memory but not yet written to disk. Each entry contains the page ID and the recovery LSN, which is the LSN of the first log record that modified the page since it was last written. The minimum recovery LSN across all dirty pages and active transactions determines how far back recovery must scan. Truncation can remove log records before that minimum. In a sharded system, each shard has its own minimum. Global truncation requires the minimum across all shards, because a cross-shard transaction may have records in multiple logs. A prepared transaction pins the log on every shard where it has prepared records. Until the decision is durable and the transaction is resolved, those records cannot be truncated.

Parallel flushers complicate checkpointing because frontiers advance independently. A checkpoint that records shard A's frontier at LSN 1000 and shard B's frontier at LSN 800 may be inconsistent if a cross-shard transaction committed on A but not yet on B. The checkpoint must record the transaction status separately from the frontiers. The frontiers are only for recovery scanning and truncation. Visibility is determined by the transaction status and the commit decision. If the checkpoint coordinator cannot gather all shard states atomically, it can use a two-phase checkpoint: first freeze the transaction table, then gather frontiers, then unfreeze. Freezing the transaction table does not stop data writes; it only prevents new transactions from entering the table until the checkpoint state is captured. That is usually a short pause.

Log space reclamation is the operational reason for truncation. A WAL that cannot truncate will eventually fill the disk. Backpressure must be applied before that happens. When the log reaches a high-water mark, the system should slow down or reject new transactions until a checkpoint advances the minimum recovery LSN. The backpressure must be visible to clients as increased latency or explicit errors, not as a crash. In a sharded system, one slow shard can hold back global truncation. The system may need to move or resolve prepared transactions, or it may need to accept that the slow shard limits log space for everyone. Monitoring the per-shard truncation frontier is as important as monitoring the durable frontier.

## Replication and Consensus: When the WAL Is a Replicated Log

In a replicated database, the WAL is often also the replication log. The leader appends records, sends them to followers, and waits for acknowledgments before committing. The commit index is the highest log position that is known to be replicated to a quorum. Followers apply entries in log order. The local durable frontier is not the same as the commit index. A leader may have a record durable locally but not yet replicated to a quorum. It must not acknowledge the client until the commit index advances past the record. Conversely, a follower may have a record durable locally but not yet committed by the leader. It must not apply the record to its state machine until the leader commits it.

Parallelism in a replicated log appears in several places. The leader can pipeline appends, sending multiple entries before earlier ones are acknowledged. Followers can write entries in parallel, but they must preserve log order when applying them. Group commit can batch replication acknowledgments and local flushes. A follower can acknowledge a batch of entries after one flush, as long as the batch is contiguous and the follower's durable frontier covers all of them. The leader can then advance the commit index to the highest entry that a quorum has acknowledged. The quorum acknowledgment must be for a contiguous prefix; a follower that has entry 10 but not entry 9 cannot help commit entry 10.

Consensus protocols add their own ordering rules. Raft, for example, has a leader term, a log index, and a commit index. A leader cannot commit an entry from a previous term by counting replicas alone; it must first commit an entry from its current term. That rule interacts with group commit because the leader may need to replicate a no-op or a term marker before it can commit older entries. A parallel WAL must respect these rules. It cannot simply flush the highest LSN and declare it committed. The commit index is a consensus property, not a local durability property.

Sharded consensus is a natural extension of log sharding. Each shard can have its own Raft group with its own leader and commit index. Cross-shard transactions then require a protocol that spans groups. Two-phase commit with a consensus-backed decision log is common. The prepare phase writes prepare records to each participant shard and flushes them. The decision phase writes a commit or abort record to a decision log, which itself is replicated and committed by consensus. Only after the decision is committed can participants apply the transaction and release locks. The client is acknowledged after the decision is committed and, depending on the isolation level, after the participants have applied it.

The interaction between local WAL flush and consensus commit is a frequent source of confusion. A local flush makes a record durable on one node. A consensus commit makes a record durable on a quorum and orders it with respect to other records. A system that acknowledges after the local flush but before the consensus commit may lose the transaction if the leader fails before replication. A system that acknowledges after the consensus commit but before the local state machine applies the record may return success for a transaction that is not yet visible to reads on that node. The acknowledgment rule must be explicit: which of local durability, quorum durability, and state machine application are required before the client sees success. Different systems choose different points, but the WAL frontier alone is not enough.

## Failure Modes and Antipatterns

The most dangerous parallel WAL bugs are not crashes; they are acknowledgments that outrun durability. A system that returns success after a write but before a flush has no durability guarantee at all. A system that flushes a later record but advances the frontier past an earlier missing record has a hole. Recovery may stop at the hole and lose the later record, or it may replay the later record without its predecessor and corrupt state. The durable prefix must be contiguous, and the frontier must never advance past a gap.

Another antipattern is comparing LSNs across shards. Two shards may use the same counter values, but those values are not globally ordered unless the system has a global sequence or commit timestamp. A cross-shard read that compares per-shard LSNs directly can observe an inconsistent snapshot. Visibility should be based on transaction status or a global commit timestamp, not on raw shard LSNs. If a global order is needed, it must be allocated explicitly and durably.

Flush errors are often ignored. If `fsync` returns an error, the bytes may be partially durable, and the device may be in an unknown state. The coordinator must fail the batch, not advance the frontier, and not acknowledge the transactions. Retrying the flush is acceptable only if the WAL format makes replay idempotent and the retry cannot create duplicate commit records that are both considered valid. A new flush epoch with a fencing token is safer. A late completion from an old epoch must not advance the frontier after a newer epoch has failed.

Concurrent flushes without fencing are another source of corruption. If two threads flush overlapping ranges and both advance the frontier, a failure in one can be masked by the other. The system may acknowledge a transaction whose flush actually failed. A single in-flight flush per shard, or a flush epoch with monotonic tokens, prevents this. The cost is some lost parallelism in the flush path, but the flush is usually the bottleneck anyway; batching is more valuable than concurrent flush issuance.

Finally, truncation without resolving prepared transactions is unsafe. A prepared transaction has a durable vote but no durable decision. Its log records may be needed for redo or undo after recovery. Truncating them because the checkpoint's minimum recovery LSN moved forward is a correctness bug. Prepared transactions must pin the log until the decision is durable and the transaction is resolved on every participant. This is one reason cross-shard transactions are expensive: they extend the log retention window and reduce the effectiveness of truncation. A system that ignores this will eventually fail recovery after a crash that occurs while a prepared transaction is in doubt.

## Instrumentation for Ordering Guarantees

A parallel WAL is only as trustworthy as the evidence it produces about its own ordering. Throughput counters and latency histograms are useful, but they do not tell an operator whether the durable frontier is moving safely. The first-class metrics are structural: reservation frontier, publication frontier, flush frontier, durable frontier, and commit frontier. Each is an LSN or a per-shard vector. The differences between them expose the pipeline. Reservation minus publication is the amount of reserved but unwritten log. Publication minus flush is written but not yet flushed log. Flush minus durable is I/O in flight or awaiting completion. Commit minus durable is transactions waiting for acknowledgment. If the system replicates, a replicated frontier sits beside the durable frontier and must not be confused with it.

These gaps should be exported as gauges with per-shard labels and as maximum gap ages. A large reservation-publication gap may indicate CPU starvation in record formatting or a stalled producer. A large publication-flush gap may indicate a slow device or a flusher that is not batching effectively. A large flush-durable gap usually means the device queue is deep or flush completions are delayed. A commit-durable gap that stays high while the device is idle points to a coordination bug: the coordinator is not issuing flushes, not waking waiters, or has lost leadership. None of these conditions is visible from a single average throughput number.

Out-of-order completion distance is another useful metric. It is the difference between the highest published LSN and the highest contiguous published LSN. If this distance grows without bound, the completion tracker is accumulating holes, and the durable frontier cannot advance. The cause may be a slow writer that reserved early and stalled, a lost wakeup, or a checksum computation that is much slower than the append rate. A bounded distance is healthy; an unbounded one is a latent availability incident. The same metric applies to flush completions: the highest flushed LSN minus the highest contiguous flushed LSN.

Frontier transitions should be logged as structured events, not as free-form text. A transition record can contain the old frontier, the new frontier, the flush epoch, the device operation identifier, and the set of slots marked durable. In a debug build, the system can also record which thread advanced the frontier and why. When a crash occurs, these events help reconstruct whether an acknowledgment was sent before or after durability. They also let the recovery reader compare log contents against the last known durable frontier and assert that no acknowledged commit is missing.

Flight recording is especially valuable for rare ordering bugs. A ring buffer of recent reservations, publications, flush issuances, flush completions, and frontier advances can be dumped when an assertion fails or when a latency outlier exceeds a threshold. The dump should include thread identifiers, LSNs, slot states, and memory order annotations. The goal is not to log every byte but to preserve the causal chain around a gap. A gap that lasts for milliseconds under load may be normal; a gap that persists for seconds is a bug or a device failure, and the flight recorder should show which operation was expected to close it.

Assertions should encode the invariants in executable form. After every frontier advance, assert that every LSN up to the frontier is written and durable. Before every commit acknowledgment, assert that the transaction's commit LSN is at or below the durable frontier and, if replication is required, at or below the replicated commit index. Before truncating a log segment, assert that its highest LSN is below the minimum recovery LSN across all shards and that no prepared transaction references it. Before reusing a slot, assert that the slot is durable or that the log has been truncated past it. These assertions are not a substitute for tests, but they turn silent corruption into immediate failures.

## Interplay with the Buffer Pool and Page Writes

The write-ahead rule is easy to state and easy to violate when page flushing is parallelized. A dirty page must not be written to its permanent location until the log records that describe its changes are durable. In a single-threaded engine, this is often implemented by comparing the page LSN to the durable frontier before issuing the page write. In a parallel engine, the page flusher may be a separate thread pool, and the durable frontier may advance concurrently. The check must be atomic with respect to frontier advancement. If the page flusher reads the frontier, sees that it is high enough, and then a flush failure occurs, the page write may become unsafe. Frontiers should be monotonic in normal operation; they are not lowered except during recovery or truncation. A flush failure must fail the affected transactions and must not allow page writes that depend on them to proceed.

The cleanest discipline is to treat page writes as consumers of a durability token. Before a page can be written, its page LSN must be covered by a successful flush. The page flusher can either wait for the frontier to reach the page LSN or enqueue the page write behind a flush request for that LSN. Waiting is simpler but can block page cleaning and cause buffer pool pressure. Enqueuing a flush request lets the page flusher continue with other work, but it requires a callback or completion queue. In either case, the page write must not be issued before the flush completion. If the buffer pool evicts a dirty page, it must obey the same rule. Eviction cannot bypass the WAL because the page is leaving memory.

Group commit interacts with page flushing in a beneficial way. A page flusher that needs a range of LSNs can join or create a group commit batch for those LSNs. That batch may contain commit records from other transactions, which amortizes the flush. The page flusher does not need to wait for a transaction-level commit; it only needs the data records for the page to be durable. This separation is important: commit durability and page durability share the log flush but have different frontiers. A page can be written once its redo records are durable even if the transaction is still active and may later abort. The undo records must also be durable for that page to be safely written, because recovery may need to roll back the transaction. In an ARIES-style system, the log records for both redo and undo are written before the page write. The page LSN covers the redo; the transaction table covers the undo. The buffer pool must not write a page whose changes are not yet durably logged, regardless of whether the transaction has committed.

Checkpointing adds another consumer. A fuzzy checkpoint records the dirty page table, but it does not force all dirty pages to disk. It only records enough information for recovery to redo changes from the log. Page cleaning can continue after the checkpoint. However, the checkpoint's minimum recovery LSN depends on the oldest dirty page and the oldest active transaction. If page cleaning is slow, the minimum recovery LSN stays low, and log truncation is delayed. Parallel page flushers can help by cleaning pages faster, but they must respect the WAL frontier. A system that parallelizes page writes without parallelizing WAL flushes will simply move the bottleneck to the WAL. A system that parallelizes both must coordinate them through the durable frontier.

## Hardware and Deployment Realities

The theoretical WAL model assumes a device that honors flush and a filesystem that reports failures accurately. Real deployments vary. A local NVMe device with power-loss protection may provide strong flush semantics with low latency. A cloud block store may have a volatile cache, variable flush latency, and occasional I/O errors that are not permanent. A filesystem may lie about fsync on certain configurations, or it may return success after issuing a flush to a device that later loses power. The WAL design cannot fix a broken durability contract, but it can make the contract explicit and testable. The coordinator should record which durability primitive it uses and expose it as configuration. It should also validate at startup that the primitive behaves as expected, for example by writing a known pattern, flushing, and reading it back through a separate file descriptor. That is not a power-loss test, but it catches gross misconfiguration.

Atomicity granularity matters. Many devices guarantee atomic sector writes, but the sector size may be 512 bytes, 4096 bytes, or larger. A record that spans sectors may tear. The WAL format should either keep commit records within a single atomic unit or use checksum and length framing that detects a torn record. If the device supports atomic multi-sector writes, the commit record can be larger, but the system should not assume that support unless it is explicitly negotiated. Zoned storage and host-managed SMR devices add another constraint: writes must be sequential within a zone. A parallel WAL that writes to random offsets within a zone will fail. Sharding can map each shard to a separate zone or stream, but the append point within a shard must remain sequential.

Deployment topology affects flush behavior. In a container, the WAL file may be on an overlay filesystem backed by a network volume. fsync may be much slower and may have different failure modes than on a local disk. The CPU quota may be low, so record formatting and checksum computation compete with the flusher. The device queue depth may be shared with other tenants, so flush latency is noisy. A group-commit coordinator should be adaptive to these conditions. It should not assume that a fixed batch size or timeout is optimal across environments. It should measure flush service time and adjust batching, and it should expose the batch policy to operators who know the workload. Backpressure should be tied to the log high-water mark and to the flush queue depth, not only to transaction concurrency.

Capacity planning for a parallel WAL is mostly about the gap between reservation and durability. The log must have enough space to hold all reserved but not yet truncated records. If the workload is bursty, the reservation frontier can jump ahead of the durable frontier quickly. The high-water mark must account for the maximum in-flight batch, the maximum out-of-order completion distance, and the checkpoint retention window. A common mistake is to size the log for average throughput and then discover that a single slow flush or a prepared transaction pins the log for much longer than expected. Per-shard log sizing is also necessary. If one shard receives a hot partition, its log will fill faster than the others, and global truncation cannot advance until that shard checkpoints. Monitoring per-shard log usage and per-shard truncation frontier is more useful than monitoring only the total.
