Every long-lived server is, among other things, a machine for remembering deadlines. An RPC handler arms a timeout so a stalled peer cannot pin a connection forever. A TCP stack schedules retransmission. A cache records when an entry becomes stale. A rate limiter decides when the next token is available. An async runtime tracks thousands of sleeps, leases, and heartbeat expirations. In each case, the system must answer a deceptively simple question: what work is due now, and what work is due next?

The obvious answer is a priority queue keyed by deadline. A binary heap gives O(log n) insertion and deletion, O(1) access to the minimum, and a familiar implementation. For modest timer populations, that is often the right engineering choice. But timer facilities in high-throughput systems have a different workload shape from general-purpose priority queues. Deadlines are monotonically increasing in practice, cancellation is common, the number of outstanding timers can be large, and the expiration path must run on every event-loop iteration. Under those conditions, pointer-chasing through a heap can become a cache and contention problem rather than an algorithmic one.

Timing wheels take a different approach: they quantize time and index timers by when they are due instead of comparing arbitrary deadline keys. The idea is old, but the engineering details are subtle. A wheel is not merely a circular array. It is a family of trade-offs among precision, memory, maximum representable delay, cancellation cost, lock granularity, and wakeup behavior. Hierarchical wheels add levels so that near-term timers remain precise while far-term timers consume little space. Hashed wheels bound memory by accepting collisions. Tickless operation removes the cost of waking an idle system every tick, but it introduces the need to find the next deadline efficiently and to program a hardware timer around it.

This article examines the timer facility as a systems component rather than as a single data structure. The assumptions matter: deadlines are expressed against a monotonic clock; timers may be canceled at any time; cancellation may race with expiration; the facility may be sharded across cores; and the acceptable latency may be coarser than the clock resolution. The goal is not to declare one design universally superior. It is to make the cost model explicit enough that the choice follows from the workload.

## What a timer facility must actually do

A timer facility exposes a small API surface, but the semantic obligations behind that API are larger than they first appear. At minimum, the facility must support scheduling a callback or event after a delay or at an absolute deadline, canceling a timer, and advancing time so that due timers can run. Production facilities usually add rescheduling, repeating timers, deadline coalescing, and some notion of timer slack.

The cost model has several dimensions. The first is operation cost. Insertion, cancellation, and expiration each have a CPU cost, but they are not symmetric. Some workloads insert many timers and cancel most of them before they fire; connection timeouts and hedged requests behave this way. Other workloads insert timers that almost always expire, such as periodic cache sweeps or heartbeat checks. A design that makes insertion cheap but cancellation expensive can be a poor fit for a cancel-heavy workload.

The second dimension is memory. Each outstanding timer needs a handle, a deadline, a callback reference, and whatever linkage the data structure requires. In a heap, the timer may need an index so that cancellation can repair the heap in O(log n). In an intrusive linked list used by a wheel bucket, the timer itself contains forward and backward pointers. The memory overhead is small per timer but multiplies at scale. A million timers with sixty-four bytes of bookkeeping is sixty-four megabytes before any payload; ten million is a different operational reality.

The third dimension is latency and precision. A timer facility does not usually promise that a callback runs exactly at its deadline. It promises that the callback does not run before the deadline and that it runs reasonably soon after it. The delay between deadline and execution is the expiration latency. Tick-based designs quantize deadlines to tick boundaries. A one-millisecond tick cannot distinguish deadlines that fall within the same millisecond. Coarse ticks reduce wakeups and bucket scans; fine ticks increase precision and overhead.

The fourth dimension is concurrency. A single global wheel with one lock can become a serialization point, especially when timer insertion happens on every request. Sharding by timer owner or timer identifier distributes the lock, but cancellation and expiration must then deal with cross-shard ownership. A timer created on one core may be canceled from another. A timer may need to migrate when a connection moves between event loops. The facility must define who is allowed to mutate the timer and how that ownership is transferred without introducing lost wakeups or double expirations.

Finally, there are semantic edge cases. Is a zero-delay timer due immediately or on the next advance? If two timers share a deadline, is their order defined? What happens when a callback reschedules itself? Can cancellation succeed after expiration has already begun? What happens if the wall clock jumps but the monotonic clock does not? A timer facility is only as good as its contract for these cases. The data structure cannot answer them; the surrounding protocol must.

## Why a min-heap is usually not enough

A binary min-heap is the default mental model for timers, and for good reason. It has predictable bounds, no dependence on a fixed time horizon, and a simple invariant: the smallest deadline sits at the root. Accessing the next deadline is O(1). Inserting or removing an arbitrary timer is O(log n), provided the timer's position in the heap array is known. If cancellation is implemented by marking a timer as canceled and letting it be discarded at the root, cancellation becomes O(1), but the heap accumulates dead entries and expiration must skip them. That is often acceptable when cancellations are rare; it is less pleasant when most timers are canceled before firing.

The deeper issue is locality. Heap operations move elements between levels, and each comparison may touch a different cache line. The root is hot, but the rest of the heap is not laid out by deadline. A timer facility that advances time repeatedly and finds that the root is not yet due may do very little work, which is good. A facility that inserts and cancels millions of timers per second performs many sift-up and sift-down operations, each with unpredictable memory access. The asymptotic bound is not the whole cost.

Contention is the other issue. A single heap needs a lock around insertion and deletion. Coarse-grained locking around a priority queue is simple, but it serializes every timer operation in the process. Sharding the heap by hash of timer ID reduces contention, but then the global earliest deadline is the minimum across shards. A tickless event loop needs to know when to wake next, so it must either inspect all shards or accept a looser wakeup bound. If it inspects all shards on every iteration, the cost of finding the minimum can dominate. If it maintains a separate earliest-deadline cache, that cache becomes another contended object and another source of staleness.

This is why systems with large timer populations often move away from comparison-based structures. A heap orders timers by comparing deadlines. A timing wheel exploits the fact that time advances monotonically and that deadlines can be bucketed by a bounded granularity. The wheel does not need to know which timer in a bucket is earliest until the bucket is processed, and often it does not need to know even then. It trades exact ordering for predictable indexing.

That trade is not free. A wheel must choose a tick duration and a number of slots. The product of those two values determines the maximum delay that can be represented directly. A timer beyond that horizon must be handled by cascading, by a coarser overflow structure, or by deferring insertion until the deadline is closer. Each option changes the cost profile. A wheel also has to handle cancellation inside a bucket. If buckets are doubly linked intrusive lists, removal is O(1). If buckets are arrays, cancellation may require compaction or tombstoning. If buckets are sorted to preserve order among equal deadlines, insertion is no longer constant time. The engineering is in choosing which of these costs the workload can tolerate.

## Timing wheels: indexing time instead of comparing it

A basic timing wheel is a circular array of buckets. Each bucket represents a span of time called the tick, and the array has a fixed number of slots. The wheel maintains a current tick index. A timer with delay d is placed into the bucket at index (current + ceil(d / tick)) mod N, where N is the number of slots. When time advances by one tick, the wheel processes the bucket at the new current index.

The appeal is that insertion is O(1): compute a slot, link the timer into that bucket's list. Cancellation is O(1) if the timer holds intrusive list pointers and the list supports removal from the middle. Advancing one tick is O(1) plus the cost of processing the bucket. The cost of expiration is proportional to the number of timers in the expired bucket, which is the necessary work anyway. With a large enough N and a tick coarse enough for the workload, buckets are sparse and most advances do little work.

The maximum representable delay is N times tick. That is the first hard constraint. If the wheel has 1,024 slots and a one-millisecond tick, it covers about 1.024 seconds directly. A timer for five seconds cannot be placed by the simple modulo formula without wrapping into a slot that represents the wrong rotation. The naive fix is to increase N, but memory and cache footprint grow with N. Another fix is to store a rotation count with each timer. The bucket is processed on every rotation, and each timer is decremented until its count reaches zero. That turns expiration into repeated scanning: a five-second timer in a one-second wheel is visited five times before it fires. It is simple but wasteful when delays are much larger than the wheel horizon.

A better fix is cascading, which is the next level of the design. A coarse wheel holds timers that are too far away for the fine wheel. When the coarse wheel advances past a bucket, it moves the timers in that bucket down into the fine wheel, where they become representable. This is the hierarchical timing wheel. It preserves O(1)-style insertion and gives near-term timers fine granularity without requiring a huge flat array.

The second constraint is quantization. A timer with delay 1.2 ticks cannot be placed in a fractional slot. The facility must round up, round down, or maintain a remainder. Rounding up guarantees the callback does not run early, at the cost of up to one tick of extra latency. Rounding down violates the deadline contract. For most systems, rounding up is the safe choice. If the workload needs finer precision than a tick, the tick must be smaller, the wheel must have more slots, or the facility must keep exact deadlines inside buckets and sort or scan them at expiration time.

The third constraint is empty-tick scanning. If the wheel advances one tick at a time and most buckets are empty, the facility still performs work per tick. That work may be small, but it is not zero, and it wakes the CPU when the system could otherwise sleep. A tickless design avoids this by jumping to the next nonempty deadline. A tick-based design can mitigate it by using a bitmap of nonempty buckets or a hierarchy whose upper levels identify the next occupied region. The choice depends on whether the facility is driven by an external periodic interrupt or by an event loop that can compute its own next wakeup.

A useful way to think about a timing wheel is that it replaces a comparison with an address calculation. The deadline becomes an index. The cost of that transformation is quantization and a bounded direct horizon. The benefit is predictable insertion, predictable cancellation, and cache-friendly bucket processing. Whether the trade is good depends on how much of the workload is cancellation, how large the timer population is, and how much latency jitter the application can absorb.

## Hierarchical wheels and the cascade

A hierarchical wheel uses several circular arrays with different tick durations. The finest level has the smallest tick and covers a short horizon. The next level has a coarser tick and covers a longer horizon, and so on. A timer is placed at the coarsest level that can represent its delay without wrapping past the level's horizon. When a coarse bucket becomes due, its timers are redistributed into finer levels according to their remaining delay. Eventually they reach the finest level and expire.

The key property is that expiration work is deferred until it is relevant. A timer scheduled hours in the future does not need to be examined every millisecond. It sits in a coarse bucket until that bucket's time span arrives. The cascade then moves it closer to execution. This is why hierarchical wheels are attractive for operating-system timer subsystems, event loops, and network stacks: they handle a wide range of delays without allocating a flat array large enough to index every tick in the maximum horizon.

The cascade also introduces a subtle latency bound. A timer in a coarse level may not be moved into the fine level until the coarse bucket is processed. If the coarse tick is much larger than the fine tick, a timer can expire later than its exact deadline by up to the coarse granularity. Some designs accept that jitter. Others arrange the hierarchy so that the coarse bucket is processed often enough to keep the error within the facility's contract. The contract should state the maximum expiration latency, not just the resolution of the finest wheel.

Cascading is not free. When a coarse bucket is processed, all timers in it are removed and reinserted into finer levels. If many timers share the same coarse bucket, the cascade can create a burst of work. A well-chosen hierarchy distributes that work, but adversarial or synchronized workloads can concentrate it. For example, if every connection sets a timeout at the same wall-clock second, the coarse bucket for that second receives a large batch. The facility must either tolerate the burst, spread insertion with jitter, or use a different structure for that regime.

A hierarchical wheel also complicates cancellation. If a timer has already cascaded from a coarse bucket to a fine bucket, the cancellation path must find its current location. Intrusive links solve this because the timer carries its own pointers and the facility can unlink it from whatever bucket currently owns it. The timer handle must be invalidated exactly once, and the cancellation must synchronize with expiration. A common pattern is a state machine on the timer: armed, canceled, executing, and done. The wheel only manipulates the armed state; the callback execution path transitions to executing before invoking user code. A cancellation that arrives during executing either fails or requests cooperative cancellation, depending on the API contract.

## Hashed wheels and bounded memory

A hashed timing wheel takes a different approach to the horizon problem. Instead of cascading through levels, it uses a fixed number of buckets and hashes the deadline into a bucket. When the wheel advances, it scans the bucket for the current slot and processes the timers that are actually due. Timers that are not due remain in the bucket for a future rotation, often tracked with a remaining-rounds counter.

The advantage is bounded memory. The number of buckets is fixed, so the wheel does not grow with the number of outstanding timers. Insertion is still an index calculation, and cancellation is still a linked-list removal if the timer is intrusive. The disadvantage is collision. Multiple deadlines map to the same bucket, and the facility must scan them to determine which are due. If the bucket is large, scanning can dominate. If the bucket is small, the wheel needs more buckets or a coarser tick.

Hashed wheels are a good fit when the acceptable granularity is coarse and the timer population is large but not pathological. They are less attractive when deadlines are highly clustered or when the facility must guarantee tight expiration latency. The hash function also matters. A poor hash over the deadline distribution can create hot buckets even when the average load is low. Since deadlines often cluster around round-number intervals, a hash that simply takes the low bits of the deadline may not distribute well. Mixing the deadline or adding a per-timer salt can help, but it adds work to the insertion path.

The boundary between hierarchical wheels and hashed wheels is not sharp. A hierarchy can be seen as a way to avoid collisions at coarse granularity by using multiple resolutions. A hashed wheel can be seen as a single-level hierarchy with rotation counters. Some designs combine both: a hierarchical structure for near-term precision and a hashed overflow region for long delays. The right choice depends on the ratio of timer count to acceptable memory, the distribution of delays, and whether the facility can tolerate scanning a bucket to find the due subset.

## Tickless operation and the earliest-deadline problem

A tick-based timer facility is driven by a periodic interrupt or event-loop tick. On every tick, it advances the wheel and processes due buckets. This is simple and predictable, but it has a cost: the system wakes even when no timer is due. In an idle server, that periodic wakeup is pure overhead. In a busy server, it may be hidden by other work. In a battery-powered or latency-sensitive system, it is not hidden at all.

Tickless operation removes the periodic wakeup. The facility maintains enough information to find the next deadline, programs a hardware timer or event-loop timeout for that deadline, and sleeps until then. When it wakes, it advances the wheel to the current time and processes all buckets that became due. If a new timer is inserted with an earlier deadline, the facility may need to reprogram the wakeup. If the earliest timer is canceled, the facility may need to find the next one. The quality of the facility now depends on how cheaply it can answer the question: what is the next nonempty bucket?

A flat wheel can answer that question by scanning forward from the current slot until it finds a nonempty bucket. If the wheel is sparse, the scan can be long. A bitmap with one bit per bucket makes the search a word-level operation: find the next set bit. A hierarchy makes the search logarithmic in the number of levels, because each level can maintain a bitmap or a coarse summary of its nonempty buckets. The fine level identifies the next occupied bucket within its horizon. If the fine level is empty, the next level identifies the next coarse bucket, and so on.

Tickless operation also changes the batching trade-off. A tick-based facility naturally batches timers that fall within the same tick. A tickless facility can wake for one timer, run it, and then find the next deadline, which may be microseconds later. That precision may be desirable, but it can cause a wakeup storm. A common mitigation is to coalesce deadlines within a slack window. Timers that are due within the same short interval are processed together, and the next wakeup is set to the end of that interval. The application must tolerate the added latency, which is why timer slack is often an explicit API parameter rather than a hidden policy.

The interaction with concurrency is equally important. A tickless event loop usually owns its timer wheel. When another thread inserts a timer into that loop, it must wake the loop if the new deadline is earlier than the current wakeup. That wakeup is a cross-thread signal, often a pipe write, an eventfd, or a futex wake. The signal itself has a cost, so a facility that batches insertions before waking can reduce overhead. A facility that wakes on every insertion can spend more time in kernel transitions than in timer processing.

## Ownership, concurrency, and the cancellation race

The hardest part of a timer facility is not the wheel geometry. It is ownership. A single global wheel with a global lock is easy to reason about but hard to scale. A per-core or per-event-loop wheel scales naturally for insertion and expiration, because the owner thread advances its own wheel. The problem is that timers are often created, canceled, and fired by different threads. A connection may be accepted on one thread, serviced on another, and closed on a third. A request may be scheduled by a client thread and canceled by a timeout thread. The timer facility must provide a stable handle and a protocol for cross-owner operations.

One common design is to pin each timer to the wheel that owns the resource it protects. The timeout for a connection lives on the event loop that owns the connection. Insertion and cancellation from other threads are posted as messages to that loop. The loop processes its own wheel and its own cancellation queue, so no lock is needed around the wheel itself. The cost is a queue hop and a wakeup when a remote cancellation arrives. The benefit is that the hot path remains single-threaded and cache-local. Many event-driven servers use this ownership model, sometimes with a lock-free queue for the cross-thread handoff.

Another design is to shard by timer identifier. The timer handle hashes to a shard, and every operation on that timer goes through the shard's lock or atomic state machine. This avoids the message hop but introduces contention when many timers map to the same shard. It also makes expiration more complex: the shard must advance time and invoke callbacks, or it must hand due timers to a worker pool. If callbacks run under the shard lock, a slow callback can block all timers on that shard. If callbacks run outside the lock, the timer must be removed from the wheel before invocation and its state transitioned atomically.

The cancellation race is the recurring correctness hazard. A timer can be canceled at the same moment it expires. If cancellation removes the timer from a bucket while expiration is iterating that bucket, the iterator can be invalidated. If expiration marks the timer as executing while cancellation marks it as canceled, the callback may run even though cancellation returned success. The facility needs a linearization point: the atomic transition that decides whether the timer will fire or not. A common approach is a compare-and-swap on a state word. The expiration path claims the timer by moving it from armed to executing. The cancellation path moves it from armed to canceled. Only one wins. If cancellation wins, the timer is unlinked and the callback never runs. If expiration wins, cancellation returns a failure or a best-effort result, and the callback may already be running. The API must document which outcome the caller can rely on.

This is the point where the timer facility stops being a container and becomes a protocol. The wheel gives the facility a way to find due timers. The state machine gives it a way to decide which operations are legal. The ownership model gives it a way to avoid locks on the hot path. A design that gets the wheel right but the state machine wrong will be fast and incorrect. A design that gets the state machine right but the wheel wrong will be correct and slow. The next consideration is how these pieces interact when the facility must support rescheduling, repeating timers, and timers that outlive the wheel that created them.

## Rescheduling, repetition, and the drift problem

Many timers are not one-shot. A heartbeat fires every second. A lease renewal runs every thirty seconds. A garbage collector may run every few minutes. The timer facility must support repetition without turning every periodic timer into a self-rescheduling one-shot that drifts.

The naive approach is for the callback to reinsert itself with a new delay equal to the period. That works, but the new deadline is computed from the time the callback actually runs, not from the previous deadline. If the callback is delayed by scheduling jitter, the period stretches. Over many iterations, the timer drifts. For a heartbeat, drift may be harmless. For a rate limiter or a protocol timer, drift can violate a contract. A better approach is to compute the next deadline from the previous deadline: next = previous + period. If the callback is late, the next deadline may already be in the past, and the facility must decide whether to fire immediately, skip missed periods, or catch up. Each choice has different semantics. Firing immediately can create a burst. Skipping periods loses work. Catching up can starve other timers.

A timer facility can support fixed-rate timers by storing the period and the next deadline. When the timer expires, the facility advances the deadline by the period before invoking the callback. If the new deadline is still in the past, the facility can either invoke the callback again immediately or coalesce the missed periods into a single invocation with a count. The latter is often preferable for periodic maintenance tasks: a cache sweep that missed three intervals does not need to run three times in a row; it needs to run once and know that it is behind. The API should expose the difference between fixed-rate and fixed-delay timers, because the two have different drift and catch-up behavior.

Repeating timers also interact with cancellation and rescheduling. If a callback cancels its own timer, the facility must not reinsert it after the callback returns. If a callback reschedules itself with a new period, the facility must handle the old timer's state transition cleanly. A common implementation is to treat the timer as a one-shot at the wheel level and let the callback re-arm it. The wheel does not need to know about periods. This keeps the wheel simple, but it puts the drift and catch-up logic in the callback or in a small wrapper. The wrapper can maintain the next deadline and call the wheel's insert function. The wheel remains a mechanism; the policy lives above it.

Coalescing is another repetition concern. If a thousand connections each set a keepalive timer for the same second, the wheel bucket for that second receives a thousand timers. When the bucket expires, the event loop runs a thousand callbacks. That burst can cause latency spikes. A facility can mitigate this by adding jitter to deadlines at insertion time, spreading the timers across a window. The jitter must be bounded so that the timer still fires within its contract. Alternatively, the facility can process a limited number of timers per event-loop iteration, deferring the rest to the next iteration. That bounds the latency impact on other events, but it also delays some timers. The right policy depends on whether the timers protect scarce resources or perform background maintenance.

## Lifetime, reclamation, and timers that outlive their wheel

A timer handle is a reference to an object that may live longer than the wheel that created it. In a sharded or per-core design, a timer may be migrated when a connection moves between event loops. In a dynamic system, a wheel may be destroyed when a thread exits. The facility must ensure that no callback runs after its associated resource has been freed, and no timer is left in a wheel that no longer exists.

The first line of defense is ownership. If a timer is pinned to the wheel that owns the resource, then the resource and the timer are destroyed together. The wheel's shutdown path cancels all outstanding timers and waits for any executing callbacks to finish. This is simple, but it requires a quiescent period. A thread that is shutting down cannot free its wheel until all cross-thread insertions and cancellations have been drained. A common pattern is to mark the wheel as closing, reject new insertions, drain the cross-thread queue, cancel all timers, and then free the wheel. The state machine on each timer ensures that a cancellation that races with expiration resolves deterministically.

Migration is harder. If a timer must move from one wheel to another, the facility must unlink it from the old wheel and insert it into the new one without losing a wakeup. The timer's state must be updated atomically, and the old wheel must not process the timer after the migration. One approach is to use a generation counter or an epoch. The timer handle includes the epoch of the wheel that owns it. A cross-thread operation that presents an old epoch is rejected. The new wheel assigns a new epoch when it takes ownership. This prevents a stale cancellation from affecting a reused timer handle.

Memory reclamation is the other half. Intrusive linked lists are attractive because they avoid allocation on the insertion path, but they make lifetime management explicit. If a timer is unlinked from a bucket while another thread is traversing that bucket, the traversal can follow a dangling pointer. A lock around the bucket solves the problem but adds contention. A lock-free list with hazard pointers or epoch-based reclamation solves it without locks, but the complexity is significant. Many production timer facilities choose a simpler model: all wheel mutations happen on the owner thread, and cross-thread operations are posted as messages. The owner thread is the only one that traverses buckets, so no concurrent traversal exists. The cost is the message hop, which is often acceptable because cross-thread timer operations are less frequent than local ones.

If the facility does allow concurrent traversal, it must define a safe memory reclamation scheme. Reference counting is one option: each timer has a reference count, and a traversing thread increments it before dereferencing. That adds an atomic operation per timer visit, which can be expensive in a hot bucket. Hazard pointers are another option: a traversing thread publishes the pointer it is about to dereference, and a reclaimer waits until no hazard pointer references the object. This is more scalable but requires careful implementation. Epoch-based reclamation batches reclamation into epochs and waits for all threads to pass a quiescent point. It is efficient when threads frequently enter and leave the data structure, but it can delay reclamation if a thread is preempted. The choice depends on how much concurrency the wheel must support and how much complexity the team can maintain.

## Integrating the wheel with an event loop

A timer wheel does not run on its own. It is driven by an event loop, a kernel thread, or a hardware interrupt. The integration determines how deadlines become wakeups and how callbacks are executed.

In an event-loop model, the loop owns the wheel and a poll set. On each iteration, the loop computes the next deadline from the wheel, polls for I/O with a timeout equal to the time until that deadline, processes any I/O events, advances the wheel to the current time, and runs due callbacks. If no timers are armed, the timeout is infinite. If a timer is armed, the timeout is the time until the next nonempty bucket. The loop must handle spurious wakeups and clock changes. A monotonic clock is essential; a wall-clock jump must not cause a timer to fire early or late.

The wakeup mechanism matters for tickless operation. A pipe or eventfd is a common way for another thread to wake the loop. The inserting thread writes a byte to the pipe; the loop's poll returns, and the loop processes the insertion queue before computing the next timeout. This adds a syscall per cross-thread insertion, which can be expensive. Batching insertions into a lock-free queue and writing to the pipe only when the queue transitions from empty to non-empty reduces the cost. The loop drains the queue and then recomputes the next deadline. If the earliest deadline changed, the loop's next poll timeout reflects it.

Callback execution context is another integration decision. If callbacks run directly in the event loop, a slow callback blocks all I/O and all other timers. That is often acceptable for short callbacks, but it is dangerous for callbacks that do blocking I/O or acquire locks. A facility can offload long-running callbacks to a worker pool, but then the timer's state machine must handle the transition from executing to done across threads. The callback's completion may need to signal the event loop. A common compromise is to require callbacks to be non-blocking and to provide a separate mechanism for work that must block. The timer facility's contract should state the execution context explicitly.

Reentrancy is a related concern. A callback may insert a new timer, cancel another timer, or even cancel itself. The wheel must tolerate these operations while it is iterating a bucket. If the callback cancels a timer that is later in the same bucket, the iteration must not visit the canceled timer. If the callback inserts a timer into the current bucket, the iteration must decide whether to process it in the same pass. A safe approach is to detach the bucket's list before invoking callbacks, so the iteration walks a private list and the wheel's bucket is empty. New insertions go into the wheel's current bucket or a future bucket, not into the detached list. This avoids iterator invalidation and makes reentrancy predictable. The detached list must be freed or recycled after the callbacks complete.

## Observability and testing of timer facilities

Timer facilities are infrastructure. When they misbehave, the symptoms are often far from the cause: a request hangs, a connection leaks, a heartbeat stops. Observability must be built into the facility, not added after an incident.

The most useful metrics are operation counts and latencies: insertions per second, cancellations per second, expirations per second, and the time spent advancing the wheel. A histogram of expiration latency—the delay between the scheduled deadline and the callback invocation—reveals whether the facility is meeting its contract. A histogram of bucket occupancy shows whether the hash or wheel geometry is distributing timers well. A count of cascades per advance shows whether the hierarchy is doing more work than expected. A gauge of outstanding timers shows growth that may indicate a leak. These metrics should be cheap to collect; per-operation atomics can be expensive, so many facilities aggregate per-thread and merge periodically.

Tracing is valuable for debugging individual timers. A timer handle can carry a trace ID or a creation stack. When a timer expires late, the trace can show whether it was delayed by a busy event loop, a cascade burst, or a lock. When a timer is canceled after expiration, the trace can show the race. The cost of tracing must be optional, because storing a stack trace per timer can dominate memory.

Testing a timer facility requires more than unit tests for insertion and deletion. It requires a virtual clock so that tests can advance time deterministically without sleeping. It requires fault injection for cancellation races: cancel a timer at the same instant it expires, repeatedly, and verify that the callback runs exactly once or not at all according to the contract. It requires stress tests with millions of timers and skewed deadline distributions: all timers at the same deadline, all timers spread evenly, and timers that are repeatedly inserted and canceled. It requires concurrency tests that insert and cancel from multiple threads while the owner thread advances the wheel. These tests can be run under a race detector and with sanitizers to catch use-after-free and data races.

A virtual clock is particularly important for hierarchical wheels. A test can advance the clock by a coarse tick and verify that timers cascade correctly, that no timer is lost, and that no timer fires early. The test can also verify the maximum expiration latency by advancing the clock in small increments and recording when each timer fires. This is how the facility's contract becomes executable rather than aspirational.

Finally, the facility should expose enough internal state for debugging without exposing implementation details. A dump function that lists the next deadline, the number of armed timers, and the occupancy of each level can be invaluable during an incident. The dump must be safe to call from a debugger or a signal handler, which usually means it cannot take locks or allocate memory. A lock-free snapshot or a per-thread summary is often sufficient.

The timer facility is a long-lived component. Its performance characteristics change as the workload changes: a design that is fast for a million timers may be slow for ten million, and a design that is precise for millisecond deadlines may be wasteful for hour-long deadlines. The architecture must be revisited when the workload crosses those boundaries. The next sections will examine how to choose the geometry for a given workload and how to evolve the facility without breaking its contract.

## Choosing the geometry from the workload

A timing wheel is defined by a small set of parameters: the base tick, the number of slots per level, the number of levels, the fanout between levels, and the sharding policy. These parameters are often chosen once and then forgotten, but they encode assumptions about arrival rate, delay distribution, cancellation ratio, and acceptable latency. Making those assumptions explicit is the difference between a facility that degrades gracefully and one that fails when the workload shifts.

Start with the base tick. The tick is the quantization unit. It should be no larger than the facility's promised precision, but it need not be as small as the clock resolution. If the application can tolerate ten milliseconds of slack, a one-millisecond tick buys precision the application cannot use while multiplying the number of buckets and the frequency of wheel advances. The right base tick is usually the coarsest value that still satisfies the tightest timer class in the system. Different timer classes can share a wheel if the facility applies per-class slack; a heartbeat timer and a request timeout do not need the same precision. The contract should state the quantization error separately from the scheduling latency, because they have different causes and different remedies.

The next parameter is the direct horizon. For a flat wheel, the horizon is slots times tick. The horizon should cover the common case, not the maximum possible delay. If most timers expire within a few seconds and a few expire in hours, a flat wheel sized for hours wastes memory and cache. A hierarchy lets the fine level cover the common case and the coarse levels cover the tail. The fine level's horizon should be large enough that cascades are infrequent relative to expirations. If the fine level covers only a few ticks, every timer cascades almost immediately, and the hierarchy adds overhead without reducing work. A useful rule is to make the fine horizon at least an order of magnitude larger than the base tick times the expected number of timers per tick, so that buckets remain sparse and cascades are not the dominant path. That rule is a starting point, not a law; the measured bucket occupancy should decide.

The fanout between levels controls the trade-off between depth and width. A large fanout means fewer levels but larger arrays per level. A small fanout means more levels but smaller arrays. Depth costs a few pointer dereferences during a cascade; width costs memory and cache footprint. For a wheel embedded in an event loop, a fanout of sixty-four or 256 is often convenient because it keeps the level count small and the arrays cache-friendly. A fanout that is a power of two allows mask-based indexing, but it can also interact with deadline distributions that cluster on powers of two. If the workload has many timers at round-number intervals, a power-of-two fanout may concentrate cascades. The geometry should be tested against the actual deadline distribution, not just the average delay.

The number of slots and the fanout together determine the maximum representable delay. If each level has S slots and the tick multiplies by F between levels, L levels cover S times F to the L minus one times the base tick. The memory cost is L times S buckets. Doubling the fanout halves the number of levels for the same horizon but doubles the slots per level. The product is what matters for memory; the distribution across levels matters for cache behavior. A deep hierarchy with small arrays may have better locality than a shallow hierarchy with large arrays, because each level's active region is small. The exception is when cascades touch many levels at once; then depth adds pointer-chasing. The workload's delay distribution should decide whether depth or width is the better fit.

Hashed wheels replace the horizon parameter with a bucket count and a rotation policy. The bucket count should be chosen so that the expected number of timers per bucket is small enough that scanning is cheap. If the wheel has B buckets and the outstanding population is N, the average bucket length is N divided by B. The scan cost on an advance is proportional to the occupancy of the current bucket, not the average, so the deadline distribution matters more than the average. A hash wheel with a uniform hash and a uniform deadline distribution behaves like a flat wheel with B slots. A hash wheel with a skewed distribution can have hot buckets even when the average load is low. The hash function should mix the deadline and the timer identifier, and the facility should monitor the maximum bucket length, not just the mean. If the maximum length exceeds a threshold, the facility needs an escape hatch: a per-bucket heap, a secondary overflow wheel, or a policy that rejects the timer with a clear error rather than allowing an unbounded scan.

Sharding is the final geometry parameter. The number of shards should usually match the number of event loops or cores that own timers, not the number of CPUs in the machine. A shard per event loop keeps the wheel single-threaded and cache-local. A shard per core may reduce the number of cross-thread operations if timers are distributed evenly, but it complicates ownership when a connection moves. The shard count also determines the cost of finding the global next deadline. If each shard maintains its own next deadline, a coordinator can keep a small heap of shard deadlines. The heap has one entry per shard, so its contention is bounded by the shard count rather than the timer count. The coordinator must handle stale entries: when a shard's next deadline changes, the old entry remains in the heap until it is popped. Lazy invalidation is usually cheaper than eager updates. If the coordinator is too much machinery, the event loop can poll all shards and take the minimum, but that cost grows with the shard count and should be measured.

Capacity planning should be done in terms of the timer handle, not just the wheel. A handle needs a deadline, a callback reference, a state word, and intrusive links. If the handle is sixty-four bytes and the system holds ten million timers, the handles alone are 640 megabytes before payloads. The wheel's bucket array and bitmaps add more. A facility that supports ten million timers should be sharded so that no single wheel holds all of them. The per-shard population should be bounded by the memory budget and by the acceptable scan length. The capacity plan should include a headroom factor for cancellation tombstones, cascades in progress, and cross-thread queues. A wheel that is sized for exactly the steady-state population will fail during a burst.

## Parameter selection under skew and burst

The geometry assumes a deadline distribution. Real workloads violate that assumption in predictable ways. Connection timeouts cluster at the configured timeout value. Heartbeats cluster at the heartbeat interval. Rate limiters cluster at the refill boundary. A wheel that is uniform in time but not in load will develop hot buckets. The facility should measure bucket occupancy and expiration latency by bucket, not just in aggregate. A hot bucket is not necessarily a bug; it may be the correct representation of a synchronized workload. It becomes a problem when the bucket's processing time exceeds the event loop's latency budget.

Jitter is the simplest mitigation. At insertion time, the facility adds a bounded random offset to the deadline. The offset must be within the timer's slack, and the API must make the jitter visible. Hiding jitter inside the facility is dangerous because the application may rely on the exact deadline for protocol correctness. A request timeout that fires early is a correctness bug; a request timeout that fires a few milliseconds late within its slack is usually acceptable. The facility should expose a slack parameter and apply jitter only within that slack. Timers with zero slack should not be jittered; they should be placed in a structure that preserves their exact ordering.

A second mitigation is to bound the work per advance. When a bucket contains more timers than a configured quota, the facility processes the quota and moves the rest to an overdue queue. The overdue queue is processed on the next event-loop iteration or after a short delay. This prevents a single bucket from monopolizing the loop. The cost is that some timers fire later than their deadline. The contract must state the maximum additional delay, and the facility must prioritize overdue timers over future timers. If the overdue queue grows without bound, the facility is overloaded; the right response is backpressure at insertion time or a larger shard count, not an ever-growing queue.

A third mitigation is a hybrid bucket. When a bucket's length exceeds a threshold, the facility converts it to a heap keyed by exact deadline. The heap preserves ordering within the bucket and allows the facility to process only the due timers. Cancellation must know whether the timer is in a list or a heap; the timer handle can carry a mode bit. The hybrid adds complexity and a second code path, but it is useful when the workload has a small number of very hot buckets and a long tail of sparse buckets. The alternative is to accept the scan cost and size the wheel so that hot buckets are rare. The choice depends on whether the hot buckets are transient or persistent. A transient burst can be handled by a quota; a persistent hot bucket suggests the geometry is wrong.

Cascades are the other source of burst. A coarse bucket that contains many timers will cascade all of them into finer levels at once. If the timers share a deadline, the cascade is unavoidable work, but it can be spread by processing the coarse bucket in chunks. The facility can move a limited number of timers per advance and leave the rest in the coarse bucket for the next advance. This delays the cascade but bounds the per-iteration cost. The contract must allow the delay. A hierarchical wheel with a large fanout can also reduce cascade bursts by making coarse buckets narrower in time, at the cost of more levels. The right fanout is the one that keeps the largest cascade below the event loop's latency budget for the expected deadline distribution.

Cancellation-heavy workloads need a different kind of attention. If most timers are canceled before they fire, the wheel's advantage is O(1) unlinking. That advantage disappears if cancellation must acquire a lock or post a message to another thread. The ownership model should keep cancellation on the same shard as the timer whenever possible. When cross-thread cancellation is necessary, the facility should batch cancellations and process them at the start of the next advance. A cancellation that arrives after the timer has expired must be resolved by the state machine, not by the wheel. The wheel should never see a timer in the executing state; the state transition happens before the callback is invoked. This separation keeps the wheel's invariants simple and makes the cancellation race a property of the handle protocol rather than the bucket list.

## Evolving the facility without breaking its contract

Timer facilities outlive the workloads that motivated them. A design that starts as a single wheel in one event loop may need to become a sharded hierarchy as the server grows. The evolution is safe only if the contract is explicit and the handle is opaque. The handle should not expose the bucket index, the level, or the shard. It should expose a generation number and an ownership epoch. The generation protects against handle reuse after a timer is freed. The epoch protects against stale operations after a timer migrates between shards. Both are cheap to check and both prevent classes of bugs that are otherwise very difficult to reproduce.

The contract should define the semantics that evolution must preserve. Does cancel return success after the callback has started? Does a zero-delay timer run on the current advance or the next one? Are timers with equal deadlines ordered by insertion, by identifier, or not at all? What is the maximum expiration latency for a timer with a given slack? What happens when a repeating timer falls behind? These questions are not implementation details; they are the promises that callers rely on. A change to the wheel geometry should not change any of them. A change to the concurrency model may change the cost of cancel, but it should not change the outcome. The test suite should encode the contract as executable cases, including the races.

Changing the geometry in place is usually harder than changing it shard by shard. A shard can be drained: stop accepting new timers, process the remaining due timers, cancel or migrate the outstanding timers, and then replace the wheel. Draining is safe if the shard owns its timers and the migration protocol preserves the deadline and the state. Timers that are already executing must be allowed to finish. Timers that are armed can be reinserted into the new wheel with their remaining delay. The handle's epoch changes, so stale cancellations from the old wheel are rejected. The cost is a brief pause for that shard, which is acceptable if the shard is small and the drain is scheduled during low load. A rolling upgrade across shards keeps the overall service available.

Shadow mode is a useful way to validate a new geometry before cutover. The facility can run the old wheel and a shadow wheel in parallel for a sample of timers. The shadow wheel does not invoke callbacks; it only records when each timer would expire. Comparing the two expiration times reveals whether the new geometry changes the latency distribution or loses timers. Shadow mode costs extra memory and CPU, so it should be limited to a small percentage of timers and a short duration. It is especially valuable when changing the tick or the fanout, because those changes affect quantization and cascade timing in ways that are hard to predict from average delay alone.

Observability is the feedback loop for evolution. The facility should expose per-shard metrics: outstanding timers, insertions, cancellations, expirations, cascade count, maximum bucket length, and expiration latency histogram. A histogram is more useful than an average because timer latency is often bimodal: most timers fire on time, and a few are delayed by a cascade or a busy loop. The histogram should have buckets fine enough to show the contract boundary. If the contract promises ten milliseconds of slack, the histogram should distinguish nine milliseconds from eleven. A facility that only reports the mean will hide the violations that matter.

Failure modes should be named and monitored. A lost wakeup shows up as a timer that expires long after its deadline while the event loop is idle. A double fire shows up as a callback invoked twice for one handle. A timer leak shows up as a growing outstanding count without a matching increase in active resources. A cascade storm shows up as a spike in cascade count and a drop in event-loop responsiveness. A lock convoy shows up as high cancellation latency under contention. Each mode has a different metric and a different mitigation. Naming them makes incident response faster and makes the architecture review concrete.

The final evolution question is whether the facility should remain a wheel at all. A wheel is optimal when deadlines are numerous, cancellations are common, and the acceptable precision is coarser than the clock. A heap is better when the timer population is small and exact ordering matters. A hybrid can use a wheel for the bulk and a heap for the precise few. The architecture should allow that substitution without changing the API. The handle, the state machine, and the ownership protocol are the stable parts; the index is an implementation detail. If the facility is designed around the handle and the contract, the wheel can be replaced when the workload outgrows it. If the facility is designed around the wheel, the workload will eventually outgrow the facility.

That is the discipline that keeps a timer facility maintainable: treat the geometry as a tunable, the contract as invariant, and the ownership model as the safety boundary. The next design question is not which wheel is fastest in isolation, but which combination of geometry and protocol keeps the promised latency under the workload the server actually sees.
